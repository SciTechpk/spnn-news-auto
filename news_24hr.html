Govt enlists oil refineries to shield citizens from global price hikes

The government said on Monday that oil refineries would bear a burden of approximately Rs15 billion for a month, which would allow petrol to be sold Rs15 cheaper than what it was in the global market amid the US-Israeli war on Iran. The statement came at a press conference in Islamabad addressed by Petroleum Minister Ali Pervaiz Malik and Information Minister Attaullah Tarar. Addressing the conference, Malik said that Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif had made efforts to meet with oil refineries and consult with them regarding interventions to ensure that people got the cheapest petrol amid impending steep fuel price increases, including an increase of about Rs15 that would have taken effect on Friday. “I am grateful to all the refineries that saw the public is in difficulty and with the government’s help … increased their throughput,” the minister said, adding that these refineries had made profits from this throughput in the last three months. “They were requested to take a step to support the public during this difficult time, and I am glad that they listened to us … the Rs15 increase that was coming in the petrol prices on Friday, they helped the government with that. Because of that we were able to maintain the price.” He added that more improvement would be seen by the public, expressing his gratitude to the refineries for standing with the government to provide assistance to the public during a time that had caused everyone difficulties — even the government as it struggled to procure oil at increasing prices. As a result of this step, he highlighted, a load of approximately Rs15 billion would be borne by these refineries, allowing petrol to be sold at a rate Rs15 per litre cheaper than what it would be in the international market. He expressed his hope that the crisis in the Middle East would soon move towards a peace agreement and that the government could advance with its purposes of “taking the system towards improvement, deregulation and refinery upgrades”. “The government doesn’t want to be cruel to anyone,” Malik stressed, saying that it intended to take actions “with consultations, keeping the public’s interests in mind, in a fair way”. He added that before the crisis, the premium of the refineries was $5 per barrel, and even today a $20 limit had been established. He further said that the government would have to step in and protect the public in case of further difficulties. Tarar also addressed the conference, noting that the premier’s vision “from the first day” had been to put as little burden on the country as possible. He said that even though the situation in the Middle East had affected every economy, including that of Pakistan, “our macroeconomic indicators have remained largely stable — Pakistan’s economy hasn’t taken as much of a hit as other countries”. Regarding Friday’s situation, when petrol prices were increasing in the international market, he said that PM Shehbaz had directed refineries to absorb the price hike in order to prevent the Rs15 increase. “During this difficult time … I think this is a great effort, as a result of which the public has been saved from this difficulty,” the information minister said. He also discussed the fuel subsidy scheme announced by the premier on Sept 13, saying: “If you told me two months ago that there would be a scheme in which, upon SMS registration, motorcycle users get a weekly concession of Rs500 on petrol, and 800cc car users, every 10 days, get 10 litres of petrol for Rs100 less per litre, then I would have told you that it’s probably not possible to implement such a scheme.” He praised PM Shehbaz’s “will power, intent and empathy for the public” that served as the foundation for the scheme. Tarar highlighted that the number of unique users who had registered for the scheme had now crossed 15 million. He said that about Rs27bn had so far been allocated towards this relief and highlighted its success. “This is a unique relief that is not available in the rest of the world,” he said, referring to both the fuel subsidy scheme and the steps taken by the government to prevent the increase in prices on Friday. Answering questions, Malik said that he was the “biggest advocate for deregulation” and for bringing competition into sectors, seeing it as the way to protect people’s welfare and deliver good service. However, he pointed out, “Have you seen any incident in the last 50 years that has been happening in the last six to eight months? What rules-based order do you want to adopt in the middle of this disaster?” He stressed that once the crisis in the region ended, the government would make efforts to return to deregulation; however, currently the focus was on public relief. The two ministers also stressed that there was no fuel shortage and that the country’s needs were being met even during difficult times. Malik said that November’s supply was fully covered, while decisions would be taken on supplies for the winter months of December and January and the prime minister had directed any necessary steps to be taken to ensure supplies. The price of high speed diesel has come down from a peak of Rs520.35 recorded on April 3. Its price had started rising from Rs281 per litre after the US-Iran war broke out on Feb 28. The petrol price had peaked at Rs458.41 on April 3 after beginning its upward trajectory from Rs266 in the first week of March. The government has reintroduced a raft of austerity measures in response to rising fuel prices amid the conflict. As of July, fuel prices are now fixed on a daily basis due to fluctuations in international market prices.

Respect for a woman’s dignity not just a matter of personal morality but constitutional obligation: Supreme Court

ISLAMABAD: The Supreme Court (SC) stated on Tuesday that respect for a woman’s dignity was not merely a matter of personal morality but a constitutional obligation the state expected of everyone in its service. The declaration came as a two-member bench, headed by Justice Ayesha Malik and including Justice Shahid Waheed, rejected a plea filed by a former employee of the Intelligence Bureau (IB) against his compulsory retirement over the allegation that he had set his wife on fire. “Off-duty conduct of sufficient gravity may constitute misconduct and acquittal in a criminal case does not undo misconduct independently established in departmental proceedings,” affirmed Justice Shahid Waheed in the nine-page judgement authored by him. The plea was filed by Jawad Abbasi, who used to work as a cook in the IB, against a Jan 15 decision by the Federal Service Tribunal’s (FST) to uphold his compulsory retirement from service. He had faced disciplinary proceedings under the Civil Servants (Efficiency and Discipline) Rules, 2020 over allegations that he had set his wife on fire and was ill-mannered and short-tempered. Abbasi was also accused of conduct that was criminal, unprofessional and prejudicial to good order and service discipline. Moreover, his conduct was said to have adversely affected the reputation and discipline of the IB. The departmental proceedings found the charges established, and by an order dated June 14, 2024, he was penalised with compulsory retirement. His departmental appeal in the case had also been rejected, and his appeal pertaining to his service had been dismissed by the FST. However, lower court acquitted him in the criminal case in the matter. Before the SC, his counsel Aftab Alam Yasir argued that the matter at hand was a private and domestic one between the married couple, had no connection with the petitioner’s official duties, and that Abbasi’s conduct outside his workplace and office hours could not constitute misconduct under the Civil Servants Rules. The counsel pointed out that his client had previously been acquitted by a trial court, further claiming that his wife had attempted suicide. He contended that the rejection of Abbasi’s plea following the departmental inquiry was erroneous. In his judgement on the matter, Justice Waheed observed that the dignity of a woman, whether as a wife, sister, daughter, mother or a fellow citizen, was a fundamental value protected by the Constitution. “Respect for that dignity is not merely a matter of personal morality; it is an obligation that the state is entitled to expect from every person in its service,” the judgement said. It added that the conduct of a civil servant was not judged exclusively by the manner in which he performed his official duties. His character, integrity and conduct in private life may also become relevant where these factors bore upon his suitability to hold a public office, the discipline of the service or public confidence in the institution he served, Justice Waheed emphasised. A person’s conduct towards those within his own home may, in appropriate circumstances, reveal qualities of character that could not be disregarded merely because the conduct occurred beyond the workplace, the judgement said. “The walls of a home are not a jurisdictional boundary, and violence does not become private because it is committed behind a closed door,” Justice Waheed stated, adding that “fire does not become a family affair because it was lit indoors”. “The allegation is not of a quarrel or a harsh word. It is that the petitioner set his wife on fire, an act which, if established, is among the gravest forms of violence one person can inflict upon another,” the judgement said. It further stated: “It is no incident of married life; it is an assault on life and the dignity of a human being.” The court also observed that the first information report (FIR) in the case was registered under Section 324 of the Pakistan Penal Code (PPC), which carried the penalty for an attempt to murder, and that burn injuries to the woman independently attracted the provisions on hurt in Chapter XVI (offences affecting the human body) of the PPC. The legislature had classified such conduct as a public wrong against the state, not a private dispute to be settled between spouses, the judgement said, adding that the right to life under Article 9 of the Constitution and the inviolability of the dignity of a person under Article 14 protected a wife within her home as fully as any citizen in a public place. “The Constitution does not stop at the threshold of a home; it walks in with every woman who lives there,” the judgement said. Moreover, Article 25(3) permitted special provision for the protection of women, and the legislature had acted upon it through, among other enactments, the Punjab Protection of Women against Violence Act, 2016 and the Domestic Violence (Prevention and Protection) Act, 2020 for the Islamabad Capital Territory, the court observed. In addition, Pakistan was a party to the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, under which violence against women was recognised as a form of discrimination that the state must act to eliminate, the judgement said. “The burning of women in their homes, frequently passed off as a kitchen or stove accident or as attempted suicide, is a grave and recurring social evil in this country,” stated Justice Waheed. “The plea of suicide is often the final injury inflicted upon a burnt woman; having been robbed of her safety, she is then robbed of the truth, and her suffering is recast as her own doing,” he said in the judgement, adding that a public institution could not remain unconcerned about such an allegation against one of its own employees and that indifference would be a failure of institutional duty, not a mark of respect for privacy. “Conduct of this gravity bears directly on the petitioner’s suitability for public service. A civil servant found, on proper inquiry, to have used potentially lethal violence against his own spouse displays a want of self-control and a disregard for human life,” the court declared. It observed that such conduct was “wholly unbecoming” of a government servant and prejudicial to good order and service discipline. “Where he then seeks to escape responsibility by casting the blame upon the victim, claiming that she set herself alight, he adds deceit to violence. A man who sets his wife on fire and then calls it her suicide has failed twice, first as a husband and then as a witness to the truth; such a man cannot be called a gentleman,” the court ruled. The judgement said public confidence in the civil service rested on the assurance that those who served the state respected the dignity of those around them and a civil servant who denied that respect to his own wife forfeited the trust on which his office depended. “Public service is a trust, and the conduct of those who hold it is a matter of public concern, at home as much as at work,” the judgement said. “Respect for women is not a private virtue that a civil servant may practise or neglect as he pleases; it is part of the character [of] the state.”

PAKISTAN’S MIRAGE OF HIGHER EDUCATION

Twenty-four years ago, on September 11, 2002, Gen Pervez Musharraf signed the Higher Education Commission Ordinance that formally launched Pakistan’s ground-breaking higher education reforms. This legislation dissolved the University Grants Commission and, in its place, created the Higher Education Commission (HEC) with vast regulatory, financial and administrative autonomy, and an express mandate to restructure the national higher education system and set Pakistan on course to become a knowledge economy. Over the next five years, the budget allocation for higher education went up by 5,000 percent. Billions were poured into setting up new universities. A landmark agreement was negotiated with the US to launch what was then considered the world’s largest Fulbright scholarship programme, worth $150 million. Some 5,000 PhD level scholarships were announced for foreign universities, the largest such programme undertaken by a developing country, as well as 3,000 indigenous scholarships. A tenure track system with very competitive salaries was launched to attract and retain the finest academics. To term these plans ‘ambitious’ would be an understatement. Other countries sat up and took notice. Professor C.N.R. Rao, former chairman of India’s Scientific Advisory Council, told former Indian prime minister Manmohan Singh in 2006, “Science is a lucrative profession in Pakistan. It has tripled the salaries of its scientists in the last few years.” This was reported in the Hindustan Times and this particular story carried the by-line: “Pakistan may soon join China in giving India serious competition in science.” Launched in 2002 with unprecedented funding and ambitious reforms, Pakistan’s higher education experiment once attracted international attention. Today, Pakistan has nearly 280 universities, millions of students and a rapidly expanding research output. Yet, beneath the impressive statistics lies a system struggling with mounting debts, poor educational outcomes and questionable scholarship. What has gone wrong? And is there any way out? Bangladesh is a big fan of Pakistan’s higher education reforms to this day. Large-scale academic delegations from Bangladesh have visited Pakistan frequently over the last two years, touring our universities, building institutional ties and doing homework for their own reforms. In 2025, Bangladesh’s Ministry of Education released the draft Higher Education Commission Ordinance, explicitly modelled on our framework. It was an auspicious start, but it was not to last. Within a decade, the HEC’s autonomy was challenged and higher education funding was dramatically slashed. In an earlier piece, we tracked the collapse of Pakistan’s tenure track system and the mass exodus of Pakistan’s best and brightest academics. Now, as our higher education experiment enters its 25th year — its silver jubilee — it is time to ask the big questions: where do we stand today? What have we accomplished? What mistakes have we made? And, most importantly, how do we move ahead? Here is a look at the good, the bad and the ugly in the wide-ranging landscape of our higher education system today. LET A THOUSAND UNIVERSITIES BLOOM The most obvious indicator of success is the explosive growth of our universities. As per HEC data, in 2001-2002, we had a total of 58 higher education institutions in the country. This count almost doubled to 116 in 2005-2006 and crossed the 200 mark in 2017-2018. As per the Pakistan Economic Survey 2025-26, we now have 278 universities, an increase of almost 400 percent, an unprecedented achievement. Leading Pakistani institutions now routinely feature in international university rankings. Exact positions tend to fluctuate year by year — and we seem unable to break into the global top-350 universities category — but it is most definitely a win. Student enrolments have similarly skyrocketed, from around 276,000 in 2001-2002 to more than two million in 2023-2024, an increase of more than 600 percent. Moreover, in 2002, student enrolments were split with 63 percent males to 37 percent females. By 2025, this gender gap had almost hit parity, with 52 percent males versus 48 percent females. Another very remarkable milestone is that in our first 54 years of Independence (up to 2002, when our higher education reforms were launched), Pakistan had produced almost 3,000 PhDs. Now we produce more than 3,000 PhDs in a single year. This also reflects in our academic publication count, which has simply exploded. Our global academic publication count ranking has climbed from 51 to 26 in 25 years. As per the SCImago database, Pakistan’s indexed publications increased from approximately 1,600 in 2002 to over 45,000 in 2025. This March, the HEC formally honoured over a thousand Pakistani researchers featured in a list (curated by Stanford University) of the world’s top two percent scientists. These are landmark achievements. Very few countries can claim such incredible progress in so short a period of time. After surveying these highly impressive statistics, one would not be remiss in thinking that our higher education experiment is faring very well indeed. FROM BOOM TO BAILOUT Unfortunately, one has only to scratch the surface to unearth grim realities beneath. Our massive university expansion is now collapsing under its own weight. This issue burst into the spotlight last year with news that almost all public universities in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP) were experiencing severe financial distress. A leading academic staff association has demanded a Rs25 billion package to bail out the entire sector. This is not just a KP issue. Deficits are running high in many prestigious universities, salaries are being doled out in instalments, pension payments are being delayed and staff are striking and heading for the courts. This August, the federal government approved a Rs2 billion bailout for Quaid-i-Azam University in Islamabad. Mounting costs had completely exhausted the university’s pension fund. It was a struggle to fund even basic operations, such as replenishing laboratory chemical supplies. In Sindh, Karachi University reported a Rs1.3 billion deficit, followed by a paralysing strike that made nationwide headlines. The Federal Urdu University of Arts, Sciences and Technology in Karachi has piled up a backlog of Rs1.2 billion in salaries, pensions and other liabilities, and requires another Rs1.7 billion infusion for daily operations. Sindh University’s 2026-27 budget includes Rs3.75 billion in grants from the provincial government and Rs1.716 billion from the federal government and the HEC. This March, the Balochistan government stepped in with Rs857 million payment to 11 universities in the province. Soon after, in a rare and very welcome move, Balochistan Governor Jaffar Khan Mandokhail announced an increase in the public universities’ budget from Rs2.5 billion to Rs8 billion. The University of Azad Jammu and Kashmir has reported a shortfall of more than Rs1.3 billion, with staff threatening campus shutdowns. Punjab University has reported a Rs3.68 billion deficit, which they intend to address through internal borrowing. THE WALKING DEAD — UNIVERSITY EDITION These numbers are just some highlights, but this crisis has been a decade in the making and the entire sector is on life support today. So, what’s the worst case scenario? In 2024, reports emerged that a leading international lending agency — its identity was not revealed — floated a study indicating that around 80 percent of our public universities would default within three to four years. This reads like a mass-extinction event. There are multiple reasons for this. First and foremost, the federal government has capped the recurring budget for universities at Rs65 billion, a limit in place since 2017-18. But back in 2017, we had almost a hundred universities less than we do today. And, of course, in 2017, the US dollar averaged Rs105. Those were the ‘good old’ days. This year, the HEC had internally calculated a requirement of Rs138 billion and had requested the government for at least Rs100 billion, but that requirement was not met. The provinces have also been grossly remiss in their contributions. This trend is not a higher education problem, it applies to the entire education sector as a whole. Last year’s Pakistan Economic Survey revealed that Pakistan’s education spending had dropped precipitously to just 0.8 percent of the country’s GDP, far below Unesco’s recommended benchmark of four to six percent. It gets much worse. To get a sense of this Rs65 billion number, I looked up the balance sheet of the University of New South Wales (UNSW) in Australia, where I did my own postgraduate studies. In 2025, UNSW reported operational expenses amounting to AUD3.28 billion, around Rs630 billion. In short, this one university in Australia — an undeniably good international university, but certainly no global superstar like the Ivy League universities in the US or the Russell Group universities in the UK — has an annual budget almost tenfold what our government allocates to all of Pakistan’s public sector universities put together. THE GREAT ‘SUPERMARKETIFICATION’ Setting up new universities in Pakistan has also taken on an unfortunate ‘franchise’ mentality — as first documented by the historian Dr Yaqoob Bangash — akin to setting up branches of a departmental store chain. Little thought is given to genuine need, excellence and standards or long term sustainability. For instance, last year in KP, with most universities on the brink of collapse, the provincial government moved to set up two new universities. Launching universities has also become a potent grift, a way for politicians and administrators to score political benefits, securing lucrative development contracts, setting up employment avenues and putting the government on the hook. In this model, explicated by former member of the Planning Commission Waqas bin Najib in his Dawn Op-Ed ‘Building the Knowledge Economy’, universities may be conceptualised as “patronage vehicles.” And the HEC acts as “a real estate fund manager.” Our universities are also decades behind international best practices, featuring cumbersome processes and paperwork reminiscent of our colonial era and abysmal governance. A Supreme Court order of 2024 noted that most universities are in contravention of their own laws and are being run on the “personal whims” of those in charge. Almost all public sector universities have too many non-academic staff, significantly in excess of the HEC’s prescribed 1:1.5 ratio of academics versus non-academics. A recent HEC survey found that a staggering 40 percent of key faculty and administrative positions in universities were lying vacant. With numbers like these, one cannot help but wonder: how are things still even working? THE EMPEROR’S NEW DEGREES But, are things really working? A common refrain we academics hear is that universities are not producing skilled and capable graduates. There is little hard data for this claim. Our systems are very good at recording and advertising glittering statistics, such as university counts, student enrolment and publications, but relatively quiet when it comes to graduate outcomes, employment numbers, career progression etc. But, every now and then, we get hints that lay bare the sheer magnitude of this problem. For instance, at a session organised by the American Business Forum (ABF) and the Lahore University of Management Sciences (Lums) this September, Federal Minister for IT and Telecommunication Shaza Fatima Khawaja revealed the results of a national baseline test of 33,000 final year computer science students from 190 universities. Across 10 employability competencies, only 0.4 percent students scored above 80 percent. A mere four percent scored above 68 percent. Overall, 61 percent students languished below the 50 percent mark. Likewise, the Central Superior Services (CSS) written examination results were announced in August. Of over 16,000 candidates appearing in all papers, only 476 qualified for the next stage, a share of 2.74 percent. This brutal rate is not an anomaly; the CSS exams are certainly very competitive. But examiners noted a definite decline in standards. In a written note to the National Assembly, Senator Ahad Khan Cheema attributed this high failure rate to poor academic background, weak analytical and comprehension skills and subpar written expression. The most alarming story I personally came across is from 2023, when the KP Public Service Commission (PSC) revealed that candidates with postgraduate degrees struggled to solve basic math problems without calculators. In language and literature tests, students’ grammar, syntax and pronunciation were found to be abysmal. “It has been noted that the quality and extent of knowledge of the candidates, having postgraduate qualification, is superficial,” stated their report, noting that candidates were “shaky and lack confidence” during interviews. They then asked the obvious question: “Are the teaching institutions properly equipped with teaching faculty and do they provide a scholarly environment to their students?” These examples are deeply unsettling. And even as there is growing recognition of this collapse in educational outcomes, we have yet to see serious meaningful solutions. Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif has called for audits and urgent reforms. The HEC has mandated professional internships and industry-relevant certifications for undergrad degree programmes. The Federal Minister for Planning, Development and Special Initiatives, Ahsan Iqbal, has directed the chairman of the HEC to undertake an immediate and comprehensive review of all university degree programmes and curricula across Pakistan to align programmes with industrial trends. THE EMPEROR’S NEW RESEARCH University research output tells an equally disturbing story. Behind the glitzy numbers, the overwhelming trend in Pakistani academia is now to publish papers in journals of middling to low quality — typically predatory or mega-journals — which charge extravagant article processing fees to rush publication without rigorous peer review. The end result is a flood of papers of mediocre and questionable quality, but which nevertheless tick all the boxes needed to get promoted in our system. This practice is not research, it is more akin to a transaction. An entire industry of shady journals now operates, earning billions every year in pumping up the research credentials of academics in developing countries like ours. This practice is widely acknowledged in our circles and is usually met with a shrug. But it has now reached critical proportions that we can now only ignore at our peril. For instance, a study from 2024 analysed around 500 social sciences and humanities articles published by 50 Pakistani professors over a five-year period. They found that almost 70 percent of the sampled papers were published in journals that were unrecognised by the HEC’s online journal recognition system and classed as predatory. Another study, also from 2024, focusing on the physical education and sports sciences domains, found that almost 90 percent of sampled papers appeared in questionable or predatory journals. These are catastrophic numbers. However, these are just snapshots of the overall sector. We urgently need more comprehensive studies to tell us how deep this rot really runs. THE RANKINGS REALITY CHECK The standards of our research output also reflects in international rankings, which specifically prioritise research quality over quantity. The Times Higher Education University Rankings are particularly research-driven and this year they included 56 Pakistani universities. However, the results were abysmal. No Pakistani university features in the global top 500. Quaid-i-Azam University, our highest-ranked institution, shows up in the 551-600 range. The vast majority of Pakistani universities are ranked 801-1,000. The Academic Ranking of World Universities (ARWU) — popularly referred to as the Shanghai rankings — which places a very high premium on quality research, included only four Pakistani universities in its assessment this year: COMSATS University, the National University of Sciences and Technology (NUST), Quaid-i-Azam University and the University of the Punjab. All of them were in the 801-900 global range. Likewise, the SCImago Institutions Rankings, a research and innovation-focused system, ranks COMSATS University Islamabad as Pakistan’s best at the 1,715 position globally. NUST is second at 2,126. The majority of Pakistani universities show up after the 5,000 mark. A 2025 study undertaken by the renowned John Ioannidis from Stanford University, a pioneer in the study of research practices, ranks Pakistan as one of the world’s leaders in paper retraction rates (at 28.1 retracted papers per 10,000). This rate lags only slightly behind Saudi Arabia (30.6) and is almost double that of India (15.2). A retraction indicates a research paper that can no longer be trusted and is therefore withdrawn from the record. This could be because of an honest mistake or oversight on the part of the authors or publisher. However, it is, more often than not, due to falsified data, fraud or plagiarism. In their 2025 study, Ioannidis and colleagues matched retraction records against their database of the world’s top-cited scientists in their sub-domains. Of the handful of Pakistani authors on the list, they found that almost 30 percent also had retracted papers on their record. In an article in the renowned journal Scientometrics, Ioannidis again highlights Pakistan prominently in his list of countries where “extreme publishing” is sharply on the rise. He identifies “spurious practices”, such as consortium agreements, self-citation and gift authorships, which researchers use to inflate their publication metrics. What do these terms mean exactly? A recent study on Pakistan’s publication culture titled ‘Research Culture in Pakistan’s Higher Education Institutions’ features a despairing quote by an anonymous academic: “People tend to self-cite or ask their peers and students to cite their work abundantly to appear in the so-called two percent list published by Stanford University. This number game has produced research cartels and people seem to scratch each other’s back.” We certainly have local researchers who are no doubt genuinely prolific, sincere to their calling and their valuable achievements should be celebrated. But our universities lack the institutional checks and, more importantly, the will to stop this unscrupulous numbers game. A METRIC THAT MATTERS When unethical practices become institutionalised and systemic, they invariably get flagged at the international level. For corruption, we have the Transparency International ranking, for democracy, there is the Freedom House score and the Economist’s Democracy Index. For research, we have the recently developed Research Integrity Risk Index. This tool evaluates universities using a metric focused on retracted papers and anomalous citation patterns. Of 19 leading Pakistani universities featured in this system this year, almost all were flagged as red flag (extreme or significant deviation from global norms), high risk, or on the watch list. Only one university (Aga Khan University) was classed as relatively normal. No Pakistani university exhibited low risk, ie “strong adherence to publishing integrity norms.” The results of this index have generated heated debate in countries such as India, Egypt, Malaysia, Indonesia and Morocco. Unfortunately, it’s crickets here in Pakistan. REFORM BEGINS WITH RECKONING So, here we stand today. The bold reforms of 2002 that were to transform Pakistan into a knowledge economy have instead mutated into a gargantuan Frankenstein’s monster, which limps into its 25th year, too big to fail and too difficult to reform. We want a knowledge economy but we do not seem to care about knowledge at all. I have written on national-level issues before — our perennial smog problem, broken elections etc — and I often get emails from readers telling me they enjoyed the piece but wish I had offered ‘solutions’. Unfortunately, there are no magic bullets here. Our problems are immense, fiendishly complex and infuriatingly bound up with our colonial baggage. The only real suggestion I can offer would read as painfully naïve: that stakeholders confront these inconvenient truths, make education a priority — the priority — set aside their egos to engage with critics and sceptics in good faith, and rebuild this system brick-by-brick. The hard truth is that many people saw this collapse coming from miles away and they sounded the alarm, but were largely ignored. As an academic who has worked with government ministries, civil servants and politicians, I also realise this is a very difficult ask. It is much more tempting to set up yet another high-powered committee, call in the international consultants or introduce some new foreign and glamorous system. In short, business as usual. NOTHING NEW UNDER THE SUN It might surprise many to learn that the original architects of our higher education enterprise foresaw this collapse. They even spelt it out explicitly for us in the original Task Force report of 2002, the founding document of our higher education experiment, which resulted in the Higher Education Commission Ordinance and the creation of the HEC. Early in the report, they listed prominent challenges faced by Pakistan’s higher education sector, amid logistical and material issues, infrastructure, governance, politicisation and the like. They noted the curious problem of “[s]trong scepticism about the realisation of reform.” At the very end of their report, they quoted a report penned in 1959 by the Commission on National Education, one of the earliest attempts to reform Pakistan’s education system. And the authors of that particular report did not mince their words, which is why their statements are worth quoting at length: “Education… can meet its responsibilities only if a revision of attitudes on the part of the professional educator is accompanied by a change in the view point of the government and the public… In education, the prevailing attitude of the public and those responsible in government was that it warranted low priority… Although our leaders were now the architects of policy rather than the implementers of the policy of others, education was neither in fact nor theory given the importance that would enable it to meet the needs of a people who controlled their own destiny… “We cannot escape the conclusion that our fundamental need is for a revolution in attitudes, through which the cynicism, lethargy, opportunism, suspicion, dishonesty and indifference that have characterised the outlook of so many of our people and officials in the past will give way to a spirit of individual initiative, personal integrity, pride in accomplishment, trust in one’s fellow men and a ‘private sense of public duty.’ We have no illusion that this reorientation of values can be brought about quickly or completely, or that it can be realised only through fiat, yet come it must if we are to achieve any substantial improvement in our education system or any other sphere of national endeavour.” Nearly 65 years later, these words could not ring more true. One is sorely tempted to frame them and set them up on the desk of every higher education professional in the country, not just teachers and researchers, but especially the administrators, the vice-chancellors and the bureaucrats. How necessity continues to be the mother of invention in Pakistan

But it appears that the boom has outrun the system built to absorb it. In 2026, the power regulator moved from “net metering” to “net-billing”, under which solar prosumers buy power from the grid at standard retail rates but sell excess solar energy back at a much lower rate. The shift has raised demand for batteries as households and businesses look to store the power they generate instead of selling it back to the government. It also shows how the energy of the sun can be harnessed in ways to address the plethora of issues the people of the country are facing. At the recently held Solar Pakistan Exhibition in Karachi — which brought together solar technology, solar power solutions, renewable energy innovations and sustainable energy products — innovation was in full display. Specifically, it showed how to use solar power to address issues around commuting, cooking and obtaining drinking water. Battery-swapping stations for electric vehicles With the US-Israeli war on Iran and its effects on global oil prices showing no signs of abating, electric vehicles (EVs) have now started trickling onto the roads in Pakistan. According to a recent Dawn report, around 150,000 electric bikes and scooties were sold across the country in 2025. The report, however, noted that despite this, EV sales were still not comparable to motorcycle sales: a CD70 costs Rs168,000, while a proper lithium-powered electric bike costs more than Rs300,000. At the Solar Pakistan Exhibition held on October 10, Bahum Global PVT, a solar service company, introduced its EV bikes with a unique answer to both the cost and the battery degradation problem: battery-swapping stations. One problem EV owners have complained of is the battery “dying” like that of a phone, which makes many wary of being stranded en route. To cater to this issue, Zafar Khan, the general manager of Bahum, told Dawn that the company is in talks with petrol pump brands like Pakistan State Oil (PSO), Shell and Total to install battery-swapping stations. The electric bikes themselves will be manufactured in Pakistan, which will hopefully cut the taxation costs as well. This way, instead of leaving the battery on charge overnight or having to abruptly halt a journey due to the battery running out of charge, an EV owner can stop at a nearby petrol pump with a swapping facility and exchange the drained battery for one that is charged one at a fixed cost. After a successful pilot at the PSO petrol pump in Lahore’s Cantt area, Bahum is set to launch the EVs and battery-swapping stations throughout Pakistan in December 2026. At Kazakhstan forum, Pakistan presents Karachi and Gwadar ports as 'strategic gateways' for Central Asian trade

ISLAMABAD: Pakistan can serve as an important trade and transit corridor for the landlocked Central Asian countries, providing them with access to international maritime trade routes and global markets, Minister for Board of Investment (BoI) Qaiser Ahmed Sheikh said on Monday. He made the remarks while addressing the opening session of the Second Tianshan Forum for Central Asian Economic and Quality Investment, being held in Kazakhstan’s Almaty. The minister termed Pakistan’s three major seaports — Karachi Port, Port Qasim and Gwadar Port — “strategic gateways that could facilitate regional trade, strengthen transportation links and provide alternative routes for the movement of goods”, according to a press release by the BoI. Sheikh particularly underscored Karachi’s importance as Pakistan’s major industrial and commercial hub, noting that its established industrial base, business infrastructure, financial institutions and port facilities offered significant opportunities for trade, industrial partnerships and foreign investment. He also highlighted Gwadar’s potential to further strengthen regional connectivity and economic cooperation. Regional connectivity was “not merely about linking roads, ports and markets, but also about connecting economies, creating investment opportunities, generating employment and building shared prosperity across the region”, he remarked. The minister invited regional and international investors to explore opportunities in key sectors, including energy, infrastructure, logistics, transportation, manufacturing and trade. He emphasised that improved connectivity and reliable energy supplies were essential for unlocking the region’s economic potential, the BoI statement said. Highlighting the importance of regional cooperation, the minister acknowledged the contribution of the Central Asia Regional Economic Cooperation (Carec) Programme in promoting transport connectivity, facilitating trade and supporting economic integration among participating countries. He observed that Carec provided a valuable platform for strengthening economic partnerships between Pakistan and Central Asian states, the statement said. The minister emphasised that Pakistan’s strategic geographical location, youthful population, industrial capacity and seaport infrastructure positioned the country to play an important role in connecting Central Asian economies with international markets. The Second Tianshan Forum will conclude in Almaty on Tuesday under the theme “Strengthening Resilient Connectivity and Quality Investment in Central Asia”. According to information provided in its official agenda, the two-day forum is a follow-up to the success of the inaugural Tianshan Forum for Central Asia Economic Cooperation, and is aligned with the new Carec Institute Strategy 2026–2030, as well as the Carec 2030 Strategy Midterm Review Rolling Implementation Action Plan. “In 2026, the Carec region continues to face a highly uncertain external environment. Rising geopolitical tensions have raised energy and food prices, disrupted transport and logistics, and weakened the reliability of trade corridors,” the agenda said. At the same time, it added that intra-regional trade remained limited due to similar economic structures, weak production complementarities, and incomplete market opening. Persistent transport and logistics bottlenecks such as uneven infrastructure quality, fragmented asset management, and capacity mismatches kept trade costs high, it added. “Non-physical barriers, including inconsistent customs procedures, weak digitalisation, non-harmonised standards, and inefficient border operations, further constrain cross-border flows. Meanwhile, digital infrastructure — especially cloud services, data centres, and AI applications — is expanding rapidly and reshaping connectivity and competitiveness,” it said. It pointed out that Carec countries were progressing unevenly with “large gaps in backbone infrastructure, digital skills, and regulatory coherence”, calling for these gaps to be bridged to “effectively support inclusive growth, resilient connectivity, and regional integration”. Senior representatives from multilateral development banks, financial institutions, high-level policymakers, business leaders, local governments, and academia across the Carec countries are attending the forum to discuss policy and operational issues in strategic areas such as transport, trade, logistics, energy, and digital connectivity, and promote pragmatic, sustainable investment in the region.

PTI lawyer Salman Safdar says he met Imran, Bushra Bibi at Adiala jail

PTI lawyer Barrister Salman Safdar on Monday said he met with party founder Imran Khan and his spouse, Bushra Bibi, at Rawalpindi’s Adiala jail, where they are both incarcerated. Imran has been in prison since Aug 5, 2023, while his wife was arrested on Jan 17, 2025 in a separate case in which the couple were sentenced. The IHC has allowed Imran twice-a-week meetings — on Tuesdays and Thursdays — with his family, lawyers and other associates. Despite multiple court directives, Imran’s meetings with his family and party leaders have been largely restricted in recent months. In a post on X on Monday, Safdar said the meeting was his first with Imran after seven months and with Bushra in 11 months. The last time he met Imran, the Supreme Court designated him as amicus. However, this time he met Imran Khan as his lawyer. It lasted 40 minutes and was “insufficient to cover all cases [and] legal matters”, the PTI counsel contended. “Both briefed me on their health conditions [and living] conditions and gave Fresh instructions for some legal matters and cases,” he wrote on X. PTI Central Information Secretary Sheikh Waqas Akram told Dawn that he spoke to Safdar in detail. “Salman told me that the meeting was held in connection with a number of applications which have been filed in courts to arrange meetings with Imran Khan,” he said. “Salman has told me that he reached Adiala Jail at around 4:30pm and the meeting continued for around 40 to 45 minutes. However, he said that it was not sufficient time for the meetings, as there were so many cases which had to be discussed and as the meeting was being held after such a long time.“ Replying to a question, Akram said that no political issues were discussed during the meeting, which he added was also attended by Bushra Bibi. Safdar spoke about the cases and asked about future strategy. When asked about Imran’s health, Akram replied that although health issues were discussed, the PTI would only speak about the legal cases. Barrister Safdar’s last publicly reported meeting with Imran was held on April 8 this year, a day after the Islamabad High Court (IHC) directed authorities to arrange the meeting. His previous meeting on Feb 10 with the former premier was also on the directives of the Supreme Court, where concerns about Imran’s health were raised. Imran’s eye ailment — right central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) — came to light in late January. While he has undergone eye procedures at the government-run Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences (Pims), his family demands that he be shifted to a hospital of his choice. The government and the opposition have been engaged in a blame game, with the latter accusing the former of a lack of transparency on the matter, of not ensuring appropriate treatment for Imran, and of not allowing his personal physicians access to him. The government denies these allegations. Imran was sent to jail for a three-year term on Aug 5, 2023 for concealing details of Toshakhana gifts. However, he and Bushra are also serving a 14-year sentence in a £190 million corruption case, also known as the Al-Qadir Trust case. The couple were convicted in another Toshakhana case in December 2025, with 10-year and seven-year terms handed to them. The PTI founder also faces pending trials in terrorism cases related to the May 9, 2023 riots. Imran’s sisters Aleema Khan, Noreen Niazi and Uzma Khan, along with opposition leaders, have staged frequent sit-ins outside the Adiala jail demanding the PTI founder’s release. They remain unable to stage more sit-ins since their detention in late September under the Punjab Maintenance of Public Order Ordinance, as the PTI geared up for a protest march to Islamabad.
