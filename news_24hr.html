Pakistan's amateur radios are tuned in for the apocalypse

When the earthquake struck in 2005, two men in Islamabad loaded their bags and headed for Azad Kashmir. They made it as far as Kohala bridge where the road got cut off. From there on, they made it by foot and hitchhiked on motorcycles and the back of pick-up trucks to Muzaffarabad. One of the few offices still functioning there was Rescue 15 whose building had half collapsed. The men asked the surprised police officials inside if they had a car battery. The officials led them to a crushed Suzuki Mehran. The men got to work. A radio transceiver was plugged into the battery and they started transmitting messages to Islamabad since telephone landlines had collapsed. In the next few days they sent nearly 3,000 messages to Islamabad by radio, which their affiliates spread throughout Pakistan by phone. Most messages were confirmations of survivals and deaths. These men belonged to the voluntary Pakistan Amateur Radio Society (PARS) which has been around since 1970. It is made up of licensed professionals who operate amateur or ham radio, which keep people connected when all else fails. Decades later, the society is still going strong, and since its fledgling foray into Kashmir has increasingly taken up the cause of rescue and relief work when humanitarian disaster strikes in Pakistan. Ham radios come to Pakistan Ham radios emerged at the end of the 19th century with licensing starting from the US around 1912. One theory suggests that it was the initials of a group of early adopters—Hyman, Almy, and Murray at Harvard University’s radio club—that shortened first into HYALMU and then the name HAM. A more popular theory is that in the early days professionals looked down at the amateurs as ‘hams,’ as in ham-fisted or unskilled. Despite the pejorative etymology, the name stuck and is used proudly today. Amateur radio operators have operated here since before Partition. The oldest membership card was issued in November 1947 and a Pakistan Amateur Radio Society one from 1950 carries the call sign AP5B. Records show that one was active in Karachi in 1949 and from Quetta’s Chiltan Hotel in 1953. Old QSL cards from Pakistan (clockwise from top): From 1947 issued under the old VU call sign, from 1949 with the new Pakistani AP call sign, the oldest society card from 1950, and the card for Quetta’s Chiltan Hotel. Photos via PARS Though these records appear in 1950, former PARS President Nasir Khan (call sign AP2NK) said in a 2019 interview that the society was formed in 1970. (This discrepancy could be explained by a change in registration rules after which the society began anew). According to Nasir Khan, many of the initial members, including him, were associated with the telecommunications industry which is why their professional interests led them to ham radio, and ultimately to create the society. When Nasir Khan passed away in 2025, PARS announced that AP2NK was now a ‘silent key’. Ham radio work is driven by enthusiasm and charitable spirit but it is still governed by regulations which say a licensed operator has to be a member of PARS to legally function. Licenses were first issued by the Post and Telegraph department, followed by the Telephone and Telegraph department, and since 1996, the Pakistan Telecommunication Authority (PTA) which now has a dedicated Radio-Based Services section. PTA records thus date back to 1962-63 even though files and folders have moved around between departments. Radio operators in Pakistan must be at least 16 years old. They join PARS as a ‘short-wave listener’ (SWL) for at least one year (like a driving learner’s license) before earning a full license (two years if you are under 18). SWLs can listen but not speak on the radio and use this time to learn mannerisms and techniques. Applications require details of equipment and operators are subjected to security verification which takes three months or more. A written test and interview follow at the PTA. Each newly licensed operator is then assigned a unique call sign, which in Pakistan begin with AP2 (or 5 for organisations) followed by the person’s initials. Radio communication takes place with phonetic codes, so AP2 will become “Alpha Papa 2”, and universally recognised Q codes, such as QTH relating to location and QSL relating to receipt of transmission. Codes such as “5 and 9”, meaning readability and signal strength respectively are at their highest, are also used. Audio of communication between hams in Islamabad and Lahore, a distance of over 283 kilometres. They announce their call signs, and confirm readability and signal strength by saying “5 and 9”. PARS helps applicants prepare and for several years, tests were conducted in coordination with the PTA. The license fee is Rs2,000 and has to be renewed every five years. Members pay the Society Rs2,000 a year, students Rs1,500 for three years and SWLs Rs4,000 for a three-year term. People over 65 years are granted lifetime licenses. Licenses can be revoked legally but according to the PTA no such case has arisen so far. Licenses usually end when holders pass away or fail to renew. The PTA does not allow mobile radio stations in its law. Exceptions can, however, be made for temporary periods in times of emergency and special need. This provision, in particular, is central to how the Society has worked in rescue efforts. ‘It’s not a hobby’ Today the group has around 600 active members of whom around 160 are amateur radio operators and the rest are SWLs or others. Most members have full-time jobs or businesses. One works in the oil and gas industry, some are telecom engineers, another is a cardiologist and several are serving members of the armed forces. Former Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Governor Amir Gulistan Janjua (AP2AGJ) was also a member. There is nothing ‘amateur’ about PARS. During initial interviews, my use of the word ‘hobby’ was met with polite clarification that every member was a professional and the operations are considered a service. Indeed, the Facebook page and website lists PARS as a non-profit organization with coordinators and representatives in all provinces. It does not accept donations or raise funds and the rule is that no politics or personal matters can ever be discussed on air. Equipment is considered expensive but lasts decades. Members learn how to repair their own machines and one member even created a specialized battery that can power several radios at once. This spirit of invention also takes members to science fairs and expos. At an event in NUST in 2026, for example, PARS taught students how to make an antenna using a clothes hanger, with which they talked to someone in Gujranwala. Some of the society’s events, such as the annual meeting of its general body, take place at a university managed by one of the members. No premises have ever been acquired and the Society’s headquarters are generally based at the licensed location of a senior member. At one point it was a school owned by a senior office bearer and later another member’s house. The boy who loved radios I met the Society’s vice-president at his house in Islamabad that doubles as the organization’s headquarters. I had the location PIN and house number but when I got there, I realised I had wasted my time looking at plaques in the street. It was the house with the huge antenna. The antenna on the shack belonging to AP2AUM. Photo by author. In a ‘shack’ on the roof I met one of its oldest active members, Asadullah Marwat (call sign AP2AUM). He is in his fifties and has the congenial air of a man who has been around people a lot. When he speaks, he moves from anecdote to anecdote, a sign of a life full of experiences. He is a lawyer who worked his way up to Assistant Advocate General of the then NWFP before switching careers to the United Nations. Seasons have come and gone, Asadullah Marwat has changed cities for jobs and became a grandfather but the radio has been the one constant in his life. The set is switched on in his bedroom when he comes home from work, and it stays on until he falls asleep. For her latest birthday, Marwat gave his supportive wife a walkie-talkie, after finally finding one that fit in her purse as she had been demanding for years. With not much else to do, the young Asad got one and started fiddling around with it. A certain Farrukh Sahab (AP2FI) in Lahore had a powerful radio that he used to talk to “faraway places” so Marwat’s father loaded his children into the car and drove from Swat to Lahore to meet him. They also met the late Nasir Khan, the long-time president of PARS who explained how things worked. The inspired duo bought radios and were granted licenses a year later in 1993. Connecting the hops PARS members often boast they are more adept than those who rely on radios for their professional duties. An instance that demonstrated this arose in 2021, when a PARS member was approached for help by policemen on duty in Azad Kashmir deputed at Chilehana, the southernmost entry point for district Neelum, making it a key check post. The task of solving this mystery fell to one of the Society’s experts, Mubeen Ajmal (AP2AJM) who had a degree in telecommunications engineering. He came across PARS at a seminar at the Federal Urdu University, where he studied in 2006. Ajmal asked for the exact frequencies the policemen operated on and fabricated a Yagi antenna (a popular directional antenna named after its Japanese founder) at his house with the precise measurements needed.
