Deadly classrooms

AT LEAST 30 children, most of them younger than 13, and a teacher have died in Peshawar after the roof of their private school collapsed during morning classes. Dozens more were injured, some critically. The victims had arrived at the Al Khair school expecting an ordinary day. Instead, rescue workers spent hours searching through rubble as parents gathered outside. Police say the building was structurally weak and its roof overloaded with additional construction. The owner has been arrested, while an inquiry is under way. These preliminary findings demand scrutiny. So do reports that concerns about the building’s condition had previously been raised. In June, 14 children died when the roof of a tuition centre collapsed in Lahore’s Kahna area. Two days later, another school roof collapse in Baghbanpura killed an eight-year-old. Three incidents in a few months should be enough to force an examination of the places where Pakistan’s children are taught. Across the country, schools and tuition centres operate in buildings ill-suited to accommodating large numbers of pupils. In crowded urban neighbourhoods, houses are converted into classrooms, sometimes with additional floors, makeshift rooms, and roofs carrying more weight than they were designed to support. Yet questions about construction quality, structural approvals, and inspections are too often left unanswered until a disaster occurs. The Lahore inquiry found that the Kahna tuition centre operated from an unregistered residential house. Peshawar presents a troubling contrast: according to the KP private schools regulator, Al Khair was registered in March, but technical personnel were absent from its inspection. If a school can receive registration without a structural assessment, what assurance does that certificate provide? Investigators must establish whether alterations were authorised, who inspected the premises, and whether warnings were received and ignored. They must also examine the regulators’ role, not merely the decisions of school management. The KP authorities’ decision to establish district-level committees to inspect private school buildings is welcome, but its value will depend on what follows. The committees have been instructed to examine buildings, identify hazards, and submit reports within three days. Authorities must make the findings public, close premises posing danger, and require independent engineering clearance before they reopen. Similar checks are needed across every province, including at tuition centres and schools operating inside residential properties. Building permission, school registration, and periodic safety certification should be linked, with clear responsibility assigned to qualified inspectors. Owners who make dangerous alterations must face consequences, as must officials found to have neglected their duties. Parents should not have to become structural engineers to judge whether their children are safe at school. To solve this problem, a Karachi-based developer has come up with a simple yet elegant solution. The ‘Ask Pakistan’ platform, the brainchild of Raheel Siddiqui, is a website where a chatbot can help people seeking government services obtain the required information, thus bridging the gap between the state and its citizens. Talking to Dawn, Mr Siddiqui said that many non-tech-savvy people struggled with getting the required information. Raheel Siddiqui’s ‘Ask Pakistan’ platform aims to help the less tech-savvy easily access govt services “The information is available and the government is actually good in providing directions on how to avail public services, but the problem is that there are a lot of services. The idea came to him when his friend saw a tweet about a US government website that collated all citizen-related services on one page. “He said that it will be great if we have something like this in Pakistan. It took me a couple of hours to build the basic app and I sent it to him. He used it and told me to post about it online, and I did.” Mr Siddiqui didn’t expect a lot of attention and said that he was surprised when it went viral. “I got something like 500,000 views in a few hours,” he added. According to Mr Siddiqui, privacy was always a concern, which is reflected in how the website is set up. Users can input their queries, with common questions such as “How do I renew my CNIC?” being offered as examples. The questions can be in English, Urdu or Roman Urdu. After processing the query, the chatbot gives a detailed step-by-step answer. The platform currently collates information from 52 government websites, including the Directorate General of Immigration & Passports, National Database & Registration Authority (Nadra), Federal Board of Revenue (FBR), Benazir Income Support Programme (BISP), Higher Education Commission (HEC), as well as many other federal and provincial departments. For ease of use, users can also browse by topic, and the website also links directly to the most commonly used government departments. He said, “This is being done for getting a CNIC and it can also be done for a passport or any other service. Pakistan has the PakID app, which is very good and a lot of services are being offered on it but people don’t know about it.” While internet services are available in villages across the country, there is a lack of awareness about the e-services being provided by the government. “My app focuses on solving this information gap problem. All you have to do is give it a prompt and it can guide you about the required service,” he said. What options does Indian law provide for CEC’s removal?

AMID growing calls for the resignation of India’s top election official Gyanesh Kumar for alleged voter roll manipulation, a key issue has come to the fore: the process of the chief election commissioner’s appointment, resignation and termination. The campaign to remove Gyanesh Kumar from office began after a newspaper report alleged that he was responsible for removing the names of 130 million voters from India’s electoral rolls — a figure slightly higher than Bangladesh’s entire voting population. While the Congress Party, India’s “cockroach” youth movement and others demand Kumar’s resignation, which is simple enough, Kumar’s removal from office is a lengthy and complicated affair, similar to the removal of a Supreme Court judge. Article 324 of the Indian constitution, whi­ch deals with the Election Commission, outlines the procedures for removal of the election chief. Article 324 (5) stipulates that the top election official shall not be removed fr­­om office “except in like manner and on the like grounds as a Judge of the Supreme Court”. In India, the CEC is appointed under the Chief Election Commissioner and other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023. The CEC is appointed by the president. The law stipulates that people who are serving or have served in positions equivalent to a secretary to the Indian government will be eligible. They also have to be “persons of integrity, who have knowledge of and experience in management and conduct of elections”. A search committee, led by the law minister, compiles a panel of five persons for consideration of the selection committee. The election chief and commissioners are subsequently appointed by the president upon the recommendation of this selection committee, which currently consists of the prime minister, who serves as the chair, along with the leader of opposition in Lok Sabha and a minister as nominated by the prime minister. The law governing their appointment, however, witnessed a significant change in 2023 where the constitution of the selection committee was changed. Earlier, the selection panel consisted of the prime minister, opposition leader and chief justice of India. The top judge was replaced by a union cabinet minister, for which Narendra Modi chose his home minister, Amit Shah. Critics argue that this change increases executive influence over the appointment process, as two of the three members of the selection committee would be from the ruling party. As for resigning from office, it is a simple procedure where the election chief or any of the commissioners “may, at any time, by writing under his hand addressed to the president, resign his office”. Removal The election chief’s removal follows a quasi-judicial procedure in line with the procedure stipulated by the Judges Inquiry Act, 1968. Supreme Court and High Court judges can be removed on two grounds — proven misbehaviour and incapacity. A written notice must be signed by at least 100 members of the Lok Sabha or 50 members of the Rajya Sabha. The Lok Sabha Speaker or Rajya Sabha Chair examines the notice and available information, and decides to admit or refuse the motion. If admitted, a committee is convened with one Supreme Court judge, one High Court judge and one distinguished legal expert. The election chief will have the right to defend against the allegation as the committee investigates. If the committee concludes that the charges are not proved, the motion is dropped, and the process ends. If the committee finds that allegations are true, the report is submitted to the parliament. The motion to remove the election chief will have to carry a special majority (two-thirds of voters present and voting) in both houses, in order to pass. If the motion passes both houses, a formal address is presented to the president, who issues the official order of removal.
