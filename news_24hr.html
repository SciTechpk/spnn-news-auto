All departments were not in the loop,” he told Dawn. According to credible sources at Pims, who requested anonymity, Imran’s CTA — commonly known as CT angiography — was recommended, but the test could not be completed because the facility was unavailable. Imran was subsequently taken back to Adiala jail. On Friday, after it emerged that Imran’s CTA could not be conducted at Pims, some senior doctors said the required machine was available in the radiology department, but the information was not brought to the attention of those treating Imran while he was at the hospital. The CTA machine at the Cardiac Centre had been available in 2011 but later became non-functional, the doctors said. A senior doctor added that the Cardiac Centre had a facility for conventional angiography, in which a vein is punctured and a camera is used to diagnose a blockage. The functional CTA machine, however, was available only in the radiology department, the doctor added. Another doctor reiterated that the CTA machine in the radiology department had been functional since March 2022. “The CTA is performed by a radiologist on the advice of clinicians as it uses radiation rather than requiring a vein to be punctured. However, no one advised that Imran be referred to the radiology department,” he said. He further told Dawn that after the matter came to light, Health Secretary Muhammad Aslam Ghauri visited Pims and verified that the machine was available. A senior Pims administrator, who requested anonymity, said cardiologists were asked about the test but told officials that the facility was unavailable. As a result, it was conveyed that Imran Khan’s CTA could not be conducted. “The fact is that most CTAs are referred to private facilities because the machine at the Cardiac Centre is out of order. When the issue was raised on Saturday, the radiology department was asked to provide records of CTAs conducted there. It provided just one report, while claiming that around a dozen others were also available,” he said. The officer, who has administrative responsibilities, said he had been receiving requests from the Cardiac Centre for years to arrange a machine, so no one considered referring Imran to the radiology department. “Moreover, cardiologists used to conduct CTAs themselves in the past, so they are not accustomed to referring patients to the radiology department,” he explained. Inquiry committee According to an official government statement, the premier took notice of the non-functional CTA facility at Pims and constituted an inquiry committee to determine the facts. The premier directed the committee to examine all aspects of the matter and submit a report. The committee would be headed by the Establishment Division secretary, while its members would include National Health Services secretary, Brigadier Dr Jahanzeb Ali of the Armed Forces Institute of Cardiology (AFIC), Dr Nusratullah Chaudhry, Professor Dr Nadeem Hayat Malik, Brigadier (retd) Dr Azmat Hayat of Pims, and AFIC CT angiography technician Umar Farooq. The committee may co-opt any other member if required. According to the terms of reference, the committee will determine whether Pims had the technical capability to perform CTA since 2022 and, if so, whether the required technical facilities, human resources and software were available. It will also examine the number, dates and nature of CTA procedures performed at Pims since 2022, along with relevant hospital records and other documentary evidence. The committee will investigate the coordination and referral mechanisms between the cardiology and radiology departments and assess the role of the hospital administration in providing the facility. In cases of such referrals, the committee will determine whether any particular private facility was given preference and how much business was diverted from the public hospital to private centres. The inquiry committee will further investigate any lack of coordination, mismanagement or misunderstanding between the cardiology and radiology departments that may have resulted in a loss to the public interest and identify the officers or offices responsible. Reading Thucydides in the wolf world

The term Thucydides’s Trap has caught on since Professor Graham Allison wrote his 2017 book, Destined for War: Can America and China Escape Thucydides’s Trap? The core concept comes from Greek soldier-historian Thucydides’s History of the Peloponnesian War who identifies the structural stress that occurs when a major rising power is perceived to threaten to displace an established power. My intention here is not to explain Allison’s work or his treatment of the trap but to point to how modern-day scholars and policymakers cherrypick episodes from Thucydides’s work to justify their own approaches. I believe, as the post-World War II international security architecture unravels and we enter what some describe as the wolf world, that it is important to analyse Thucydides’s history in view of the book’s hermeneutics rather than picking up episodes that suit one’s own predilections. Thucydides arrived in the United States policy corridors with Dr Henry Kissinger. He had taught the text at Harvard and was keenly familiar with it. But upon joining Richard Nixon’s administration in the White House, he sought fit to focus on the Melian Dialogue, colouring outside the lines. The one line, “The strong exact what they can; the weak concede what they must,” became the slogan of Cold War strategies. Kissinger knew that Thucydides message was far more nuanced and he was not justifying imperial overreach. But that was for the classroom. The world Kissinger had to tackle was one in which ‘man was wolf to man,’ homo homini lupus of Plautus popularised by Thomas Hobbes. That misrepresentation has generally informed US policymakers since then. Now that cruder minds are ruling America and the precepts of International Law, for whatever they were worth, have been defenestrated and America’s foreign policy has become a Kabuki theatre, a few words about Thucydides’s work are in order. The structural architecture of History of the Peloponnesian War relies on a psychological arc that connects the Plague of Athens, the Melian Dialogue (nearly 14 years later) and the Sicilian Expedition (a year later). Far from putting down a simple chronicle of military manoeuvres, Thucydides tracks the internal rot of the Athenian psyche through the description of the Plague. By tracing this trajectory, the reader discovers that the moral dissolution initiated by the plague directly fostered the intellectual hubris that were to lead to Melos, which inevitably culminated in the material ruin of the Sicilian campaign. The foundational fracture occurs in Book Two with the Plague (430 BCE). Thucydides describes a city where the sudden, indiscriminate terror of death obliterates law (nomos) and religious restraint. Faced with immediate mortality, citizens abandon honour for instant gratification, arguing that the virtuous and the wicked die alike. This lawlessness (anomia) institutionalises a new societal ethos: the abandonment of traditional justice in favour of self-interest and survival. The plague does not merely kill bodies; it extinguishes the moral constraints of the polis. Might and appetite replace civic virtue. The plague, Thucydides tells us, also claimed Pericles and his family. That death, within the hermeneutics of the text, becomes a metaphor for the demise of nomos. The emergence of more populist and aggressive figures, such as Cleon, who prioritised personal popularity and raw emotion over steady strategy, comes to symbolise anomia. This internalised lawlessness manifests later as aggressive foreign policy in the Melian Dialogue (416 BCE) of Book Five. But the seeds of Melos were sown earlier. Two years after Pericles’ death, Athens had the Mytilenian debate between Cleon and Diodotus. Cleon fiercely argued in favour of executing the entire population of Mytilene to send a message of terror. While Diodotus narrowly persuaded the assembly to reverse the decision to execute all citizens, the Athenians still exacted a brutal and bloody punishment on the city. At Melos, the Athenian envoys articulate a chilling political realism, famously declaring that, “the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.” This worldview seems to be the direct philosophical descendant of the plague’s nihilism. Having lost their respect for divine law and cosmic justice during the pestilence, the Athenians now claim that the rule of the stronger is a universal law of nature. The moral decay hardened into intellectual hubris. Athens no longer seeks to justify its empire through justice or leadership, but purely through raw, unyielding power. The immediate narrative pivot to the Sicilian Expedition (415–413 BCE) in Book Six reveals this hubris in action, dramatised through the opposing speeches of Nicias and Alcibiades. Nicias represents the ghost of old Athenian moderation. He desperately warns the assembly against overreach, cautioning that they are leaving enemies behind to chase a massive, unknown island. But the plague-damaged psyche of Athens, now driven entirely by imperial appetite and the illusion of invincibility, cannot tolerate restraint. Instead, they rally behind Alcibiades (notice the shift from the Cleon-Diodotus debate). Alcibiades is brilliant, reckless, and entirely devoid of civic responsibility. He appeals to the city’s insatiable desire for expansion, arguing that an empire must grow or it will wither. Ultimately, Thucydides’s narrative acts as a tragic warning. The lawlessness born in the plague pits became the strategic blindness that was to mock the Melians and finally drove Athens to sail into the Sicilian trap. In Sicily, the inversion is completed. The Athenians are utterly destroyed, reduced to the same desperate reliance on “hope” and suffering that they had ridiculed at Melos. Through this connection Thucydides demonstrates that the real danger of power overreach is not always external opposition but the internal erosion of the moral and intellectual clarity required to understand the limits of power. The beneficiaries of war

WARS are generally assessed through a binary lens. Strategic analysts, policymakers and the media tend to focus on a simple question — who won and who lost? The recent US-Iran war has generated a similar debate. Has Washington been able to achieve its objectives? Has Iran successfully withstood American military pressure and preserved its capacity to resist? But this binary framework overlooks another, often more consequential, dimension of modern warfare. Wars do not merely produce military winners and losers. They also redistribute wealth, accelerate technological change and create commercial opportunities for industries positioned to profit from insecurity. In the case of the US-Iran conflict, the most obvious beneficiaries may ultimately be neither Washington nor Tehran. The two capitals have both paid a substantial price. They have expended military resources, suffered economic disruption and experienced varying degrees of tactical and strategic pain. The conflict may therefore produce no clear geopolitical victor. Although, decisions on war and peace are determined by governments, it is equally difficult to ignore the political economy associated with prolonged warfare. This raises an uncomfortable question: if war creates exponential profits for some industries, would they prefer the settlement of conflicts or their prolonged continuation?
