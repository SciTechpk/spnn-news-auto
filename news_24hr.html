Retrospective tax penalties are ‘unlawful’: Supreme Court

• Says sanctions under ITO 2001 cannot apply to assessments governed by repealed law• Settles conflict between two previous rulings ISLAMABAD: The Supreme Court on Monday ruled that penalties imposed under the Income Tax Ordinance (ITO) 2001 for assessments completed before June 30, 2002 were illegal and legally unsustainable, emphasising that such penalties could not be applied retrospectively. A five-judge SC larger bench, headed by Justice Shahid Waheed, also resolved a conflict between two decisions of three-member benches — the 2009 Eli Lilly Pakistan (Pvt) Ltd case and the 2016 Islamic Investment Bank Ltd case. The question before the SC was whether penalties under Sections 182, 184 and 186 of the ITO 2001 were lawful in view of the provisions of Section 239(3) of the ordinance. Authored by Justice Aqeel Ahmed Abbasi, the 17-page judgement explained that in the Eli Lilly Pakistan case, the court had held that assessments completed under the repealed ITO 1979 must be governed strictly by the old law, while assessments made after its repeal were to be governed by the ITO 2001. However, in the Islamic Investment Bank case, the court had taken a contrary view, departing from the strict prospective approach and declaring that the right of the state to calculate and collect the correct tax liability vests at the end of each accounting year. The judgment held that the conclusion drawn by the subsequent bench of co-equal strength in the Islamic Investment Bank case was erroneous in law, whereas the decision of the earlier co-equal bench in the Eli Lilly case, treating the amendments as substantive in nature and prospective in application, reflected the correct legal position. The controversy revolves around Khadim Hussain, the respondent and an existing taxpayer falling under the jurisdiction of the director general of Regional Tax Office, Rawalpindi. The case arose when the petitioner, commissioner inland revenue (legal), RTO Rawalpindi, initiated tax proceedings against the respondent after receiving information that he had purchased a piece of property on Sept 7, 1999, without filing a corresponding tax return. Following his non-compliance with notices issued under Sections 61 and 62, a notice under Section 13(1) (aa) was served on June 28, 2007, requiring the respondent to explain the source of investment for the property. Due to the respondent’s continuous non-appearance and failure to submit documentary evidence, an ex-parte assessment was framed under Section 63 of the repealed ITO 1979, creating a tax addition of Rs300,000 for the assessment years 2000-01 to 2002-3, along with the imposition of a penalty under Section 184 of the ITO, read with Section 111 of the repealed ordinance. Subsequent compliance and reminder notices issued under Section 190 also went unheeded. Aggrieved by the assessment and pena-lty orders, the respondent filed an appeal before the commissioner of income tax (appeals), which upheld the income addition but deleted the penalties on Jan 31, 2008. The tax department preferred a second appeal before the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, challenging the deletion of the penalties. The appeal was subsequently dismissed on the ground that the penalties were legally unsustainable under Section 239. The department then approached the Rawalpindi bench of Lahore High Court by filing a reference, which was ultimately dismissed on Oct 27, 2014. Subsequently, the tax department approached the Supreme Court, seeking leave to appeal against the LHC order. Justice Abbasi explained that in the absence of clear legislative language expressly giving retrospective effect to the provisions of the ITO 2001, they could not be invoked in respect of assessments governed by the repealed ITO 1979. Any contrary interpretation, he observed, would undermine the settled principle that a taxpayer’s substantive rights and liabilities crystallise under the law applicable to the relevant assessment year and cannot subsequently be aggravated through later legislation. “It is a well-settled legal position that in a taxing statute, if an amendment is introduced which is penal in nature or increases the tax liability of a taxpayer, it applies prospectively for the tax year in which such amendment has been introduced and cannot be given retrospective effect, unless such retrospective effect is given through the amending law itself in express language,” the judgement explained. The Supreme Court consequently ruled that the penalties imposed under Sections 182, 184 and 186 of the ITO 2001 were unlawful and legally unsustainable. Accordingly, the question proposed in the instant case relating to the imposition of penalties under these sections was held to be legally not maintainable, and the civil appeal was refused. The sudden change in the office of the secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council (SNSC), amid the volatile situation in the Gulf and with the Oman-Iran talks on the future of the Strait of Hormuz yet to produce a final arrangement, has implications that extend beyond Iran’s internal affairs. The replacement of Bagher Zolghadr by veteran Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) commander Mohsen Rezaei is significant because the SNSC, constituted under Article 176 of Iran’s Constitution, brings together the country’s top civilian, military and judicial leadership for deliberation on defence and foreign policy, with its decisions requiring confirmation by the Supreme Leader. Its secretary, however, is merely an administrative official controlling the Secretariat, setting the agenda, coordinating between state institutions, preparing policy options and implementing decisions. But, what makes Rezaei’s appointment particularly consequential is that he has also been appointed the supreme leader’s representative on the Council, giving him a more powerful dual-hatted role that Zolghadr did not have. Rezaei will consequently have both control over the machinery through which national security policy is processed and a formal seat and vote on the Council as the leader’s representative, giving him a stronger institutional voice and a more direct channel to Supreme Leader Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei. The shift importantly signals the rebalancing of power sharing arrangement that had emerged in Tehran after former supreme leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei’s assassination. One should not miss out that Zolghadr’s departure followed a mini controversy in Tehran over his reported resignation and President Masoud Pezeshkian’s “reluctance” to endorse Rezaei for the position. The issue was apparently resolved after a rare meeting between Ayatollah Mojtaba and Pezeshkian, following which Khamenei appointed Rezaei as his representative on the SNSC, Zolghadr was moved to the position of the leader’s political adviser and Pezeshkian subsequently appointed Rezaei as secretary. While there is no official confirmation that the meeting was convened over the SNSC dispute, the sequence makes it difficult to treat the episode as an ordinary bureaucratic change. There is, however, a danger in reading the change simply as the triumph of what many see as ‘hardliners’ in Tehran over the ‘moderates’. Zolghadr himself was an IRGC figure, and his position on the Strait of Hormuz was not fundamentally different from that of Rezaei. Both regard Iranian control or management of the waterway as a sovereign right and an important source of leverage against Washington, and both have linked any comprehensive reopening of the strait with an end to US military pressure, removal of the naval blockade, sanctions relief and the release of Iranian assets. The difference between the two is, therefore, less about the destination than about the authority and political weight of the person now responsible for managing the journey. Rezaei brings to the position a standing that Zolghadr simply did not possess. Having commanded the IRGC from 1981 to 1997, including through most of the Iran-Iraq war, and subsequently headed the Expediency Discernment Council for more than two decades, he is deeply embedded in the security establishment and has considerable political experience. More importantly, he has been publicly involved in the present confrontation, repeatedly linking military deterrence with diplomacy and describing Hormuz as a strategic red line. That makes the appointment particularly relevant to the unresolved Hormuz question as the negotiations with Oman have increasingly moved towards technical issues, including routing, monitoring, maritime services, environmental cooperation and possible fees, with Iranian officials indicating that progress has been made on the broad geography of a future shipping arrangement. Yet Tehran has consistently maintained that reaching a technical understanding with Oman is not the same thing as restoring the Strait as a secure international waterway because, in its view, the larger problem was created by the US-Israeli military campaign and the presence of US forces around Iran. This distinction could become more important under Rezaei because his record suggests that he is unlikely to allow the technical track to become detached from the political one. Oman can negotiate the mechanics of navigation with Iran, but that would not resolve the dispute between Tehran and Washington over the US blockade, military presence and the conditions under which commercial shipping can resume. Rezaei is likely to insist that any operational reopening of Hormuz should produce demonstrable gains for Iran and that the military dimension should remain available as leverage. This should not be read either as Iran having chosen confrontation over diplomacy. In fact, the more accurate reading is that Tehran will pursue both, but has placed a stronger security establishment figure to make tactical adjustments along the course. Rezaei has been deeply sceptical of Washington’s intentions, accusing the US of using negotiations to buy time and demanding tangible confidence-building measures, including the release of frozen Iranian assets, before Tehran returns to substantive negotiations. Therefore, this change could make diplomacy more difficult in the short term, but it could also make a future negotiation more structured. A negotiator with Rezaei’s military credentials may be better placed to sell an eventual compromise to Iran’s security establishment, provided Washington is prepared to offer reciprocal and verifiable concessions. If those concessions do not materialise, however, his presence could strengthen the argument within Tehran that negotiations are merely a US ruse for buying time while retaining military and economic pressure. This is where the wider significance of the appointment lies. The June Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) had already demonstrated the difficulty of separating the immediate question of Hormuz from the larger US-Iran confrontation. Its ambiguities over navigation, sanctions, frozen assets and the sequencing of reciprocal measures eventually allowed both sides to accuse the other of violating the understanding. Iran’s subsequent position has increasingly been that navigation cannot be restored simply by resolving the technical arrangements with Oman while the US blockade and military pressure remain in place. The path forward, therefore, is unlikely to be a single comprehensive breakthrough, but a plausible sequence would begin with an Iran-Oman technical arrangement on navigation, followed by reciprocal US steps concerning the blockade and Iranian commercial shipping, which could then create enough confidence for indirect or direct US-Iran negotiations to resume. Only after that would the much more difficult issues of the nuclear program, sanctions, frozen assets and the wider regional security architecture become negotiable. For Pakistan, which has invested considerable diplomatic capital in creating the Islamabad process, the development deserves close attention. Islamabad’s interest is not simply in seeing ships move through Hormuz but in preventing the waterway from once again becoming the principal instrument of coercive competition between Iran and the US. Therefore, Rezaei’s appointment means that Tehran is now preparing for negotiations from a position of greater security establishment control and with greater emphasis on coercive leverage. To put it simply, the hardline consensus on Hormuz in Tehran remains intact, but the person now responsible for translating that consensus into policy carries considerably greater military authority, political experience and institutional weight.

Security forces have killed five terrorists, including a key commander, in an intelligence-based operation (IBO) in the Sarkini and Chedgi areas of Panjgur district in Balochistan, Radio Pakistan reported on Monday. The operation was conducted as part of Operation Radd-ul-Fitna-3 in Balochistan, the state broadcaster said. “According to security sources, the security forces conducted an intelligence-based operation against Fitna al Hindustan terrorists in Sarkani and Chedgi areas of Panjgur, killing five terrorists, including a key Fitna al Hindustan commander, Wazir,” it said. Fitna al-Hindustan is a term used by the Pakistani military for India’s alleged role in terrorism and destabilisation in Pakistan. “Four motorcycles, machine guns, heavy weapons and ammunition were also recovered from the slain terrorists,” Radio Pakistan reported. The terrorists were allegedly involved in robbery, extortion, kidnapping and attacks targeting local traders and other residents in Panjgur, it added. Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi lauded security forces on the successful operation, according to state-run APP. Paying tribute to the professionalism and operational capabilities of the security forces, Naqvi said the successful action had thwarted the nefarious designs of the terrorists and dealt a major blow to their activities in the region. Naqvi said the successful operation demonstrated the security forces’ preparedness, professionalism and unwavering commitment to combating terrorism. He stressed that the elimination of terrorist elements was essential for restoring lasting peace and stability in Balochistan and across the country. He said the government and the people remained committed to supporting the security forces in their efforts to eradicate terrorist networks and ensure peace and security throughout the country. The operation comes a day after security forces killed 15 terrorists in multiple intelligence-based operations across Balochistan. The operations were conducted in Mastung, Bolan, Washuk, Awaran, Sibi, Khuzdar, Harnai and Nushki under Operation Radd-ul-Fitna-3, according to state media. The latest operations by the security forces in Balochistan take place as the province continues to face security challenges. According to the Pakistan Institute for Conflict and Security Studies’ (PICSS) monthly security assessment released on July 31, the security situation deteriorated most significantly in Balochistan in the country last month. The overall death toll increased by 241pc in Balochistan, from 109 in June to 372 in July, the report said. Security personnel deaths in Balochistan increased from six in June to 62 in July, representing a rise of 933pc. Terrorist deaths rose from 75 to 238, while civilian deaths increased by 93pc, from 28 to 54. Eighteen members of peace committees were also killed in the province. Last month, security forces and law enforcement personnel jointly launched Operation Shaban in Balochistan in retaliation for a deadly attack on a police post at the Mangi Dam pumping station in Ziarat on July 6 when armed attackers kidnapped and killed 27 police officers.
