Punjab proposes holding local government elections in three phases in April

LAHORE: The Punjab government appears set to delay the long-awaited local government elections in the province once again, despite the polling schedule proposed by the Election Commission of Pakistan (ECP), it emerged on Thursday. Earlier this month, the electoral body proposed holding local government elections in Punjab on December 14 and sent a draft of the suggested schedule to the Punjab chief secretary for consultation. The Punjab cabinet has instead proposed holding the elections in phases on April 4, 11 and 18, following recommendations by a ministerial committee formed to consult the ECP on the conduct of local government elections in the province. In this regard, the Punjab Local Government and Community Development Department wrote to the ECP on Wednesday. The letter, which conveyed the ministerial committee’s decision, cited smog, dense fog and adverse winter weather conditions during December and January, followed by the commencement of Ramazan in February, as reasons for not holding the elections as scheduled. The letter said the cabinet’s ministerial committee had held its second meeting on October 5, during which it deliberated at length on the ECP’s draft election schedule. The committee discussed scenarios arising from the smog, dense fog and adverse winter weather conditions expected in Punjab during December and January, followed by Ramazan in February, the letter stated. It proposed holding the local government elections in phases to facilitate the effective deployment of election staff and security personnel, the efficient transportation of polling materials, better administrative oversight and improved accessibility for voters. The phased approach would also help avoid adverse weather conditions typically experienced during December and January and the period immediately following Ramazan, the letter said. The letter added that the Punjab cabinet considered the committee’s recommendations early on Wednesday and approved a proposal to hold the elections in three phases on April 4, 11 and 18, 2027, all of which fall on Sundays. The cabinet also decided that the allocation of districts among the three phases may be deliberated and thereafter mutually agreed with the ECP in a dedicated meeting, keeping in view administrative, logistical, security and geographical considerations as well as weather conditions, the letter said. On October 5, the ECP, in a meeting, upheld that it was prepared to hold local government polls across the province. On August 6, the Punjab cabinet had approved an allocation of Rs12.5 billion for holding local government elections in the province. The last local government elections in Punjab were held in 2015 under the Punjab Local Government Act, 2013. Polling took place in phases between October and December 2015, and elected councils assumed office in 2017 after indirect elections for mayors and chairpersons. Since then, local body polls in Punjab have experienced repeated delays. The PTI government had dissolved the elected local bodies in April 2019 before they completed their tenure. The move was later struck down by the Supreme Court, which restored them. They eventually completed their term on Dec 31, 2021. Successive provincial governments repeatedly amended or replaced the Punjab Local Government Act. Each legislative change required the ECP to restart preparations, including delimitation and election planning. Changing laws and administrative delays by successive governments contributed significantly to the postponement of the local body elections. Under Article 140A of the Constitution and the Elections Act, local government elections should be held within 120 days of the expiry of local bodies. Since the councils’ term ended in Dec 2021, elections were due by April 2022, but they have been repeatedly postponed.

Published jointly by Welthungerhilfe, the Institute for International Law of Peace and Armed Conflict at Ruhr University Bochum, and the Swiss organisation Helvetas under the theme “No Security Without Food Security”, the report reveals that progress in South Asia has largely stagnated, with conditions in Pakistan and Afghanistan deteriorating since 2018. In Pakistan, economic instability and sharp food price inflation have eroded households’ purchasing power and constrained access to food. Undernourishment in the country has climbed from low in 2018 to medium, while child wasting has risen toward the high threshold. Across South Asia, the situation remains serious with a regional score of 23.5. Although the region’s score has fallen substantially from an alarming 37.5 in 2000, most of that progress occurred before 2018. While Pakistan and Afghanistan have moved from the alarming to the serious category since 2000, child stunting remains very high. Pakistan’s stunting rate continues to fall, whereas Afghanistan’s has edged up since 2018. Child mortality rates in both nations have declined from very high levels in 2000 to medium levels today. The report warned that high levels of acute food insecurity will persist across the region. Heavy rains and flooding caused localised crop losses in parts of Pakistan and Bangladesh, while below-average rainfall and above-average temperatures negatively affected Afghanistan’s 2026 cereal-growing season. According to Food and Agriculture Organisation estimates, 218 million people in South Asia, including Iran, were undernourished between 2023 and 2025, representing roughly one-third of the global total. Globally, hunger is rising again after nearly two decades of progress. The 2026 global score stands at 18.1, worsening from 17.6 in 2018. Hunger has increased in 32 countries since 2018, and 57 countries are on track to miss achieving even low hunger levels by 2030. Three of the index’s four indicators — undernourishment, child stunting and child wasting — have worsened, while only child mortality has continued to decline slightly. State-based armed conflicts, now at their highest level since World War II, alongside frequent extreme weather events, are major drivers of this reversal. The war in Iran and shipping disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandab Strait are driving up fuel, transportation and fertiliser costs. Meanwhile, Russia’s war in Ukraine and Black Sea export restrictions threaten higher wheat prices, making basic supplies unaffordable in the Global South. Compounding the crisis, international spending on food security has fallen to 2016-2017 levels, forcing cuts to aid programmes and emergency rations.
