The party has also staged massive protests outside Meta offices, accusing the company of acting in the interests of Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP), in what has become one of the Facebook and Instagram parent’s biggest challenges in its largest market by users. How has Modi’s content takedown regime become stricter? For years, only India’s IT and broadcasting ministries and courts could order content to be removed, and only on narrow grounds such as national security and public order. In 2023, the government let federal and state agencies, including the police, send takedown notices for content barred under any law. And in 2024, Modi’s government launched a website, called Sahyog, which channels those notices to platforms. Elon Musk’s X has been locked in a court dispute with Modi’s government over that website, which the social media company calls a “censorship portal”. The latest dispute has arisen from critical changes to India’s IT law made in February: social media platforms must remove content deemed unlawful by an authority within three hours rather than 36, and big tech platforms such as YouTube, X and Meta must deploy technical measures such as “automated tools” to find “exactly identical” content and remove it. The changed law also requires “appropriate human oversight” and periodic review of the automated tools for accuracy and bias. Platforms that fail to comply risk losing “safe harbour”, the legal protection that shields them from liability for what users post. The digital rights group Internet Freedom Foundation has criticised the new system, saying that “censorship by automation is still censorship”. How have content takedowns surged in India? Meta’s online disclosures show it restricted access to 5.44 million items of content in India in the first half of 2026, about 130 times the 41,357 restricted in the previous six months. It restricted nearly 70,000 items in the whole of 2025. These include Facebook and Instagram posts and accounts. Meta attributes the jump to automated detection of “100 per cent identical copies” under Indian law, not to an increase in the volume of legal requests. Meta’s numbers also remain high as it has more than 500m users in India. Google’s online disclosures show it received formal requests for more than 65,000 pieces of content in India between January and June 2026, the highest ever, but it does not provide details on use of any automated tools. X’s website does not disclose the latest data on content removal requests received from the Indian government. These numbers do not include actions that social media platforms take against content for breach of internal content guidelines. How do Meta’s numbers in India compare to other countries? India and Indonesia are among the “limited” countries where Meta says it is obliged to restrict content automatically and at scale under local law. Together they accounted for about 96pc of the 27.1m items Meta restricted in specific countries between January and June this year. Meta’s 5.44m content restrictions in India were second only to Indonesia, where the number stood at 20.6m, but the company says over 20 million of Indonesia’s were “for alleged violation of local laws on gambling”. And unlike India, where Meta’s restriction numbers in the period were the highest ever, Indonesia’s numbers have been falling over time.
