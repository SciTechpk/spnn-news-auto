Water supply to parts of Karachi resumes after power at Dhabeji pumping station restored

KARACHI: Water supply to several parts of Karachi resumed in phases on Monday after electricity supply was resumed at the Dhabeji pumping station, around 22 hours after a power breakdown, Karachi Water and Sewerage Corporation spokesperson Abdul Qadir Shaikh told Dawn. The spokesperson said K-Electric (KE) had restored power supply to the pumping station at 7:20am on Monday, around 22 hours after a cable fault suspended the supply at 8:43am on Sunday. He said the “prolonged outage had resulted in a shortfall of nearly 48 million gallons of water in the city” and the affected areas included parts of North Nazimabad, Nazimabad, Gulberg Town, New Karachi, Landhi, Korangi and Scheme 33. “Following the restoration of electricity, water supply to the affected areas has resumed in phases,” the spokesperson said. The Dhabeji pumping station, Karachi’s primary water intake point, is highly dependent on uninterrupted electricity. Even brief outages result in immediate service disruptions. The power breakdown on Sunday had resulted in the temporary shutdown of three pumps at Dhabeji, which KWSC initially attributed to a cable fault. The water utility’s spokesperson had told Dawn that a technical team had reached the site to rectify the fault. However, a KE spokesperson had clarified that the fault in KDA Pumping Feeder 2, which supplies electricity to the Dhabeji Pumping Station, “is internal in nature and is not related to K-Electric”. “Power supply to 19 out of 22 pumping motors at the Dhabeji Pumping Station is continuing as normal,” the spokesperson had said, adding that a power utility team was in contact with the relevant authorities to assist KWSC in addressing the fault. Earlier this month, an electricity outage at Dhabeji had affected its K-2 Pump House, where the manifold chamber and main valves developed a fault. As a result, three major pumps at the K-2 Pumping Station had been temporarily shut down and water supply to several areas of Karachi was affected. Last month, on June 1, supply from Hub Pumping Station was also suspended to parts of Karachi due to a fault in KE’s main cable. KWSC said that the power suspension had resulted in a daily water shortfall of 85 million gallons per day (MGD) for the city. It was the third consecutive day of power failures at key pumping stations disrupting distribution across the metropolis. On May 31, the city faced a shortfall of 122 MGD after electricity supply to the North East Karachi Pumping Station was disrupted due to a fault in K-Electric’s main cable. The outage came just a day after an emergency shutdown at the Dhabeji Pumping Station, caused by a fault in a transformer at the grid station, forced 10 of the station’s 21 pumping units to shut down.

I’m on an admissions committee and universities need to talk about this

I have worked on my university’s Admission Committee for 16 years, long enough to have watched several cycles of both growth and contraction. But nothing in those years prepared me for what the last three or four have looked like, and nothing about the current decline is evenly spread. It is concentrated, sharply, among girls applying to evening programmes. When I began speaking directly with hesitant applicants and their parents to understand why confirmed seats were being given up, the reasoning was strikingly consistent, and stated without much hesitation. “In this inflation, why should I spend such a heavy amount on my daughter’s education when there is no job security for her in the future?” one mother asked me plainly. With no guaranteed job waiting at the end of an evening degree, she said, that money was better saved for her daughter’s dowry and wedding than spent on tuition. She was not the only parent to put it like that. These were not abstractions to me. They were mothers, often visibly uneasy themselves, explaining a trade-off they felt they had no real choice but to make. Every admission cycle now brings the same quiet moment in our committee meetings: a target intake number that used to be a formality is instead a subject of real concern. Punjab’s Higher Education Department reports admissions down 20 to 30 per cent across many public universities this year, and national enrolment has fallen nearly 12pc, or roughly 262,000 students, since its 2020-21 peak, according to the Pakistan Economic Survey 2025. Sitting on my own university’s admission and financial aid committees, I no longer read those figures as an abstraction. I watch them happen, one hesitant applicant at a time. It is worth pausing on why this trade-off falls so heavily on girls specifically. Evening programmes have long served students, many of them women, who could not attend morning classes because of household responsibilities or affordability, making the evening seat itself already a hard-won compromise. That families are now weighing a daughter’s tuition against her dowry tells us this is no longer only a story about the price of education. It is a story about whose future a stretched household chooses to invest in when it can no longer confidently invest in both, and a reminder that when trust in a degree’s payoff collapses, it is often girls who lose their seat first. Much of the public conversation about this decline reaches for the obvious culprits: inflation, tuition, currency depreciation, a job market that cannot absorb the roughly 445,000 graduates Pakistan produces each year, of whom by some estimates over 31pc remain unemployed. These are real pressures, and they matter. But as someone who has spent 16 years inside a public sector education department, I think the more uncomfortable diagnosis is closer to home: we in higher education have been slow to prove that what we teach is still worth four years of a young person’s life. I say this carefully, because it is not a comfortable thing for an academic to admit. But when I talk to prospective students and their parents, the hesitation is rarely just about money. It is about whether the degree on offer will actually change anything. Families increasingly compare a four-year programme against a six-month coding bootcamp, a freelancing certificate, or simply a plane ticket. Too often, and too honestly, the university does not win that comparison, because our curricula have not moved as fast as the labour market has. This is where I think the profession, including institutions like my own, needs to look inward rather than only outward. Degree programmes across the public sector still largely run on syllabi and delivery methods designed for a different economy. Employers now want demonstrable, specific skills; we are still largely credentialing years completed. Until departments treat curriculum review as a standing, funded obligation rather than an occasional exercise, no amount of scholarship money will fully answer the question families are asking: what, exactly, will this degree let my child do that they could not do otherwise? None of this means the economic pressures are secondary. Over 727,000 Pakistanis left for work abroad in 2024-25 alone, and roughly one in three graduates aged 25 to 35 say they have considered migration. For many households, a local postgraduate degree increasingly looks like a costly detour on the way to an exit visa rather than a necessary step. Addressing that will require targeted, income-linked scholarships and student-loan schemes rather than the thinly spread support most provinces currently offer. But if I had to choose one lever that universities themselves control, and are not pulling hard enough, it is trust. Departments should publish graduate employment outcomes as openly as they publish merit lists. Every programme should be able to show, in plain numbers, what its last three cohorts are doing now. Where that number is uncomfortable, it should drive reform, not silence. Where it is strong, it should be the first thing prospective students see, not an afterthought buried in a prospectus. I do not think the decline in admissions is a temporary dip that will correct itself once inflation eases. It is a verdict, delivered by thousands of individual families doing their own arithmetic on cost, risk, and return. Reversing it will take more than another extended deadline. Shifting geopolitics of tech

The development and governance of artificial intelligence should be a global effort. It should not be a solo performance by any single country, but rather “a symphony of global cooperation”, Chinese President Xi Jinping said at the opening of the World Artificial Intelligence Conference in Shanghai on July 17. The remark encapsulated the broader message that extended well beyond technology. It was as much about geopolitics and emerging contests for global influence as about AI. For Pakistan, the conference deserved far greater attention than it received. The country is among the 29 founding members of the newly established World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organisation (Waico), a China-led intergovernmental initiative formally launched on July 16. Yet its creation and the opportunities it presents for Pakistan and other developing economies received little discussion in the country’s mainstream media. Waico has the potential to become an important platform for countries seeking to participate more actively in the AI revolution rather than remaining dependent on technologies developed and controlled elsewhere. For Pakistan, where fiscal constraints, limited computing infrastructure and shortages of skilled professionals continue to impede digital transformation, collaboration on AI research, training and infrastructure could prove especially valuable. China’s push for a more inclusive global AI order offers us opportunities The Shanghai conference underscored a broader reality: artificial intelligence is no longer merely a technological issue but a central element of global strategic competition. China is increasingly positioning itself as an alternative to the United States in shaping AI governance. Its message is that access to AI models, computing resources, expertise and infrastructure should not be restricted to a handful of advanced economies but should also be available to developing countries at an affordable cost. Waico reflects this vision. Its founding members include several major Global South countries, among them Indonesia, Brazil, Malaysia, South Africa, Senegal, Russia and Pakistan. Officially, the organisation aims to promote international collaboration on AI while encouraging regulatory frameworks that ensure the technology remains safe and beneficial. At the same time, it provides Beijing with a platform to strengthen its influence over emerging international AI governance, particularly as global rules are still taking shape. This initiative unfolds against the backdrop of an intensifying US-China technology rivalry. Washington has imposed restrictions on China’s access to advanced semiconductor technologies on national security grounds, while Beijing has responded by limiting exports of critical minerals and other dual-use technologies. Increasingly, competition extends beyond hardware to encompass AI standards, supply chains and the rules governing the technology’s global development and use. Although US export controls continue to limit China’s access to the most advanced chips, Beijing retains significant strengths. It has invested heavily in computing infrastructure and data centres, dominates the production of several rare earth minerals essential to advanced technologies, and possesses substantial electricity-generation capacity to support energy-intensive AI systems. China is also distinguishing itself through international technology partnerships. At the Shanghai conference, it pledged 5,000 AI training and seminar opportunities for developing countries, announced plans for regional AI cooperation centres, and offered support for deploying Chinese AI models and applications overseas. For countries such as Pakistan, these initiatives address a pressing challenge. Artificial intelligence is rapidly becoming a general-purpose technology with applications across education, healthcare, agriculture, manufacturing, finance, defence and public administration. Nations that fail to develop domestic computing capacity, technical expertise and supporting infrastructure risk becoming long-term consumers of foreign AI systems rather than active participants in their development. This concern featured prominently throughout the Shanghai conference. Participants argued that international AI governance should encourage wider participation rather than create regulatory barriers that entrench existing technological advantages. United Nations Technology Envoy Amandeep Singh Gill similarly stressed the importance of ensuring that AI capabilities do not remain concentrated within a small number of countries and regions. China has sought to position itself as a partner in addressing this imbalance. Xi pledged support for developing countries in strengthening AI innovation, applications and governance. The appeal of this approach lies not only in access to technology but also in the accompanying investment in skills and institutional capacity. Nevertheless, strategic considerations are inseparable from these initiatives. China’s outreach coincides with parallel US efforts to build international technology partnerships and secure AI-related supply chains. Waico therefore represents more than a technical cooperation mechanism; it also forms part of a broader contest between competing visions of technological leadership. Pakistan’s National Artificial Intelligence Policy 2025 aligns with many of the priorities highlighted through Waico, particularly in expanding AI infrastructure, mobilising investment and strengthening human capital. Likewise, the AI Seekho programme’s objective of training one million people could provide a stronger foundation for meaningful participation in the global AI economy if implemented effectively. Realising these opportunities, however, will require more than policy commitments. Islamabad must identify practical areas of cooperation with China and other Waico members, including research partnerships, workforce development, computing infrastructure and technology transfer. Equally important, it should use its membership to advocate for international AI governance frameworks that reflect the needs and developmental realities of emerging economies rather than reinforcing existing technological inequalities. The global AI race is well underway. For Pakistan and other developing countries, the promise of “AI for all” will ultimately depend not on access alone but on their ability to build, adapt and govern artificial intelligence as active participants in its future.
