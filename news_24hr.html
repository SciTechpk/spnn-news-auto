A hard state?

THESE days we are repeatedly told that the Pakistani state has been soft on anarchy for far too long. This is the considered and stated position of many state officials as well as of many supporters of those in power. Common people may hold any number of positions that one finds distasteful but the same sentiments being voiced by those in power invite both scrutiny and concern. For the last three years, there has been much chatter about turning Pakistan into a ‘hard state’. No one has given an adequate definition of what this entails, but based on disparate statements and impulses, we can gather this has something to do with protecting the rights of the state and the decision-makers who staff it. These rights are being obtained via a frequently violent trade-off with the rights of the people of Pakistan. The projected consequence of such a trade-off is that protest of all shades becomes a privilege. Demands can only be voiced with due approval through vetted content and NOCs. Speech is to be regulated via defamation, criminalisation laws, and an apparently pliant judiciary till it takes on an acceptable hue, or preferably till it becomes an echo of the state’s. These impulses are neither unique to the Pakistani state nor are they exceptional to this current moment. Trysts with authoritarianism dot the country’s past and arguably lie at the roots of its state structure. British viceregal authority bequeathed an overdeveloped administrative apparatus, which has since then done exceedingly well to reproduce itself and the privileges of those who staff it. When given a chance, politicians of nearly all stripes — centralists, federalists, provincial magnates, or constituency-level bigwigs — have been more than happy to fortify this arrangement for their own and their clients’ benefits. And yet there is a question that should gnaw at anyone who thinks about the trade-off at the table. What is all of this for? If people are being asked to voluntarily or otherwise forfeit their constitutional or social rights, it only makes sense to interrogate what end goal this forfeiture is designed to serve. Beyond the protection of geographic boundaries, what other objectives do limits on individual liberty serve? Forfeiture of individual liberty is commanded by all states in service of some basic objectives. Usually the foremost of these objectives is territorial sanctity: no state tolerates geographic loss and will usually strip people of their rights, property and lives to prevent it. The Pakistani state is fairly typical in this manner. It deals with separatism/secessionism the way many other post-colonial states do, and with variable degrees of success. The bigger question on that front then is whether its strategy for preventing the rise or growth of this factor is useful at all. Beyond the protection of geographic boundaries, what other objectives do limits on individual liberty serve? Other states offer different types of answers. The case of China — paradigmatic for the 21st century — has a very simple one. It places limits on various forms of individual rights for the protection and promotion of the CCP’s endeavours. The main endeavour, since the 1970s at least, has been the growth and collective prosperity of the Chinese people via the unleashing of productive forces (labour and capital), both by the state and by private enterprise. The state under one-party rule decides the degree of this unleashing, its objectives, and ultimately the distribution of its fruits. In the previous century, socialist states decided that the true fulfilment of human life lay in getting rid of privatised forms of exploitation. And so a suspension of individual liberty was done in service of collective ownership of resources, commandeered by the state. Social democratic states in Western Europe decided to infringe on the rights of capital owners over their profit in order to redistribute benefits to the working class via taxation. The late-20th-century Asian Tigers took away social rights of the working poor to generate double-digit capitalist growth. There are other, less palatable answers too. The fascists in Germany stripped the rights, property, and lives of one minority group and various ideological dissenters (primarily the communists) in service of its ethnonationalist and expansionist agenda. At present, the ruling party in India targets individual liberties of specific segments, such as Muslims, to fulfil the cultural agenda of its majority base. In all of these examples, the use of state power is for some larger agenda; a larger agenda in the name of the public (or at least an important part of it), built on specific ideational values and postulates. Something being done in service of something bigger, grander, and to varying degrees, collective. So one must pose the question: what objective does this particular, three-year-old vintage of the ‘hard state’ serve in Pakistan? To date there is no coherent answer. We do not know what the killing of protesters — regardless of the agreeability of their demands — in AJK is going to achieve. What goal banning political movements in the peripheries serves. What Form-47ing election outcomes does. What the hollowing out of the opposition, judiciary, media or civil society helps obtain. If the answer is to maintain territorial integrity, then the vast majority of those whose rights stand stripped away carry no geographic ambitions. If the answer is to unleash GDP growth rates of the kind unseen in the last two decades, then one must reluctantly point out that the needle is yet to move up at all. If the answer is to project sovereignty and stature on the world stage at a time of global upheaval, the logical follow-up is a) whose stature and b) sovereignty in whose name? There are no ready grand answers at the end of this line of inquiry because there is likely no grand thought among those steering the ‘hardening’. The actual answers, if one were to hazard a guess, are probably several degrees more modest: the prolonging of duration in office, an improvement in personal quality of life, and the distribution of gifts and sinecures to a small coterie of loyalists at the expense of everyone else. Living with leopards in the Himalayas

In the afternoon of Feb 22, 67-year-old Bulal Maharjan, a farmer on the outskirts of Kathmandu, was sitting on his porch chopping cauliflower leaves to feed his chickens. Hearing a loud thud as a parked motorcycle toppled, he turned and watched in horror as a leopard leapt away from the fallen vehicle and lunged at him, piercing its sharp canines into his forehead, its claws lacerating his hands. Within seconds, neighbours swarmed in, and the leopard ran into a nearby house. They eventually trapped the animal inside. After nearly two hours of panic that saw two more people injured trying to control the distressed big cat, forest division officials managed to sedate it and bring the situation under control. Maharjan still can’t shake off memories of the attack. “For three days straight, I couldn’t even sleep. Every time I began to doze, the scene of this attack would jolt me up!” he says. “I think we should just get rid of this animal. Period.” Conflicts with people are growing as expanded forest land and climate change push the creatures into human settlements Sentiments like Maharjan’s are common across Nepal, where leopards have been responsible for significant loss of human life and livestock. Research shows that from the years 2000 to 2020, conflicts between humans and four large mammals — elephants, rhinos, tigers and common leopards — claimed 887 human lives and led to the killing of 1,139 animals. Of these, common leopards were the leading cause of human deaths at an average of 19 deaths per year, and the species most frequently killed. To try to bring the conflict under control, Nepal and other countries with human-large predator conflicts are exploring a range of solutions. Nepal has three ecological zones: lowland dominated by Bengal tigers, mountainous regions where snow leopards roam free, and the vast mid-hill ruled by common leopards as the top predator. While protected areas in the lowlands and mountains receive dedicated conservation funding for their charismatic predators, the mid-hills are largely managed as community forests. The celebrated community forest program is a key reason for leopards’ thriving in the mid-hills. In the early 1990s, amid worsening floods and landslides linked to forest degradation, King Birendra handed over management of large swaths of forest to local communities so that they would have stakes in conservation, and benefit from timber and fruit harvests. Today, almost 47 per cent of Nepal is covered by forest patches. About a third of that area belongs to community forests, most of which are run by people living in the mid-hills. “But what the communities didn’t realise was they also restored habitats so that the top carnivores could return,” said Kedar Baral, a wildlife biologist for Nepal’s Bagmati province. In 2020, Baral’s team conducted an analysis of leopard scat collected in the mid-hills that showed that 27pc of the animals’ diet consisted of domestic animals, while the wild ungulates that big cats typically prefer to hunt, like barking deer and wild boars, made up only 10pc. In search of sprouting leaves, barking deer venture from the dense community forests to farmland, and “as the deer enter the farmlands, that’s where the leopard follows,” says Baral, bringing them into contact with domestic livestock. Nepal’s hydropower and construction projects are also expanding into remote forested areas at higher elevations, disturbing habitat and bringing workers as well as a new hunting pressure against top predators, including common leopards, living there. That forces the leopards and their prey to leave their territory, says Madhu Chetri, a wildlife ecologist at the National Trust for Nature Conservation. Climate change might be influencing conflict patterns, too. While he was working as a district forest officer in Nepal’s western mid-hills, Baral and his team were repeatedly called to respond to common leopard attacks on local communities. Over time, he noticed a change in the pattern: Conflict incidents and human or livestock deaths were increasingly reported from higher-elevation settlements. Reporting in 2023, Baral and colleagues investigated whether climate change was driving this shift. Using data from 343 sites across Nepal based on signs of leopard scat, animal tracks and kills, he modeled habitat preferences of leopards, considering factors such as temperature, rainfall and elevation. The results suggest that leopards prefer mid-hill conditions at around 1,000 meters with moderate temperatures and high rainfall. Warming climates are likely expanding the range of suitable habitat uphill, which could allow the leopard population to grow and, as a result, have a higher risk of conflict with humans. Of course, it is a conservation success to be celebrated when numbers of threatened or endangered large carnivores increase — whether they be lynx, wolves, tigers or leopards — but it also presents new challenges, says Philip Nyhus, a conservation biologist at Colby College in Maine. Solutions to the problem have two parts, he adds: first, to keep wildlife separate from people and their livestock to prevent conflict to begin with, and second, to mitigate impacts when conflicts do occur. “A lot of attention has been given to the second piece,” he says. “What would be better is if we could prevent conflict from happening in the first place.” There are international examples to draw from. In the Argentinian Dry Chaco forest, for example, small households in woody areas have faced frequent attacks by pumas on sheep and goats. Sofia Nanni, a conservation biologist at the National Scientific and Technical Research Council of Argentina, worked with locals to come up with the idea of trying spiked leather collars on livestock, an approach that had proved successful in reducing leopard attacks on cows in countries like Iran. “We found a local artisan who made collars for dogs and we asked him if he could make collars,” Nanni says. Tracking collared goats and sheep for two years, her team found that the puma predation in uncollared animal groups was 10 times higher than that in collared groups. Several other strategies are successfully reducing leopard and other top predator conflicts globally. Still, changing attitudes and implementing effective interventions, as well as better compensation, can take years, while frustration among affected communities continues to grow. The Pakistan United Workers Federation was formed through the merger of ten moderate labour federations. PUWF is the largest and represents over one million workers in 637 unions. However, most workers’ federations lack the critical mass to build a movement to register unions and have their voices heard in the corridors of power. Although major political parties do have their wings or bureaus, the latter seldom have a formidable say in the parties’ policies. There are labour leaders who are still in a time warp. Their favourite is the issuance of appointment letters for workers because, in their opinion, an appointment letter guarantees job security, even though any worker who is underperforming, displays signs of hostility, or is a chronic absentee will soon be chucked out. Even if that worker knocks at the portals of the courts and even if reinstated, that worker will undoubtedly face resistance from management. Skills, productivity, experience, specialisation, market demand, and sectoral requirements generally determine wages of semi-skilled and skilled workers. Such wages are more appropriately regulated through market mechanisms, collective bargaining, and enterprise-level arrangements. In the majority of countries, a single minimum wage is announced by the government, which ensures that it is paid to every worker. It is standard practice and an accepted norm that the employer determines the wages of skilled employees. A skilled worker will not accept minimum wage unless in dire circumstances. Then there is the mantra of what labour leaders refer to as contract workers. This system is more aptly known as outsourcing of manpower or business functions or third-party employment. It is an employment arrangement in which workers perform services for a principal employer but are formally employed by another entity, such as a contractor, service provider, or outsourcing company. Third-party employment is widely used in both the private and public sectors, particularly for labour-intensive functions or those requiring staffing flexibility. They prefer third-party employment for specific reasons, such as operational flexibility, cost efficiency, specialised expertise, business focus, risk management, and project-based employment. Workers must join hands with employers and agitate for the rationalisation of heavy taxation measures, vociferously condemn and denounce the increase in blatant extortion demands on businesses by extremists and criminals, and influence the government to introduce favourable policies to spur industrialisation. Rethinking our wheat policy

Pakistan is set to import one million tonnes of wheat as the federal government finds strategic reserves insufficient to meet the provinces’ requirements. More surprisingly, Punjab — the country’s only wheat-surplus province — has also sought 1m tonnes. Wheat imports are not unusual for Pakistan. The country has imported wheat in several financial years over the past decade to bridge supply gaps or rebuild strategic reserves. What makes the current decision unprecedented, however, is its timing. It comes barely three months after harvest, despite repeated official claims, made until only a few weeks ago, that 29.8m tonnes of domestic production, along with over 2m tonnes of carryover stocks, would be sufficient to meet the country’s annual requirements. Adding to the puzzle, poultry feed mills are not consuming wheat this year because maize — the poultry sector’s preferred feed grain — is considerably cheaper. Moreover, the border with Afghanistan has remained closed to both formal exports and cross-border smuggling. Yet wheat prices have surged from around Rs3,300 to Rs4,700 per 40 kg since harvest — well above the import parity price. It reflects a significant supply-demand imbalance. All these developments raise serious questions about the credibility of official wheat production estimates, adequacy of strategic reserves, and the overall management of Pakistan’s wheat supply chain. The proposed 1m tonne import has revived farmers’ fears of a repeat of FY24, when imports triggered a price collapse The core issue is that, while complying with international financial institutions’ conditions, Pakistan’s federal and provincial governments pursued a wheat policy that failed to balance the interests of farmers, who produce the crop, and consumers, who seek affordable flour. They abandoned the decades-old public procurement system without first establishing a credible market-based alternative that could ensure fair returns for farmers, market stability, and adequate strategic reserves. Since Punjab province produces 77 per cent of Pakistan’s wheat, its wheat policy largely shapes the country’s wheat supply chain. Over the last three crops, it adopted three different policies: in 2024, it abruptly stopped wheat procurement from farmers; in 2025, it introduced the Electronic Warehouse Receipt system; and in 2026, it shifted to a private sector-led procurement model by engaging 11 companies. However, none of these delivered the desired results. Meanwhile, despite claiming wheat market deregulation, the Punjab government increasingly relied on administrative interventions — district-specific prices, artificial price caps, raids on stocks held by farmers and private stockists, and restrictions on wheat movement during 2025 and 2026. These measures, aimed at keeping wheat flour affordable for urban consumers, may have temporarily contained prices, but they did so at the expense of farmers by distorting market incentives. The question now is: why has this supply-demand mismatch emerged barely three months after harvest? The answer partly lies in the lower yields and changing behaviour of market participants. After recurring financial losses, farmers cut back on costly phosphate fertilisers. Moreover, heat waves and crop lodging at the maturity stage adversely affected the crop. Consequently, yields declined by an estimated three to five maunds per acre compared with the previous year’s crop. Similarly, on the supply side, learning from last year’s experience, many farmers who could meet their immediate financial needs chose to retain part of their wheat stocks. While individual holdings may be small, the cumulative effect of millions of farmers retaining just a few bags extra translates into millions of tonnes. Likewise, a large number of urban consumers, who usually purchase wheat for a few months’ consumption during harvest, appear to have stocked more this year. Concerns over the Iran conflict, rising world energy prices, and disruptions in global fertiliser supplies fuelled fears of global food inflation. Another important market development has been the growing presence of private stockists in agricultural commodity markets as investors have diverted capital from manufacturing, services, and real estate toward commodity trading. Unlike most farmers, these investors — many of them politically well-connected — have the financial capacity to hold stocks for extended periods. Together, these market dynamics have reduced market supplies and tightened wheat availability. Against this broader backdrop, the central question is: what should Pakistan’s wheat policy be for the next crop? Farmers, already on the verge of financial collapse, are demanding only a harvest-time price at least equal to import parity to offset soaring costs of diesel, electricity, fertilisers, and pesticides. Balancing this legitimate demand while safeguarding consumers’ interests calls for a fundamental rethink of wheat policy. The following three reforms are proposed as a starting point for a broader national debate. First, instead of announcing a support or indicative price, the government should allow unrestricted wheat exports during the harvest time while removing inter-provincial movement restrictions. This would automatically align domestic prices with international markets. Once this happens, exports would naturally become financially unviable for exporters. Second, the government should build and maintain strategic wheat reserves of at least 4m tonnes. These must be procured and released at prevailing market prices rather than under the current practice of administratively fixed rates. These reserves should serve as a market stabilisation tool, released whenever domestic prices rise above import parity levels. If international prices rise sharply, low-income households should be protected through targeted subsidies instead of suppressing farmers’ prices. Third, in a deregulated market, the government should recognise the legitimate role of private stockists — as in the rice and maize markets — rather than branding them as hoarders. By absorbing surplus wheat during harvest, they perform an essential supply-chain function and provide farmers with an alternative marketing channel that reduces the risk of exploitation. However, the government should promote a broad-based network of stockists to prevent excessive market concentration. In conclusion, the proposed 1m-tonne import has revived farmers’ fears of a repeat of FY24, when imports of 3.59m tonnes triggered a collapse in wheat prices. It is high time to formulate a wheat policy through public debate and consensus among all stakeholders, rather than allowing the whims of a few to shape decisions that create opportunities for vested interests and ultimately harm both farmers and consumers. The economy Pakistan forgot to measure

Pakistan’s economic discussion centres around what is measured. However, one of the nation’s most significant productive sectors remains mostly unnoticed, not due to a lack of importance, but rather because it is unpaid. Every morning, well before the offices open and the markets buzz with activity, countless individuals in Pakistan are already engaged in their work. Some dairy animals are milked before dawn. Some individuals contribute to the operation of family farms, shops, workshops, and small businesses without earning a wage. Simultaneously, countless individuals engage in meal preparation, home cleaning, childcare, elder care, water collection, animal husbandry, and a myriad of other responsibilities that ensure the smooth operation of households. Before the official start of the formal economy, a significant amount of productive work has already taken place. However, a significant portion of this effort is not reflected in Pakistan’s economic data. GDP growth, exports, investment, and employment are the main topics of discussion in Pakistan’s economy. These signs are definitely important, but they only provide a portion of the picture. They mostly record work that is officially recognised and paid for. They fail to consider a much more important issue: what happens to productive work when no wage changes hands? If unpaid market and domestic work are considered together, nearly 78pc of women are engaged in productive activity The answer reveals an economy that is both vast and remarkably diverse. Two different types of unpaid labour make up Pakistan’s hidden economy. Unpaid market labour is the first. This includes those who labour in household businesses, retail stores, livestock operations, family farms, and workshops without getting paid directly. Because they are unpaid family workers who contribute to the production of goods and services, their contributions are sometimes overlooked in economic debate. According to the 2024 Labour Force Survey (LFS), 5.9 million men and almost 8.8m women work in the unpaid market. In actuality, nearly half of working women support their families without being paid. Despite not being listed on a payroll, these workers are unquestionably a part of Pakistan’s productive economy. The second component is unpaid non-market work. Unlike unpaid market work, these activities are performed within households for the direct benefit of family members rather than for sale in the market. The Labour Force Survey recognises 14 categories of such activities, including cooking, cleaning, childcare, caring for elderly family members, collecting water, livestock care, and other household responsibilities. These services are indispensable to family well-being and labour productivity. If households did not provide them, someone would have to be hired to perform the same tasks. Yet because they take place within the home, they are rarely viewed as part of the economy. The study by the Pakistan Institute of Development Economics (Pide) reveals that although three-fourths of working-age women are outside formal economic activities, 76 per cent continue to perform non-market domestic work every week. They are at work but not counted. Women in Pakistan spend two to 10 times as many hours as men on various non-market domestic activities, specifically 2.1 times more on care work and nine times more on cooking, cleaning, and household management. Together, these two forms of unpaid work challenge one of the most common assumptions about Pakistan’s labour market that women do not work. The evidence tells a different story: once unpaid market work and unpaid domestic work are considered together, nearly 78pc of women are engaged in productive activity. Women are not absent from work; rather, much of their work falls outside conventional definitions of employment. This also sheds light on one of Pakistan’s enduring policy enigmas. If women are becoming more educated, why is women’s participation in the workforce still among the lowest in the area? The response is not merely a matter of unwillingness to engage in work. For many women, the burden of unpaid duties in the home and family businesses often leaves little time or flexibility to engage in paid work. Marriage and raising children significantly increase the burden of unpaid caregiving duties, especially during the peak years of one’s career. The issue at hand is not women’s participation in the workforce, but rather that a significant portion of their contributions goes unpaid, unacknowledged, and consequently overlooked in economic policy discussions. There are costs associated with ignoring this hidden economy. When unpaid labour is excluded from economic assessments, its significance is overlooked in policy considerations. Family businesses that depend on unpaid work continue to be neglected in productivity initiatives. The significant weight of unpaid care work is regarded as a personal household issue rather than an economic constraint. As a result, funding for childcare, rural support services, reliable public transport, water supply, and efficient infrastructure is frequently seen as merely social welfare initiatives rather than strategies that can enhance labour productivity and contribute to economic growth. Recognising this hidden economy does not mean redefining economics; rather, it means enhancing the methods of economic measurement. Pakistan should start by consistently integrating the significance of unpaid work into satellite national accounts, allowing policymakers to grasp the complete extent of productive activity. Labour statistics need to more clearly differentiate unpaid market workers, making sure that contributing family workers are recognised in employment, agricultural, and enterprise policies. Simultaneously, alleviating the strain of unpaid caregiving through accessible childcare, enhanced rural infrastructure, dependable water access, and adaptable work options would empower more women to engage in the workforce, while also recognising the economic significance of their existing contributions. Economic policy relies heavily on the quality of the evidence that supports it. When millions of people contribute to production without receiving a wage, their work does not become economically irrelevant; it simply becomes statistically invisible. Pakistan cannot hope to formulate effective labour, productivity and growth policies while ignoring a sector worth one-third of its economy. The economy Pakistan forgot to measure has always existed. The challenge now is to ensure that it is finally recognised, valued and reflected in the country’s economic decisions.
