SMOKERS’ CORNER: WHY POLITICAL RADICALISM EATS ITSELF

Political radicalism sits uneasily within the paradigm of mainstream politics. Interestingly, this phenomenon has increasingly manifested among the middle classes. The lineage of both left and right ideological extremes can be traced to two historical junctures. Left-wing radicalism finds its genesis in the 18th century French Revolution and right-wing extremism in the ascent of Nazism in 1930s Germany. In both instances, the working classes served primarily as foot soldiers. They were mobilised by middle class concepts and aspirations. The French Revolution was animated by the theories of ‘enlightened’ middle-class intellectuals and Nazism was built upon convoluted race theories conceived by middle-class thinkers. In 18th century France, radical philosophies emerged from an ascending and fledgling middle class that felt its economic and social progress impeded by the remnants of the ancien régime, such as decadent monarchs, landed nobility and an exploitative clergy. These were overthrown through bloody upheaval, driven largely by downtrodden mobs. However, when this triumph’s middle-class architects attempted to translate their radical philosophies into governance, they plunged the nation into further turmoil. This instability was checked by the rise of Napoleon Bonaparte, who established a dictatorship. The resultant political trajectory finally matured into the secular democratic system that endures in France till today. The maturation was a synthesis of clashing ideas, crystallised by an overwhelming need for moderation and stability in a country that had been turned on its head. From revolutionary France to contemporary Pakistan, radical movements have repeatedly demonstrated how the pursuit of political absolutism can become an instrument of self-destruction By the 1930s, Europe’s middle class was expanding its influence within this evolving arrangement. But the arrangement was suddenly unsettled by the expansion of socialist movements following the 1917 Bolshevik Revolution in Russia. As socialist ideologies gained political traction among the growing working classes, the middle class began to experience severe insecurity. They felt they were being squeezed between entrenched elites and increasingly active working class constituencies. In Germany, this middle class anxiety manifested as hostility toward democracy. It began being blamed for Germany’s economic ruin, military humiliation and the perceived infiltration of communism. To the middle classes, communism threatened the socio-economic hierarchy through which their own aspirations were sustained. As a result, in 1933, a significant portion of the German middle class cast its ballots for Adolf Hitler’s radical far-right Nazi party, viewing it as a bulwark against communism. Many middle class intellectuals, professionals and jurists aligned themselves with traditional economic and religious elites to elevate a fanatic to power. But Hitler’s ensuing radical agenda plunged Germany into catastrophic warfare and widespread devastation, underscoring the destructive capacity of radicalism when integrated into mainstream statecraft. Political radicalism in mainstream politics often operates through a self-consuming dynamic, ultimately destroying the very leaders and movements that champion it. According to American political scientists Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt, when radical movements enter the political mainstream, their rejection of political norms invites counter-mobilisation and their own downfall. Radical actors trigger a chain reaction that eventually dismantles their power, fractures their movements, and leaves them vulnerable to judicial and political prosecution. Levitsky and Ziblatt suggest that the main problem with bringing radical ideas into mainstream politics is that they simply do not work with normal governance or the art of working together. Governing effectively requires making deals and knowing when to compromise. Radical political figures who refuse to compromise or work within institutional rules eventually undermine their own ability to govern. They view political rules and traditions not as guidelines, but as traps set by their ‘corrupt’ rivals. Their strict ‘no-compromise’ attitude makes it nearly impossible to build stable alliances. Moderate voices inside the party are slowly pushed out or silenced. This leaves the leader surrounded only by yes-men and extreme loyalists, destroying the party’s ability to think pragmatically or adapt when a major political crisis hits. On a social level, political radicalism creates deep divisions in society and leaves the public exhausted. Indeed, tapping into anti-establishment anger can be an effective way to turn economic frustration into massive street rallies, but keeping that radical momentum alive requires the constant sustaining of the outrage. This leads to protest fatigue. According to the Dutch political scientist Cas Mudde, radical populism splits society into hostile camps, turning normal debates over policy into dramatic ‘us versus them’ battles. Over time, average citizens, the more thoughtful voters, and the business community grow weary of the continuous turmoil. The country becomes politically exhausted and economically fragile. The researchers Erica Chenoweth and Maria J. Stephan concluded that when society begins to crave stability, radical politics rapidly loses its broad electoral appeal. Its practitioners become isolated. When radical leaders urge their followers to confront state authorities directly, public sentiment gradually begins to move toward the desire for order and security, legitimising the state’s countermeasures. In 1972, the British psychologist Irving Janis demonstrated that radical movements are driven by collective arrogance and a charismatic leader’s illusion of invincibility. Such leaders often develop “hubris syndrome”, mistaking mass rallies for absolute mandates. They believe they are immune to legal or institutional limits and begin to couple their personal political survival with the destiny of the nation itself. This prompts them to make constant strategic blunders. A modern illustration of all this is Imran Khan’s Pakistan Tehreek-i-Insaf (PTI). PTI entered mainstream Pakistani politics by mobilising public discontent against established political parties. Khan put forward a radical agenda that mostly attracted the middle classes. However, after bumbling across his three-year government, and losing parliamentary support in 2022, he and his party escalated their anti-establishment rhetoric to unprecedented levels, framing the struggle for Khan’s political survival as a ‘national liberation’ movement. This radical route culminated in the violent events of May 2023, and November 2024, where party supporters targeted state and military installations. The state responded with crackdowns, mass arrests and judicial trials. Khan’s strategic reliance on populist radicalism and uncompromising confrontation resulted in his imprisonment, and then his party’s gradual organisational paralysis, demonstrating how mainstream radicalism provokes its own undoing. PTI’s undoing now seems complete with the sidelining of its one remaining provincial government in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. What’s more, the party’s core middle class constituency followed it all the way till it crumbled into a political black hole. Radical populists destroy their own political longevity by rejecting formal and informal political norms, and confusing their irrational bullheadedness as an uncompromising commitment. The German sociologist Max Weber warned of a mindset where leaders prioritise absolute moral purity over pragmatic political outcomes. Weber argued that political figures driven by radical moral conviction inevitably produce catastrophic results and trigger their own downfall, whereas enduring statesmanship requires an “ethic of responsibility”, grounded in compromise, foresight and restraint. He remained under treatment at the Sindh Infectious Diseases Hospital & Research Centre (SIDH&RC) for at least 10 days. Speaking on the condition of anonymity, a senior doctor at the hospital said that another infant patient with mpox was still in critical condition. “His key health indicators are not good. Death takes Sindh’s tally to 12, with 98 cases reported so far this year According to the sources, at least seven patients, including a SIDH&RC worker who is believed to have contracted the infection from the hospital, are currently under treatment for mpox at the healthcare facility. They all are residents of Karachi. These cases include two workers employed at the fruit and vegetable market on Superhighway and a female patient, who reportedly contracted the infection from her husband. First infant’s death in city The sources said the latest death is the first death of an infant in Karachi. Earlier, two patients with chronic lung and kidney diseases were admitted to the SIDH&RC with mpox. They died during treatment but their chronic illnesses were recorded as the cause of their deaths. With the latest death, the total number of mpox deaths recorded this year in Sindh has risen to 12 with 98 cases reported so far. The sources said nearly all victims were either infants or newborns who contracted the infection from hospital/clinic settings lacking infection control protocols. In April, an outbreak of mpox, which was earlier restricted to patients with a history of foreign travel, was reported from Khairpur, claiming the lives of at least seven infants. The babies were under treatment in the intensive care units of two hospitals. Investigations showed that these facilities lacked infection control protocols. The district of Sukkur also saw two infants’ deaths from mpox. Sources of transmission According to experts, mpox transmission occurs through close contact with someone who has mpox, contaminated bedding, towels, medical instruments, surfaces or objects. “People with mpox can spread it to animals or vice versa through close contact. Vertical and perinatal transmission is possible as well. Some people are infectious 1-4 days before getting symptomatic,” said Dr Samreen Sarfaraz, consultant infectious diseases and the Chairperson of Infection Control Services at the Indus Hospital. Most patients, she said, recover fully within 2-4 weeks without treatment, while some develop complications. “A patient remains infectious till his or her rashes are completely healed (and a fresh layer of healthy intact skin has formed underneath),” she said. Prof Faisal Mahmood, senior infectious disease specialist at the Aga Khan University Hospital, said that mpox can spread through the air to a limited extent, but only when lesions or material from lesions are aerosolised. “This may happen when a sample is taken or when lesions are in or around the airway. Compared with chickenpox, mpox is much less contagious. Chickenpox can spread through the air over a considerable distance and does not require direct contact. The same applies to measles,” he said. He further added: “Mpox outbreaks can occur where infection prevention and control measures, such as environmental cleaning, are suboptimal, or where people are in close quarters or have significant body-to-body contact.” It might be recalled that the Sindh Health Care Commission has recently sealed Ibn-i-Hassan Hospital at Malir 15 following a laboratory report confirming an mpox case in an infant, after concerns were identified regarding infection prevention and control practices. Decoding defence deals

SINCE September 2025, Pakistan has signed multilayered and multi-domain defence agreem­e­nts with several Arab and Muslim states. It has sig­­ned a Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement (SMDA) with Saudi Arabia, the Makkah Joint Def­­ence Agreement (MJDA) with Turkiye and Saudi Arabia, and a defence pact with Kuwait. Pak­istan has recently signed another defence agree­ment — the Badr agreement. Pakistan, Turkiye, Qatar, and Indonesia are signatories to this pact. These defence deals have, in the past year, increased Pakistan’s strategic relevance in the Middle East. Interestingly, each accord carries the explicit provision for joint defence production and investment in the defence industry. This clause in every defence pact reveals Islamabad’s long-term strategic objective of moving from a buyer-seller relationship to a co-production partnership with reliable partners in the region. There are four primary strategic factors that have driven Islamabad’s emphasis on the joint defence production clause in each defence pact. The India factor remains the primary driver. New Delhi’s defence budget now exceeds $80 billion, and its rapid induction of advanced platforms has widened the qualitative gap. Joint production arrangements with middle powers are likely to allow Pakistan to accelerate defence modernisation without bearing the full research and development cost. By manufacturing components and subsystems domestically, Pakistan can sustain a higher operational tempo and maintain credible conventional deterrence. Islamabad believes that strategic parity in South Asia does not require numerical equivalence. It primarily requires the ability to absorb, adapt, and indigenise technology at a pace that may prevent New Delhi from achieving decisive superiority over Islamabad. These defence deals, therefore, function as a force-multiplier for Pakis­tan’s conventional and strategic capabilities. Pakistan’s defence pacts over the past year have increased its strategic relevance. Islamabad’s second strategic objective is the diversification of defence suppliers and reduced reliance of its defence supply chain on Western weapons. Historically, Islamabad has relied on Chinese weapons but the extent of dependency has increased significantly in the last two decades. The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute has noted that this dependency is nearly 80 per cent. Although Pakistan and China enjoy unprecedented trust and depth in their all-weather strategic relationship, in theoretical terms, heavy reliance on any single external source worries Islamabad. Similarly, Islamabad is increasingly seeking to decouple its military procurement from Western-reliant supply chains. This approach is rooted in Pakistan’s historically bitter experiences with sudden embargoes and sanctions, alongside Islamabad’s broader resistance to the West’s tendency to use defence exports as political leverage over the Global South during geopolitical crises. Primarily, Islamabad is eager to cultivate robu­­st, non-Western defence partnerships to secure reliable and uninterrupted arms supplies free from the influence of foreign powers. By integrating affluent Saudi and Qatari capital with the technologically advanced Turkish weapons industry, Pakistan is building an insulated, collaborative defence network with the middle powers to achieve this strategic objective. Islamabad belie­ves that these defence deals, alongside its strategic alliance with China, may allow Islamabad to minimise its conventional vulnerabilities, help mod­­ernise its armed forces, and ultimately achieve a much higher degree of long-term strategic autonomy. Moreover, Islamabad is acutely aware that the intensifying US-China strategic competition will make it increasingly difficult to maintain a purely China-centric defence supply chain in the long run. Therefore, by building parallel defence production partnerships with Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Turkiye, and Indonesia, Pakistan is creating a middle-power buffer to avoid unwanted political pressure from the US. Interestingly, these countries are not fully aligned with either Washington or Beijing, and provide Islamabad greater room for manoeuvre. Third, the economic factor equally occupies the strategic motivations of Islamabad. Pakistan wants to enhance its ability to modernise the defence production industry and increase defence exports. Pakistan’s official defence exports stood at approximately $280m in 2024. The recently concluded deals can significantly enhance defence exports to $650-700m by 2027, crossing the $1bn mark by 2030. The Gulf states, seeking to reduce their own dependence on Western suppliers, constitute the most immediate and lucrative market for Pakistani defence exports. Joint ventures under the Badr agreement, MJDA and SMDA are designed to produce systems that meet the Gulf’s operational requirements, while carrying Pakistani certification, and, crucially, a lower price tag. Successful penetration of these markets would generate much-needed foreign exchange for Pakistan, create skilled employment, and justify further capital investment in the domestic defence industry. Beyond the Gulf, there is also potential for niche exports to African and Central Asian countries that face similar budgetary constraints and seek cost-effective, reliable platforms. The fourth objective linked to the third is to attract foreign direct investment to Pakistan’s defence sector. The joint production clauses are structured to encourage equity participation rather than simple technology licensing. Early commitments already indicate potential inflows of $1.2-1.5bn over the next five years in manufacturing facilities, testing ranges, and human capital development. These investments will upgrade the physical infrastructure of Pakistan’s public-sector defence industry. These inflows can stimulate related industries, including electronics, metallurgy, and software, while creating thousands of high-skill jobs for individuals who would otherwise migrate overseas. Time will tell whether these defence deals can deliver a meaningful technology transfer. The most important question is whether such a multipronged approach can genuinely enhance Pakis­tan’s long-term strategic autonomy or will merely redistribute its dependencies. It is also unclear to what extent these defence deals can genuinely reduce Pakistan’s strategic vulnerabilities. However, despite these uncertainties, these defence deals will bring critical capital and technical know-how, while reducing vulnerability to external supply shocks. Yet Islamabad must calibrate its exposure with precision. Over-commitment in the complex security theatres of the Middle East can risk entangling Pakistan in conflicts whose political and military logic lies beyond Islamabad’s core interests. Therefore, Pakistan must pursue these opportunities with strategic clarity and within well-defined red lines. The writer is a strategic analyst of international security. Pakistan export initiative

DOES any such initiative exist in our country, or is it a figment of my imagination? For sure, there is an initiative for remittances — but not for exports. One wonders about the presence of one and the strange absence of the other. Exports are stagnating, and remittances are rising. Are exports falling because of unwise policies or despite them? Are remittances rising because of good policy initiatives or despite them? The point here is not to denigrate the remittances initiative or its continually renewed push, but to emphasise that exports should be given greater policy priority than remittances — because, in some growth-promoting way, exports matter far more than remittances. Here we explore the reasons. This article focuses on increasing exports, not reducing remittances. In fact, remittances should continue to be promoted. Both exports and remittances matter to our economy. Both help contain the current account deficit. Exports are driven by agricultural, industrial, and services output — sectors that already provide a direct impetus to employment in the country. This impetus also has an indirect impact on the economy through spending by household members employed in export industries. Remittances follow increased output in foreign countries that employ Pakistani workers and pay them abroad. Part of their overseas earnings/ savings is sent home to their families. The economic impact of remittances is indirect and arises from spending by remittance-receiving families. Hence, both help fill the gap in the current account, but the growth impact of exports is both direct and indirect, while it is only indirect for remittances. Another, far more crucial point is that increasing exports to raise the economy’s productivity is paramount for growth. This is not true for rising remittances in Pakistan, although it is theoretically possible if a larger proportion of remittances goes towards export production. Our policymakers seem to have forgotten the importance of exports. A policy to increase exports is more likely to raise productivity than a policy to increase remittances. Increased productivity can fuel long-term sustainable growth. Exporting exposes domestic firms to international competition, better management practices, international quality standards, global supply chains, and new technologies. There is considerable empirical evidence that exporters tend to be more productive and that export expansion can generate productivity gains. For example, the World Bank reports that a 10 per cent increase in exports across its cross-country sample was associated with higher employment, earnings, and productivity. Another point to note is that exports are always preceded by the actual rise in production and employment (direct impact), as well as the indirect impact through consumption made possible by spending of wages by export-related employees. Contrast this with remittances, whose economic impact is not only indirect but also follows the receipt of remittances. This means that prioritising sustained export growth would create more employment in the country and reduce the desperation of unemployed people pushed abroad to find jobs, even at the risk of their lives. At the same time, increasing exports would have boosted the productivity our economy so badly needs. This is why remittances should not be seen as a substitute for exports. Exports should be promoted with more vigour than remittances. In terms of promoting growth, exports matter far more than remittances. Our analysts tend to blame high unemployment almost entirely on high population growth. The fact is that our unwise economic policies are at the root of frequent economic crises that cause growth and productivity to decline. The resulting rise in joblessness leads to greater migration abroad, which, in turn, drives remittances up. Of course, Pakistan’s remittance initiative has helped. But if we had implemented prudent policies that avoided various crises, exports as a proportion of GDP would have risen much faster than remittances. The export-to-GDP ratio peaked at 17.3pc in 1992 (according to World Bank data). It went down to only 10pc of GDP in 2025. The trajectory of the fall was not linear. Every onslaught of a balance-of-payments crisis precipitated a large decline. It fell to 9.6pc of GDP in 2000 in the wake of the 1998-99 crisis. With more prudent policies after the crisis, it recovered somewhat — only to decline again when imprudent policies followed, leading to other crises between 2000 and now. In contrast, our remittances have been rising almost consistently during this period as a proportion of GDP. Remittances dipped from 3.2pc of GDP in 1992 to 1.1pc of GDP in 2000, then rose to 9.9pc. The presence of a falling trend in exports and a rising trend in remittances indicates that exports always respond positively to prudent economic policies, while remittances largely show autonomous growth. Refinements in the banking infrastructure have indeed helped to increase remittances, but economic policies also played a role that pushed people into boats to find employment abroad. Subsequent remittance growth has largely occurred despite these economic policies. Pakistan’s export potential is much higher than its current level suggests. The simplest way to estimate what the level of exports could have been in 2026 is to assume the export-to-GDP ratio of 1992 (17.3pc). This translates to exports valued at $78bn, compared with the actual export value of $41bn (FY26 State Bank data). Frequent balance-of-payments crises in our country seem to have clouded the thinking of our policymakers. When they think about exports or remittances or even foreign investment, they only think in terms of foreign exchange earnings or inflows that fill a gap in the balance-of-payments. Constant firefighting and pressing short- and mid-term foreign financing needs have created a policy psyche that is obsessed with mobilising foreign exchange liquidity and has forgotten productivity and growth. Exports are badly in need of an initiative based on collective policy expertise in the country. The ‘World Social Report 2026’ says geopolitical fragmentation has considerably heightened global risks, with multiple centres of power competing for economic, military and political dominance. According to the report, the number of countries exercising substantial influence in another country has risen from six in the 1970s to 34 today. The rise in geopolitical fragmentation is also evident from the neighbouring countries relations indicator, which has substantially deteriorated since 2008, with 59 countries recording poor ties with neighbours in 2024-25, while only 19 improved. The evolving global order demonstrates a change from two competing countries (like the bipolar world during the Cold War) to a multipolar economic, military and political power structure. In theory, a more multipolar world could strengthen multilateralism, compared to a world order dominated by two competing countries or one superpower. However, the current geopolitical fragmentation and pursuit of strategic dominance risk undermining multilateral processes and global collective actions, giving rise to “minilateralism” or “coalitions of the willing”, the report says. Accelerating climate change, rapid technological advancements and geopolitical fragmentation demand a new generation of development planning that places social inclusion at the centre of economic, digital and energy transformations. The report suggests that a renewed multilateral agenda should focus on three priorities. Second, promoting wider access to technology, innovation and digital infrastructure so that developing countries can participate more effectively in the global economy. Third, reforming global governance arrangements to make them more representative, inclusive and responsive to contemporary challenges, thereby restoring confidence in collective action and strengthening accountability to development outcomes. Rising conflicts and geopolitical tensions are weakening international cooperation, disrupting trade and supply chains, and diverting financial and political resources away from development priorities. Not surprisingly, military expenditures are also rising sharply: more than half of countries increased defence spending as a share of GDP between 2020 and 2024, and all nine nuclear-armed states have expanded their arsenals since 2022. In low-income and vulnerable economies affected by conflict and fragility, including least developed countries, these are not background conditions but direct constraints on both transformation and inclusion: conflict destroys productive assets, deters investment and weakens institutions, while displacement disrupts education, health services and livelihoods.
