Interpol Pakistan issues 286 red notices in 7 months, 53 fugitives repatriated

ISLAMABAD: Interpol’s National Central Bureau (NCB) in Pakistan issued 286 Red Notices in the last seven months as part of efforts to track fugitives involved in murder, fraud, terrorism and human trafficking, according to Federal Investigation Agency (FIA) Director General Dr Usman Anwar. A Red Notice is a request to law enforcement agencies by Interpol to locate and provisionally arrest a person pending extradition, surrender or similar legal action. Anwar, who also heads NCB-Interpol Pakistan, reaffirmed the agency’s resolve on World Day of International Police Cooperation, adding that Islamabad would continue to strengthen collaboration with Interpol and global partners. NCBs serve as each country’s focal point for all Interpol activities. They connect national law enforcement agencies with those in other countries and with the Interpol General Secretariat through a secure global police communications network known as I-24/7, allowing real-time access to databases and services. Anwar said NCB-Interpol Pakistan issued a total of 290 Interpol notices during the period, including two Blue Notices and two Yellow Notices. Yellow Notices are meant to help locate missing persons, often minors, or identify people who are unable to identify themselves, while Blue Notices are issued to collect additional information. Of the 286 Red Notices, 225 were issued for suspects wanted in murder and attempted murder cases, while another 33 were issued for individuals wanted in kidnapping, corruption, robbery and cybercrime cases. The bureau also issued 19 Red Notices for financial fraud and deception, six for human trafficking and three for terrorism. Through international cooperation, 53 fugitives wanted in Pakistan for serious crimes were arrested in different countries and extradited to Pakistan during the same seven-month period. “Today’s crimes are increasingly international. Cybercrime, fugitives and stolen goods driven by organised groups — none of these respect borders,” Anwar told Dawn. “We seek information from other NCBs to help investigations in Pakistan, and we share our criminal data to assist other countries.” He added that NCBs were critical because they could trigger law enforcement action in their respective countries and work directly with counterparts, even between states without diplomatic relations. “When a crime goes beyond our national jurisdiction, we need international support to solve it. Interpol provides that platform,” he stated. According to Interpol’s framework, NCBs contribute national crime data to global databases in accordance with national laws. Red Notices, for example, alert police in all 196 member countries to wanted persons. NCB Pakistan works with domestic law enforcement agencies, other NCBs and sub-bureaus worldwide, as well as the General Secretariat’s offices, to coordinate cross-border investigations, operations and arrests. The FIA director general noted that regional cooperation was growing as countries combined resources to tackle shared threats. “We are also building capacity at home. NCBs develop training programmes for national police to raise awareness of Interpol’s tools and databases so that our officers can use them effectively on the ground,” Anwar said. “FIA NCB-Interpol Pakistan remains committed to ensuring that no fugitive finds safe haven abroad. Through I-24/7 and our network of partners, we will keep pursuing criminals and bringing them to justice.” In April this year, Interpol issued Red Notices against Bahria Town owner Malik Riaz and his son Ali Riaz, Nat­i­onal Accountability Bur­eau (NAB) Chairman Lt General (retired) Nazir Ahmed said. Last month, the National Cybercrime Investigation Agency (NCCIA) arrested two suspects and initiated the process for obtaining Red Notices for the remaining four alleged members of the “Tycoon2FA Cybercrime Network”.

Senate panel passes bill proposing death penalty for perpetrators of acid attacks

ISLAMABAD: The Senate Standing Committee on Interior on Monday passed a series of key criminal law amendments, including a bill proposing the death penalty for acid attacks, as it moved to strengthen protections for vulnerable groups and public safety. Chaired by Senator Faisal Saleem Rehman, the committee considered private members’ bills relating to acid crimes, protection of domestic workers, liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) safety, anti-rape legislation and consumer protection. The bills were passed despite opposition from State Minister for Interior Tallal Chaudhry, with PPP lawmakers also voting in favour. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Bill moved by Senator Muhammad Abdul Qadir, seeking to amend Section 336B of the Pakistan Penal Code (PPC) relating to hurt caused by corrosive substances, was passed in the absence of the mover. Citing “the recent incident of acid attack on a lady doctor in Quetta”, the bill states that despite the 2011 amendment, acid attacks continue to cause lifelong disfigurement and trauma. It proposes the punishment of “death or imprisonment for life or imprisonment of not less than 14 years and a fine of Rs2 million” to serve as a strong deterrent. The committee also unanimously passed the Criminal Laws (Amendment) Bill moved by Senator Sarmad Ali, proposing to criminalise the abuse of domestic workers. The Statement of Objects and Reasons notes that “domestic workers constitute one of the most vulnerable segments of society, often subjected to physical violence, sexual abuse, forced labour, and other forms of mistreatment by employers.” It adds that “existing provisions… do not specifically address the abuse arising from dependency and control inherent in domestic work settings”. The proposed section covers physical violence, sexual harassment, threats, forced labour, wage theft, confiscation of identity documents, denial of food or medical care, and humiliating treatment. The proposed penalties are “imprisonment for a term which may extend to 10 years and fine which may extend to Rs100,000” and “imprisonment for life and fine which may extend to Rs500,000” if the offence causes serious injury or death. The bill also makes employers liable if abuse is committed by household members, “unless the employer took reasonable steps to prevent such abuse”. Schedule II of the Criminal Procedure Code has also been amended to make the offence non-bailable and triable by a court of session. In a move to address public safety hazards, the committee passed the Criminal Laws (Amendment) Act, moved by Senator Shahadat Awan, to amend Sections 286 and 286A of the PPC to criminalise the unauthorised transfer of LPG into smaller cylinders. Noting that such decanting in residential areas and roadside setups poses severe risks, the bill increases the punishment under Section 286 from six months to two years and the fine from Rs1,000 to Rs100,000. Under Section 286A, imprisonment is increased from two to five years and the fine from Rs10,000 to Rs500,000. Explainer: Climate finance is great but how do you get it?

In the summer of 2022, monsoon rains combined with glacial melt to produce one of the worst flooding disasters in Pakistan’s history. In this flood, roughly two million homes were damaged, and thirty-three million people were affected. Nearly 8m people are reportedly displaced. The World Bank’s post-disaster needs assessment estimated total damages at close to $15 billion, with reconstruction needs exceeding $16bn. At COP27, Climate Minister Sherry Rehman turned this catastrophe into a diplomatic breakthrough, helping secure global agreement on the Loss and Damage Fund. In the eyes of many who covered the summit, Pakistan became the poster child of climate justice. Three years on, the fund exists. Pakistan’s problem has not gone away. The country’s climate financing need is very large and cannot be ignored. The World Bank estimates that a comprehensive response to Pakistan’s climate and development challenges between 2023 and 2030 will cost around $348bn. Of that, $152bn is for adaptation and resilience, and $196bn is for reducing emissions across the economy. Compared with this needed amount, the country receives very little. Donors pledged more than $9bn at the Geneva conference in January 2023 to support flood recovery. But a large share of that consisted of loans and previously committed development finance rather than fresh grants, and disbursement has been slow and uneven. People retrieve bamboos from a damaged house following rains and floods during the monsoon season in Dera Allah Yar, district Jafferabad, Balochistan, Pakistan, on August 25, 2022. — Reuters The newer Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage, operationalised in 2025 under a World Bank-hosted trustee arrangement, tells a similar story globally. It had attracted pledges of roughly $768m from 27 contributors as of April 2025; significant, until it is set against researchers’ estimates that low- and middle-income countries could need $290bn to $580bn annually by 2030 to cope with climate losses. For a country as exposed as Pakistan, ranked among the world’s most climate-vulnerable states despite contributing under one per cent of global emissions. The money on the table remains a fraction of what is needed. How climate finance works It would be easy to end the analysis there: rich countries have not paid what they owe. That is true, and important. But it is not the whole picture, and focusing only on this side hides another uncomfortable truth: Pakistan has also struggled to draw down the finance that already exists. Climate finance doesn’t work like foreign aid, in which one government transfers to another government. International Climate Finance, such as the GCF, GEF, Adaptation Fund, and the Fund for Loss and Damage, has specific procedures and requirements that countries must follow to access these funds. Each fund requires an approved organisation, called an “accredited entity,” to receive and manage the funds, and this organisation must demonstrate strong financial management systems and the ability to handle the funds responsibly and transparently. In Pakistan, the Ministry of Climate Change serves as the National Designated Authority to the GCF, which reviews and approves projects, but approval does not automatically mean the funding is received. Pakistan has struggled to develop national institutions that meet the strict requirements to become accredited to receive and manage international climate finance. For example, when Punjab’s Environment Protection Agency tried to be accredited, the process needed a detailed institutional gap assessment by an international consultancy before it could even begin. The National Disaster Risk Management Fund (NDRMF) is one of the few Pakistani institutions that has built strong capacity and remains the country’s rare success story in this area. A family takes refuge on a higher ground following rains and floods during the monsoon season in Dera Allah Yar, district Jafferabad, Balochistan, Pakistan, on August 25, 2022. — Reuters Bankable projects Officials and practitioners working on Pakistan’s climate finance say that slow progress is not caused only by limited donor funding. A major problem is the shortage of “bankable” project proposals. Bankable projects are those that are detailed, financially credible, properly designed, and able to meet the strict due diligence requirements of international funds. Preparing such proposals requires specialised technical skills. Many federal and provincial departments have limited in-house capacity and depend on continuous external support. The 18th Amendment further complicates the issue by transferring many environmental and climate-related responsibilities to the provinces. The provincial governments have much of the implementation authority and local knowledge, while the Ministry of Climate Change in Islamabad remains the main national body dealing with international climate funds. It also contributes to the slow pace of Pakistan’s engagement with these funds. Trust is another important issue that money alone cannot solve. Donors look not only at whether a project proposal is technically strong. They also look at whether the country has a history of using funds transparently and effectively. In Pakistan, some externally funded infrastructure projects have drawn criticism for their performance. For example, local communities blamed a World Bank-supported drainage canal in Sindh for worsening flood damage in 2022; such incidents increase donor concerns and make it difficult to rebuild trust quickly. Restoring donor confidence requires more than one successful project; it needs consistent transparency, proper monitoring, and regular reporting of results over time. The solution to our climate problem Pakistan’s climate problems cannot be solved by international promises alone. A realistic understanding of the challenge requires recognising two facts: the international community has not provided all the climate finance it pledged, and Pakistan has also not developed strong enough institutions and systems to effectively access and use the funding available. Closing this second gap is more within Pakistan’s control than solving the first. Pakistan needs to invest in a few strong national institutions that can meet international accreditation standards instead of depending on temporary, donor-funded assessments whenever an agency applies for funding. The NDA secretariat also needs enough staff and technical expertise to manage a growing project pipeline, and the country should also create a strong federal-provincial coordination system so provincial capacity can be turned into well-prepared, fundable projects instead of separate, fragmented requests. A woman and children sit on rope beds amidst rain waters besides their damaged house following rains and floods during the monsoon season in Dera Allah Yar, district Jafferabad, Balochistan, Pakistan, on August 25, 2022. — Reuters Pakistan also needs a proper project preparation facility to turn climate priorities into strong, bankable proposals, rather than preparing each application at the last minute. Lastly, transparency and monitoring should not be seen as burdens imposed by donors; they should be treated as essential tools for building trust and securing more climate finance in the future. Pakistan’s case for climate justice is strong, and its exposure to climate disaster is not in question. But justice delayed by institutional gaps is still, in practical terms, justice denied. If Pakistan wants the next flood, the next heatwave, the next glacial outburst to be met with finance that arrives on time and at scale, the country’s most urgent climate policy task may not lie in Baku, Belém, or wherever COP31 convenes next. It may lie in Islamabad, building the institutions capable of receiving what the world eventually agrees to send. President’s London tour sets tongues wagging

ISLAMABAD: Amid the ongoing debate over the creation of new provinces and the PPP’s outright rejection of the proposal, President Asif Ali Zardari’s extended presence in London has raised questions about the political dimensions of the tour, particularly when Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi, who had recently floated the idea, is also in the UK. While some believe the president travelled to Lon­don solely for a medical check-up, others view the visit as a political move aimed at putting pressure on the government. An official video clip of Mr Naqvi’s meeting with President Zardari also generated discussion about the president’s position. After all, it was the interior minister who ignited the fresh debate through his diatribe about the collapse of the existing governance sys­tem, and called for a ‘reset’. Following his London sojourn, Mr Naqvi rece­ntly doubled down on his dem­and for new administrative units. Addressing a National Policy Dia­logue on ‘Devol­ution of Powers and Good Governance in Pakistan’ at the University of Management and Tech­n­o­logy on Saturday, Mr Naqvi clarified that his proposed “reset” did not mean a change of government or political party, rather a comprehensive review and restructuring of the administrative system to make it responsive to the needs of citizens. He even called for a debate on granting provincial status to Islamabad, saying residents of the federal capital did not receive a share under the National Finance Commission (NFC) award. ‘Apolitical’ trip The president’s meeting with the interior minister was followed by gatherings of senior PPP leaders with Mr Zardari in London and, subsequently, the passage of a resolution by the Sindh Assembly rejecting the creation of new provinces. The sequence of events has led to speculation that the PPP’s position was discussed during the London meetings and that the Sindh Assembly resolution was adopted with the president’s consent. The issue of President Zardari’s ‘Euro trip’ and his meeting with the interior minister and Law Minister Azam Nazeer Tarar was also raised at last week’s Foreign Office briefing. However, the FO spokesperson did not respond, instead referring the question to the Presidency. When contacted, President Zardari’s spokesperson Murtaza Solangi told Dawn he was unaware of the details of the president’s visit, or when he would return to the country. “It is solely a private visit. I never seek details of his private visits,” he said. PPP Secretary General Nayyar Hussain Bokhari also maintained that the president had travelled to London only for a medical check-up and that there was nothing political about the trip. Responding to a question about Mr Zardari’s meeting with Mr Naqvi, he said the interior minister had maintained a close association with the president for a long time. “There was nothing serious if the minister met Zardari sahib to inquire about his health,” he said. Another PPP source, however, confirmed that the president’s visit had reasons beyond his medical treatment. PPP leaders meet Zardari The source said that PPP Chairman Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari, Senator Saleem Mandviwalla and Dr Asim Hussain, considered close confidants of the president, also joined him later in the UK. “President Zardari has conveyed a clear message to the government through passage of a resolution in Sindh Assembly that he and his party ruling in Sindh reject any plan to create new provinces,” the source said. According to the source, the PPP had initially agreed in principle to the creation of two new provinces in Punjab — south Punjab and Bahawalpur — but changed its position after the government began considering the creation of new federating units in all four existing provinces. The party subsequently opposed the broader proposal, and has recently questioned Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif’s silence on the politically sensitive issue of creating new provinces and sought a clear position from him. The party had initially not opposed the idea of creating additional federating units, but insisted that any such move should be undertaken in accordance with the procedure prescribed by the Constitution. Now, it is openly opposing the proposal, with the PPP-led Sindh government securing passage of a resolution in the provincial assembly rejecting any further division of the country’s four existing provinces. The development is politically significant because, although the PML-N-led government enjoys a two-thirds majority in the National Assembly, it relies on PPP support. If the PPP withdraws that support, imp­lementing any constitutional amendment aimed at creating new provinces would become considerably more difficult. IPP chief slams govt Adding to the growing criticism from within the ruling coalition, Comm­uni­cations Minister Abdul Aleem Khan, who belongs to another government ally, the Istehkam-i-Pakistan Party (IPP), also criticised the government on Sunday over what he described as its poor performance. He said people were not being provided with basic necessities and that no one who genuinely loved the country could be satisfied with the existing system. Mr Khan pointed to shortages of clean drinking water, deteriorating hospitals and the large number of children deprived of education. He also highlighted the poor condition of schools across the country. In a message on X, Mr Khan said people remained deprived of basic facilities while their hardships were increasing day by day. “If the provinces were delivering effectively under the existing system, why would the people be in such a condition?” he asked. He said there was a need to set aside prejudice and examine the actual condition of the people, arguing that the public was frustrated and deprived of basic amenities.
