HOCKEY: HOPE AND REALITY

Pakistan’s hockey team won the bronze medal at the 2026 Asian Games. Is this satisfactory or unsatisfactory? The answer to this is not straightforward. A medal is always welcome. Pakistan failed to win a medal at the previous two Asian Games. Yet, for a country with an enviable Asian Games record of eight gold medals — compared to India’s five — a bronze hardly represents a return to the top. There is another, more important, way of looking at it. The Asian Games winner earns direct qualification for the 2028 Olympics. Pakistan has missed that opportunity and will now have to negotiate the Olympic qualifying process in early 2028. Sixteen teams will compete in two eight-team tournaments for four or five Olympic berths. That is a daunting prospect for a country that has failed to qualify for the last three Olympics. So, purely in terms of results, there appears to have been little progress. But results alone do not tell the whole story. Pakistan’s hockey team managed to snag a bronze at the recently concluded Asian Games. What does it tell us about the trajectory of Pakistan Hockey? ASIAN GAMES ASSESSED Pakistan began the Asian Games ranked third in Asia and 14th in the world, behind India, which is ranked eighth in the world and Malaysia, ranked 12th. In its pool games, Pakistan had a comfortable passage against the lower-ranked sides, beating Thailand 9-0, Uzbekistan 5-1, China 3-0 and Oman 7-2. Malaysia was the only genuinely testing opponent. It was a pulsating contest, with the lead changing hands repeatedly. Pakistan led 4-3 with only four minutes remaining and, on the run of play, deserved to win. All four Pakistani goals came from penalty corners. Pakistan earned eight penalty corners; Malaysia had only three but converted all of them. Malaysia created only four or five clear chances from open play and scored twice. Pakistan, meanwhile, dominated the third and fourth quarters — apart from the final four minutes — but squandered several opportunities, including some sitters. The contest eventually ended with Malaysia edging Pakistan 5-4. That defeat forced Pakistan into a semi-final against India, the overwhelming favourites and the Pool B winners. Pakistan have not beaten their traditional rivals for more than a decade, and recent encounters have frequently produced one-sided scorelines. Once India surged into a 3-0 lead, it seemed that history was about to repeat itself. But then Pakistan came alive. Captain Abu Bakar Mahmood reduced the deficit with a penalty-corner conversion at the stroke of half-time. The match was transformed in the 45th minute, when an electric move down the right flank ended with Abdul Hannan Shahid finding the net. Soon it was 3-3. Rana Waheed completed another superb attacking move from the right to take Pakistan level. What followed was a thrilling, end-to-end contest, with chances at both ends. Then came the cruelest of endings. India’s winner arrived in the final minute, in bizarre circumstances. Abhishek’s attempted pass was deflected into the Pakistan goal by a Pakistani stick, turning what had seemed an amazing Pakistani recovery into another defeat. The bronze-medal playoff provided another comeback. South Korea led 3-1, but Pakistan recovered to win 5-3. There is a pattern here. Pakistan’s ability to fight back after falling behind has become one of the more encouraging features of the present team. As in the World Cup, the players repeatedly showed that they could recover from two or even three-goal deficits. Even when they could not turn those comebacks into victories or draws, they often transformed apparently lost contests into competitive matches. But the frequency with which Pakistan has to recover from poor starts is also a serious weakness. Why does this team so often concede early goals? Is it tactical? Is it psychological? Is it a problem with game management? The questions require serious technical analysis. But there are other positives. Sufyan Khan is developing into a world-class drag-flicker. His 12 goals made him the joint leading scorer at the Asian Games. Yet Pakistan’s use of its penalty corners against India raises questions. The team earned seven penalty corners but converted only one. Against Oman, three of Pakistan’s penalty-corner goals had come through a two-touch variation. Curiously, that indirect option was not employed against India. There is no shortage of individual talent. Hannan Shahid and Rana Waheed have established themselves as genuinely world-class forwards. Goalkeeper Ali Raza also produced an impressive performance. Captain Abu Bakar is a dependable defender who has the added value of scoring from penalty corners, and his recent signing for Melbourne Cobras in Hockey One — Australia’s premier domestic competition — is another indication of his growing stature. Meanwhile, Abdul Rahman’s goal against Wales at the 2026 FIH World Cup won him the Poligrass Magic Skill Award, selected by fans for the moments that best captured the magic of hockey. So, even during one of the darkest periods in Pakistan hockey’s history, individual talent remains available. Pakistan’s great teams have traditionally possessed a potent weapon: attacks from the right wing. The opponent’s left side is generally regarded as the more vulnerable defensive flank, making the right-wing route particularly effective. Pakistan’s second-half fightback against India provided a vivid demonstration. Both goals that brought Pakistan level originated from superb attacking movements down the right. The lesson is obvious: strengths must be identified, developed and repeatedly exploited. At the other end of the pitch, however, Pakistan continues to commit too many unforced errors. Mistimed tackles and poor clearances in deep defence gave away penalty corners and, on occasions, resulted in soft goals. The team management must address these deficiencies. The broader picture is also worth considering. The Olympics, the World Cup, the Asian Games, the Asia Cup and the Commonwealth Games are the sport’s principal title tournaments. In these competitions, positions matter. Pakistan did not achieve the desired finish in either of the two title events of 2026 — the World Cup and the Asian Games. Given Pakistan hockey’s decline over more than a decade, this was hardly unexpected. But what matters now is whether the current upward trend can be converted into sustained progress. There is no title tournament before the 2028 Olympics. That should simplify Pakistan Hockey’s priorities. From now until the Olympic qualifiers, virtually every major effort should be directed towards building a team capable of qualifying for Los Angeles. The performance graph does appear to be moving upwards. Pakistan went toe-to-toe with India despite the considerable difference in world rankings and a decade of adverse results. That should provide the players with a substantial psychological boost. But there is still a sizeable gap before Pakistan can seriously challenge the world’s top six. One indication of that gap was the difference in tempo between the 2026 World Cup and the Asian Games. The World Cup’s final and semi-finals were played at a frantic pace. The intensity and tempo at the Asian Games were nowhere near that level. Pakistan must bridge that gap. The next step should be extensive and meaningful international exposure. The PHF should seek matches against teams such as New Zealand, France and Ireland, ranked around ninth to 11th in the world. Matches against opponents of that calibre would provide a much more accurate measurement of Pakistan’s progress than victories over lower-ranked Asian sides. The objective should not merely be to play more international hockey. It should be to play the right opponents, under demanding conditions, and to learn how to compete for 60 minutes at the highest tempo. The bronze medal in the Asian Games is, therefore, neither a failure nor a triumph. Pakistan have demonstrated resilience, individual quality and flashes of the attacking hockey that once made them feared across the world. But they still concede too early, make too many defensive errors and struggle to maintain the intensity required against the very best teams. The Olympic qualifiers will tell us whether Pakistan has actually moved forward. For now, the most urgent question remains unanswered: why does a team with the ability to come back from the brink so often put itself there in the first place? What we know about Pope Leo XIV's lung cancer operation

Pope Leo XIV had a cancerous tumour surgically removed from his right lung on Tuesday in a 90-minute operation described as “successful” by the Vatican. Here are what several medical experts told AFP about the surgery and diagnosis. What is pulmonary adenocarcinoma? Pulmonary adenocarcinoma, the type of tumour removed from the Pope, generally comes from “a primary cancer,” said Dr. Juanita Mora, a medical spokesperson for the American Lung Association. She told AFP that “is a good thing” because it means the tumour is from lung cancer instead of generating from the spread of another cancer. There are two main types of lung cancer: “small cell” cancers, which spread aggressively and make up about 10 to 15 per cent of diagnoses, and “non-small cell” cancers, which make up the vast majority. “Adenocarcinoma is the most common kind of what’s called non-small cell lung cancer,” David Spigel, a medical oncologist specialising in lung cancer research at Nashville’s Sarah Cannon Research Institute, told AFP. Promising outlook David Carbone, director of the Oncology Center at Ohio State University, noted that while smoking is the most well-known risk factor for a lung cancer diagnosis, “most lung cancers that we diagnose today are in people who haven’t smoked, or (stopped) many years ago.” Lung cancer is the top cause of cancer death worldwide, according to the World Health Organisation (WHO), but the prognosis can vary significantly depending on the type of lung cancer and how advanced it is when first detected. “When we look at five-year survival rate from lung adenocarcinoma, and it’s nicely localised, the rate is approximately 63 to 67pc,” Mora said. “But if we catch it early, it becomes 80 to 98pc, especially after surgical removal.” In such a case, the patient “will achieve a cure and never have their life threatened by cancer,” Spigel added. A standard procedure Pope Leo XIV announced the presence of the lung nodule on Sunday, saying it had been “detected at a very early stage” and was localised. The Vatican said “the lower lobe of the right lung was removed” during Tuesday’s operation to remove the mass. “He had part of his right lung taken out, which is the standard procedure,” Carbone told AFP. “There’s a lot of reserve in the lung, and people can have an entire lung removed and live a normal life,” he added. “I would expect that he’d be back to completely full level of activity very soon.” What now? The question now is whether Pope Leo XIV will need additional treatment such as chemotherapy or radiation. According to the experts consulted by AFP, the answer will depend on several factors, such as the size of the cancerous mass removed, how aggressive it was, its genetic characteristics, and whether or not it has spread. A Vatican source indicated that additional testing is underway. For Carbone, the fact that the Pope has already undergone surgery is encouraging. “Because if it had spread to, for example, the lymph nodes in the centre of the chest, he would have had some treatment before surgery, which he didn’t have,” he explained. “They really do go straight to surgery only for the earliest stage tumours, which makes me think this was a very early stage tumour, and has a quite good prognosis.” He stressed that treatment for lung cancer has improved considerably over the past few decades, “and the prognosis is better than ever. So I would be hopeful for a full recovery”.

But in a variety of patriarchal systems, it is accepted that men have the exclusive right to select, demand and enforce possession over a woman, irrespective of her wishes.” The judgment stated that a woman “challenges the suitor’s perceived ownership when she exercises her fundamental right to say ‘no’”. “Under a patriarchal mindset, this rejection is taken by that man as a blow to his ‘honour’ (ghairat), which results in stalking, home invasion, acid violence or femicide,” the judgment added. The judgment also referred to several customs, such as that of ghag, to illustrate the manifestation of this mindset. Ghag (proclamation in Pashto) is an archaic customary practice where a man unilaterally claims a woman as his bride without her or her guardians’ consent. The court observed that historically, ghag was executed by firing gunshots outside the woman’s residence or making a public declaration. “Ghag acts as an informal seal or taboo intimidating other perspective suitors away, holding a woman’s marital status hostage as if she is a territory, which is now marked by this man,” the judgment said, adding if the woman or her family resisted, the suitor would frequently employ violence to enforce his claim. As a result, a woman may remain unmarried for years, or even decades, solely due to a declaration that was never followed by a legal or religious ceremony, engagement, or marriage, the court said. It added that the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Elimination of Custom of Ghag Act, 2013, had already declared the custom a cognisable, non-bailable and non-compoundable criminal offence. The judgment then referred to the customs of suara, also known as vani or badal-e-sulh, which it said operated differently from ghag, despite the fact that both portrayed situations where the decision to marry a woman was made by others for a purpose that was not in accordance with her own desires nor benefit. Suara involves a girl or young woman from a family that has committed an offence, such as homicide, given in marriage to a man from the victim’s family as a form of compensation through decision made by a jirga, and serves as a substitute for a criminal proceeding or financial compensation, the court observed. The practice was criminalised through Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2004 and further solidified by Prevention of Anti-Women Practices Act, 2011, it noted. Similarly, there was the custom of watta-satta — another example where the setting of marriage was at the cost of the bride’s well being, the judgment said. Watta-satta, which translates to “give-take”, is a “reciprocal marriage that is prevalent in our country involving a brother and sister from one family marrying a sister and brother from another family”, the judgment said. “The common thread that unites all of these customs is that in this male-dominated society having patriarchal customs and traditions, a woman’s assent to her own marriage is considered irrelevant,” the court regretted. It further stated that it is impossible to reconcile this mindset with Islam, which required woman’s free assent as a prerequisite for a valid nikah. Any adult person, either a man or a woman, was equally entitled to decide whether, when and with whom they would marry, the judgment said. That entitlement, the judgment said, was not a concession that her family, tribe or society, as a whole, had granted to her, the judgment emphasised, adding that a woman possessed this privilege in her own right. “There is no excuse for such behaviour, regardless of whether it is committed in the name of honour, custom, injured pride, or emotions.” The judgment further highlighted that Pakistan was party to the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW). Its Article 16 obliged states to ensure that a woman had the same right to freely choose a spouse and to enter into a marriage only with her free and full consent, the court noted. “A domestic custom or such prevalent mindset that nullifies this choice by force cannot be permitted to survive under the guise of local practice,” the judgment said. In the case at hand, the petitioner, who murdered a woman in cold blood, seemed to have “suffered from the mindset which led to beliefs that a woman is a commodity and it was the right of the suitor to have her, with no possibility of refusal because commodities cannot express”, the court held. The court further said that the attempt to present a woman’s lawful refusal as “provocation” or as a mitigating circumstance was a very “absurd ground” for the plea at hand because no individual had the right to deprive another of their life just because of refusing a marriage proposal. Accepting such an argument would constitute judicial endorsement of the notion that a woman’s will was subordinate to a man’s preference, the court declared. The court held that the petitioner had failed to point out any error to justify the SC’s review jurisdiction under Article 188 of the Constitution. “The submissions advanced are, in substance, a request to rearrange and reweigh matters, which this court, sitting in review, cannot undertake,” it said.
