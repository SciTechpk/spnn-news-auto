Google boosts news and link visibility in AI mode

Google announced on Thursday that it will start displaying links to websites and articles more prominently and higher up in search results powered by artificial intelligence (AI), as brands and news publishers warn of a sharp decline in their traffic. The Alphabet subsidiary began integrating more AI features in its search results over two years ago. In May 2024, it launched AI Overviews, where Google generates summarised answers to search queries, rather than only showing links as it historically did. Then, in March 2025, Google added AI Mode, which opens in a separate window and provides a more comprehensive answer and more links than AI Overviews. Now, contextual links will be featured more “more frequently” and higher up in AI-generated search content. Google will also expand worldwide its link “carousel,” which is cards displayed directly below AI Overviews that show headlines with images for relevant articles. The algorithm will prioritise articles from sites a user has previously identified as “preferred sources,” and it will also be able to pull in content from relevant social media posts and other forums. Google will also make links displayed at the end of AI-generated summaries “more engaging and prominent” for users who want to “dig deeper.” During its second-quarter 2025 results, Alphabet CEO Sundar Pichai said that AI Overviews had more than two billion monthly users in over 200 countries. Several studies have shown that replacing traditional search with AI summaries significantly reduces the rate at which users open the suggested links. According to a report by consultancy Chartbeat, news website publishers saw a 40 per cent drop in traffic coming from Google between July 2025 and July 2026. In August, Apig, a group representing daily newspapers in France, alerted the Competition Authority that AI summaries were in breach of agreements reached with news publishers in 2022.

Partners and equals

IT has been nearly a year since I last wrote an opinion piece. This was not really intentional, just a result of a feeling of hopelessness and helplessness that made me question whether writing is an exercise in futility. But recently, I was surprised when an educationist told me that my last piece on the threat to the Margalla Hills wildlife corridor, was put up in their school for students to read. I decided then, that I need to return to writing. And what better day to voice my thoughts than today. Oct 16, 2026, marks the 75th death anniversary of Shaheed-i-Millat Liaquat Ali Khan, the first prime minister of Pakistan, the Quaid-i-Azam’s close friend and supporter, and a stalwart of the independence movement. On that fateful day, he left behind his life partner, Ra’ana, and their two sons, Ashraf and Akber, in their home in Karachi, to fly to Rawalpindi to address the nation. His speech was being aired live by Radio Pakistan. He had been told his life was in danger, but he believed that the message he wanted to give was important enough to face the danger head-on. He never got to address the nation — and his written speech was never allowed to be published. To this day, we can only speculate at what he was going to reveal. His assassination created a vacuum in the political and civil life of the newly birthed Pakistan, the plan of those who stood in the shadows, having plotted to acquire this land for themselves. It also upended the lives of his family, but the resilience of his wife, although tested to the limit, helped them to move forward. Reflecting on the way my grandparents lived, and the values they instilled in their children, helps me to engage more deeply with one of the profound spiritual questions of our human existence — what is our purpose on this earth? Their values, courage, independence of thought, and intentions that led to right action, are beacons of hope, not only for me, but also for my younger compatriots searching for their truth. We don’t find this learning in the history taught in our educational institutions or in textbooks. These subjects treat history as a test question needing to be memorised, rather than as the outcome of lived choices, decisions, actions, and consequences. Integrating historical record with personal and familial insights into who my grandparents were, has allowed me to better understand them as individuals. We live in interesting times, and so did they, but they bore greater hardships than us. My grandfather was the son of a nawab, yet refused to follow the system, disobeying his father to study at Oxford, much to the horror of the nawab and his courtiers. He joined the independence movement very soon after his return. My grandmother was the only girl in her class at the University of Lucknow where she graduated with an MSc degree in economics. She was a professor in economics when she met and married my grandfather. For him, she changed her religion, and her name. And became his equal partner in the independence movement. Colonial oppression and resistance were a daily affair, as was the burden of responsibility that each carried, when they became founders and role models for their new nation and its beleaguered citizens. Their personalities were moulded by the events of their time — but this moulding led not to the hardening of hearts, nor to an inability to accept adversity. Rather, in each was developed a sense of empathy towards others regardless of their station in life, compassion that resulted in positive action, and the courage and tenacity to stand for what was right and just. After they married in 1933, Liaquat and Ra’ana visited England for their honeymoon and to persuade Mr Jinnah to return, as the time was ripe for independence. On his request, they returned to tour the provinces so that they could describe to Mr Jinnah the state of affairs of the Muslims of India, and did so in such a way that he was persuaded to return from England to lead the Muslim League. Described by the Quaid as his “right-hand man”, Liaquat was appointed by him as the secretary-general of the Muslim League. My grandmother not only advised the party on economic matters but also learned to type so that she could assist my grandfather with his official correspondence. Their marriage flourished as it was based on mutual love, mutual admiration, and mutual support. When Partition occurred, my grandparents left all their wealth behind in India. My grandfather refused to claim any evacuee property until after each migrant to this land had been compensated for what they left behind. The day he died, he owned no property and had a paltry amount of money in his account. On the flip side, they gifted their mansion in Delhi, named Gul-i-Ra’ana, to the government of Pakistan and it remains the residence of the Pakistan high commissioner. Neither of my grandparents sought to keep any wealth or property for themselves. They gave because, in my view, they were living Islam. Their purpose on this earth was clear to them — live full lives in giving to others, love and be loved, have patience, persevere even in the face of the greatest adversity, remain steadfast even though others try to tear you down, and last but not least, trust in Allah to show you the true path. I pray that more of us can live as they did. Persistence of force

WHILE the Balochistan insurgency has been simmering for many decades, in recent years, it seems to be spinning out of control. In July this year, Balochistan became the country’s most violent province, replacing KP for the first time. Some sources say there were 176 terrorist attacks, killing 438 people, including 127 security personnel, the highest ever in a month. This consistent increase in the insurgency’s lethality and spread implies a major mismatch between the threat and the measures taken to counter it. One major weakness is the imbalance between using armed force to address the challenge and subordinating the political dimension that sustains it. This begs the question: why do we continue to rely totally on the use of force to combat this insurgency, knowing that it has not worked for decades? Historically, COIN (counter-insurgency) strategies have succeeded only when they struck the right balance between armed force and political measures. At best, force can create the right conditions to end an insurgency, but only politics can use that opening to bring about peace through negotiations. In fact, David Galula, in his book Counterinsurgency Warfare: Theory and Practice, argues that a successful COIN strategy is “80 per cent political and 20pc military”. His “First Law of COIN” is that “the support of the population is as necessary for the counterinsurgent as the insurgent”, and that this support can be achieved only through political measures. Similarly, Rupert Smith, in his book The Utility of Force: The Art of War in the Modern World, defines insurgency as “a war amongst people”. He points out the strategic error of using armed force in an insurgency in a way that alienates the local population, reducing the utility of the force used. Thus, local support is crucial to maximising the utility of armed force in dealing with an insurgency and giving the counter-insurgents the upper hand. Two broad narratives currently shape the Baloch insurgency. The first one, led by Baloch political leaders like Akhtar Mengal and Dr Abdul Malik Baloch (both heads of political parties and former chief ministers of Balochistan), emphasises that the insurgency in Balochistan is predominantly a political problem and requires a political approach for resolution. The second narrative — the military-led state view — which is being implemented in Balochistan is based on the premise that the Baloch insurgency is essentially sponsored by hostile foreign intelligence agencies, and local grievances are of secondary importance. At a workshop on Balochistan at GHQ last year, the military leadership pointed out that foreign-sponsored proxies continually sought to fuel violence and undermine stability in Balochistan. They would be met with a firm response by the military, it was said. This fundamental difference in the threat assessment of the Baloch insurgency between the establishment and the local political leadership leads to divergent responses. The externalisation of the insurgency in Balochistan explains the state’s predominant use of force. Framing the Baloch insurgency as being solely driven by Indian agencies makes it a part of the broader Pakistan-India hostility, and hence defines it as a security threat and not a political challenge. This narrative, naturally, supports a purely coercive response. Interestingly, as a response to the spike in violence in Balochistan in 2026, the state has responded by adding another command to FC Balochistan — with no concomitant political initiatives. Two broad narratives currently shape the Baloch insurgency. Indeed, there is much weight in the establishment’s assertion regarding external support to the Baloch insurgents, which is backed by solid evidence, such as the arrest of Indian agent Kulbhushan Jadhav. Minorities in Pakistan continue to face rights violations despite legislative measures to protect them: HRCP

LAHORE: The Human Rights Commission of Pakistan (HRCP) on Thursday concluded that religious minorities continue to face violations of their fundamental rights — including through the misuse of blasphemy laws, forced marriages and conversions, hate speech and discriminatory practices — despite recent legislative measures aimed at protecting them. In its seventh annual report on freedom of religion, titled “Sanctuary Denied: Freedom of Religion or Belief in 2025/26”, and an accompanying press release, the HRCP highlighted that scores of individuals accused of blasphemy remained in legal limbo, while alleged perpetrators of an “organised entrapment network” had yet to be investigated. The report documented attacks on the Ahmadi community, including two attacks in Chenab Nagar in October 2025 and June 2026, and pointed out “increasingly overt attempts to exclude Ahmadis” from public and commercial life. Girls belonging to Christian and Hindu communities continued to face abductions and forced marriages, compounded by poverty and inadequate police responses, the report said. Discriminatory employment practices, controversial changes to school curricula and the marginalisation of minority concerns in mainstream media further undermined the principle of equal citizenship, it added. At the report’s launch, HRCP Director Farah Zia warned that the state’s approach to religious minorities was becoming increasingly exclusionary, according to the press release issued by the commission. Minority rights defender Nabila Feroz Bhatti highlighted the case of Kamran Gill, a Christian medical practitioner from Sheikhupura, whose dispensary was set ablaze by a mob on Oct 9 following allegations that he had shared blasphemous content on social media. HRCP Vice-Chair in Punjab Raja Ashraf said blasphemy accusations had become a “means of exploiting and endangering vulnerable people”. Arafat Mazhar, director of research and advocacy collective Shehri Pakistan, urged the public to consult lawyers representing people accused in blasphemy cases and allow courts to determine the appropriate legal procedures, rather than turning accused individuals into symbols of the broader debate over blasphemy laws. Former HRCP chairperson Hina Jilani highlighted the institutional nature of discrimination against minorities, saying: “Democracy should have no concept of ‘minority’ and ‘majority’.” On a positive note, the HRCP welcomed the passage of the National Commission for Minorities Act 2025, as well as laws in Punjab and Balochistan that raised the minimum marriageable age to 18. However, it noted that this minorities commission had yet to be constituted months after the law’s passage, raising questions about the government’s commitment to implementing the legislation. Recommendations In its recommendations, the HRCP called on the federal government to implement the National Commission for Minorities Act without further delay by constituting the commission, framing its rules and appointing its chairperson and members in accordance with the law. It urged federal and provincial governments to publish annual, disaggregated data on blasphemy cases, mob violence, forced conversion complaints and prison conditions to improve transparency and enable independent scrutiny of official claims. The HRCP also called on the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa government to enact legislation setting the minimum marriageable age at 18 for both men and women, bringing the province in line with the laws recently adopted in Punjab and Balochistan. It recommended prompt legal action against individuals and groups inciting violence, mandatory training for police and investigative agencies, including the Federal Investigation Agency, on freedom of religion or belief, and stronger safeguards for human rights defenders, lawyers and journalists working on minority rights. The report called for an end to discriminatory recruitment practices that confine many religious minorities to sanitation work, and urged an overhaul of the Evacuee Trust Property Board — a government department that administers evacuee properties — to make it more transparent, accountable and representative of minority communities. It further called for regular monitoring of seminaries linked to forced conversions and underage marriages, and recommended that religious conversions “take place in court or in the presence of a government official”. Emphasising the need to curb hate speech, the HRCP urged authorities to take legal action against incitement to violence, particularly on social media, while respecting the freedom of speech guaranteed under Article 19 of the Constitution. It called for improvements in police intelligence gathering, crowd control and community policing to prevent mob violence, as well as effective departmental accountability mechanisms to address corruption within law enforcement agencies. The report recommended reviewing school curricula to promote inclusivity and pluralism, operationalising provincial research centres envisaged under countering violent extremism laws and keeping interfaith committees active throughout the year. It also urged authorities to prevent pressure from bar associations and religious groups from influencing police and district administrations to act against particular sects or minority communities. The HRCP called for the full implementation of the Supreme Court’s 2014 judgment on religious freedoms, protection of minority places of worship and the reopening of closed places of worship. Given the severity of blasphemy charges, it recommended that investigations under Section 295-C of the Pakistan Penal Code be conducted by senior-ranking officials. It also called for public awareness campaigns explaining sensitive laws carrying capital punishment, the methods used in alleged entrapment schemes in the digital age and developments in cyber laws. The report stressed that mainstream media should be able to represent all segments of society, particularly marginalised groups and stated that no place of worship should be demolished without a clear court order issued after hearing both sides. It added that those attempting to vandalise places of worship should face legal prosecution.
