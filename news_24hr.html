Smog diplomacy

EACH winter, Lahore and Delhi engage in a peculiar rivalry, trading places at the top of the world’s air-pollution rankings. On Friday, Delhi was declared the world’s most polluted major city while Lahore slipped to seventh. For people breathing this air, the distinction offers little comfort. A change in the weather can improve a city’s ranking without removing the sources of pollution. The smog that settles over northern India and Pakistan comes from vehicle exhaust, industrial emissions, dust and agricultural fires, compounded by winter conditions that trap pollutants near the ground. Much of this pollution is generated locally, and both governments have work to do at home. In Pakistan’s Punjab, satellites detected 18,825 fires, most believed to involve crop-residue burning. Punjab’s authorities report more inspections, penalties and closures targeting polluting industries and brick kilns. Enforcement is necessary, but its effects must outlast the smog season. There is also a regional dimension that neither government can afford to ignore. Polluted air crosses the border regardless of the state of bilateral relations. Air pollution is not the meeting’s purpose, but the visit offers a rare opportunity to broach practical cooperation with Indian officials. Water demand outstrips supply in Punjab: UN agency

• FAO report says province losing 5.5bn cubic metres of groundwater annually • Highlights growing need for water resource management • Recommends setting up commission to review policies, existing strategies• Identifies shortcomings in data collection, info consolidation ISLAMABAD: A detailed assessment of Punjab’s water resources by a United Nations specialised agency has warned of serious challenges ahead, saying the province’s water demand already exceeds supply across all sectors. The Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) said groundwater was being depleted by about 5.5 billion cubic metres annually over a five-year period, while surface water resources were also under growing pressure. To assess the province’s water stress, the FAO sought expert advice from water specialists, including former Water and Power Development Authority (Wapda) chairman Shamsul Mulk and former Indus River System Authority (Irsa) chairman Rao Irshad Ali Khan. The findings, published in a report titled Water and Land Accounting in Punjab, were based on annual water accounts covering five water years from 2012 to 2017. The report highlighted the growing need for comprehensive water resources management, saying Punjab required more advanced and integrated water services across different sectors and regions. It strongly recommended the establishment of a commission to conduct a one-time review of provincial water resources, water-related policies, institutional mandates and existing strategies. The commission’s tasks would include ensuring coherence between national and inter-provincial policies and water initiatives, and establishing an integrated mechanism to assess the impact of transboundary water projects and other interventions. It would also identify overlapping responsibilities and changes needed in institutional mandates, assess water and land resources, current demand and projections until 2050, and establish a provincial coordination cell within the Planning and Development Department for day-to-day operations. Given Punjab’s major contribution to the national economy, the management of its water resources is particularly critical, especially as Pakistan’s overall water supply is considered insecure. The gap between water supply and demand has become particularly acute in Punjab’s urban areas because of limited access to freshwater and declining groundwater reserves, the report observed. Agricultural production is also increasingly at risk because of water stress and the depletion of groundwater storage, it said. Freshwater shortages have led to greater reliance on the recycling of low-quality water, while pollution from agro-chemicals and urban wastewater has intensified. The analysis estimated that Punjab needed an additional 10 billion cubic metres of usable water annually to bridge the supply-demand gap and sustain its groundwater aquifer. It stressed the need for timely intervention, including exploring ways to increase surface water resources, improve gro­undwater recharge and develop provincial mechanisms for inter-seasonal water transfers. The report said rivers flowing through Punjab and its freshwater bodies had diminished significantly, with river flows declining by about 30 billion cubic metres following the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty with India. The province has only 1.7 per cent of wetland areas, while its natural landscape has declined considerably. Rainfall remains the only source of water outside canal command areas and urban centres, it noted. The report also observed that groundwater recharge sources had diminished, reducing the province’s net renewable water resources. Punjab’s canal system operates as a run-of-the-river system for half the year and cannot draw its full authorised share of river water. “The reduction in drainage flows suggests a consistent water supply and demand gap,” it added. A technical diagnosis of the water accounts identified four main challenges: increasing spatio-temporal water scarcity; continuous groundwater depletion; competition among sectors for water and unaccounted inter-sectoral water transfers; and the need to protect annual renewable water resources, wetlands and the natural landscape. The report also identified shortcomings in data collection and information consolidation. It recommended expanding the land-use classification system to cover the entire province. The burning question

IN EARLY Nove­mber, as rice harvesting nears its end in Punjab, one can easily spot smoke rising from fields as farmers burn rice residue after harvesting, while travelling along the Lahore-Islamabad Motorway. Here and there, patches of farmland appear shrouded in smoke, with colu­mns rising above the fields and drifting across the surrounding landsca­pe. The scene is not continuous, but occurs sporadically along different stretches of the motorway during the four-ish hour drive between the two cities. For regular travellers, the smouldering fields have become a familiar seasonal sight. Yet, these scenes only offer a glimpse into what is a much larger problem. This convenient method of clearing fields for the next crop carries consequences far beyond the farms where it occurs, contributing to deteriorating air quality, public health risks and environmental degradation. It also raises an important policy question: why does this practice persist despite government restrictions and efforts to promote alternatives? Stubble burning persists not just because farmers ignore rules, but due to a lack of alternative, cost-effective methods for field clearing The roots of the problem lie in Punjab’s rice-wheat cropping pattern. Rice is cultivated on approximately 6.8 million acres, second only to wheat. Mechanised harvesting leaves substantial quantities of residue in the fields — some estimates put the amount at 8.5m tonnes. Farmers generally have 10 to 15 days between rice harvesting and wheat sowing, leaving little time to collect, transport and process the residue. Doing so also requires labour, machinery and financial resources, making burning an attractive option. This mismatch between the speed of mechanised harvesting and the limited availability of residue-management options is reflected in satellite monitoring of agricultural fires. However, different monitoring systems present widely varying estimates. According to the Pakistan Air Quality Initiative (PAQI), satellite data from Oct 1 to Nov 9 recorded 18,825 fire detections across Punjab, of which approximately 16,714 were classified as likely crop-residue fires. The highest daily count was recorded on Nov 1, with 1,102 detections. However, the Space and Upper Atmosphere Research Commission (Suparco) reports a considerably lower figure. Its data showed 643 fire anomalies between Aug 1 and Nov 10, representing a 21 per cent decline from 817 during the same period in 2025. Of these, 18 were recorded in August, 12 in September and 613 from Oct 1 onwards. The estimated area burnt also declined by 36pc, from 898.5 acres in 2025 to 579 acres in 2026. Although the figures differ, both datasets point to the persistence of the problem. But the significance of these fires goes beyond the agricultural landscape. The smoke adds to the pollution burden of urban centres, particularly Lahore, which regularly features among the world’s most polluted cities. According to PAQI, average PM2.5 concentrations in Lahore reached 126 micrograms per cubic metre between Oct 1 and Nov 9. This is more than eight times the WHO’s 24-hour guideline of 15 micrograms per cubic metre, although the figures relate to different averaging periods. Other urban centres, including Multan, Faisalabad, Bahawalpur and Gujranwala, face similarly serious pollution levels. Yet, attributing deteriorating urban air quality to stubble burning alone risks overlooking the complexity of the crisis. A former director general of the agriculture department cautions against this assumption, saying that this is only one contributor to a much wider problem. During November and Decem­ber, lower temperatures, weak winds and atmospheric inversions restrict the dispersion of pollutants. Smoke from agricultural fires mixes with emissions from vehicles, industries, brick kilns, construction activities and waste burning, as well as urban dust, intensifying the problem. Cross-border pollution may also affect regional air quality under certain wind conditions. The resulting concentration of fine particulate matter poses serious health risks, particularly to children, older people and those suffering from respiratory and cardiovascular diseases. Increased res­piratory illnesses and hospital visits can place additional pressure on households and healthcare facilities. Against this backdrop, Punjab has introduced legal, administrative and agricultural measures to discourage burning. The Punjab Environmental Protection (Smog Prevention and Control) Rules, 2023, prohibit stubble burning and provide for penalties against violators. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) conducts inspections and satellite-based monitoring, while district administrations are directed to take action. The scale of enforcement is reflected in the latest EPA figures. Between Aug 1 and Nov 10, 2026, fire-related evidence was found at 423 sites, 80 FIRs were registered and fines amounting to Rs3.08m were imposed, of which Rs2.71m had been collected.
