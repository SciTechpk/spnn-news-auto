Pakistan’s polio paradox

PAKISTAN’S latest polio case, a seven-month-old boy in Khyber district reported on Nov 6, is the ninth of 2026 and the seventh in Khyber Pakh­tun­khwa. It is another reminder of how elusive eradication has proved. After three decades of campa­i­gns, billions of dollars of international support, a field force of more than 400,000 workers, and the creation of one of the largest polio programmes in the world, the country has still not eliminated a pre­­ventable disease. Pakistan has not turned its abi­­lity to run campaigns into a system of public health protection that families trust, and that failure, which lies behind much of the refusal, is why the virus survives. The obstacles usually cited are real: insecurity, population movement, and difficult terrain keep teams from some children, while refusals, misinformation, and religious misconceptions keep others from being vaccinated. They do not account for the survival of the virus in communities that vaccination teams reach campaign after campaign and where families still turn the teams away. The reason lies beyond the vaccine, in the relationship between the state, its health institutions, and the communities they serve, and in whether those communities trust them. Political scientist Bo Rothstein has argued that trust in government depends on the quality and im­­partiality of public institutions: people trust ins­­titutions they experience as fair and reliable. Com­munication alone does not build trust, and a nati­onal health priority cannot command it. It grows out of people’s everyday experience of the state. The polio programme mobilises on a scale few public institutions in Pakistan can match. It deploys hundreds of thousands of vaccinators, maps households, monitors coverage, and returns repeatedly for the children who were missed. That capacity has saved many thousands of children from paralysis and remains essential to eradication. Beside that achievement stands the failure: the state’s ability to reach almost every household with polio drops has not become a working system of routine immunisation and basic healthcare. For many families, particularly in underserved parts of the country, the polio team may be one of the few regular points of contact with the state. A vaccinator arrives at the doorstep, sometimes several times a year, to administer polio drops. The same household may have no functioning health facility within reach, no routine childhood vaccines, no maternal care, and no clean drinking water. The state is visible when it wants to vaccinate a child, and often invisible when that child or family needs anything else. Pakistan’s polio programme has acknowledged that communities are using demand-based boycotts as a bargaining chip to draw attention to their problems. The contrast is sharpest in southern KP, where communities have boycotted polio campaigns to press the government on demands that have gone unanswered for years. Misinformation and religious opposition explain few of these boycotts. The British Medical Journal reported in December 2023 that tribal jirgas in southern KP had declared 677 boycotts during the year, with demands that ran from electricity, roads, and livelihoods to ref­u­­se collection. Pakistan’s own polio programme ack­nowledged that communities were using dem­and-based boycotts as a bargaining chip to draw attention to their problems. When a vaccination rou­­nd is the only occasion on which citizens can obtain ba­­sic services, the fault lies in the system of government as a whole. In some places a boycott is one of the few acts to which the government is compelled to respond, and communities have learnt as much. The frontline has weaknesses of its own. Too few female vaccinators and frequent changes of team members, with thin training for all of them, spoil the encounter at the door. Vaccinators do not always explain convincingly why a child needs repeated doses, least of all to parents who believe that earlier campaigns should have been enough, and programmes make too little use of trusted local figures who understand the concerns of families. Even a better trained and more persuasive team, though, cannot make up for the absence of reliable public services. Repeated visits can also do harm when families experience them as an imposition. For families who see little improvement in their lives, each arrival of a polio team can confirm that the state cares about one disease and one campaign, and little about the wellbeing of their children. The response is to make vaccination part of a broader and more consistent public health presen­­ce, by strengthening routine immunisation and pri­­mary healthcare. The outreach capacity the pol­io programme has built should find the children rou­­tine immunisation has missed and connect their families with regular health services. That takes trained health workers, reliable vaccine supplies, accessible facilities, and continuity of care, well beyond the occasional catch-up activities atta­ched to polio campaigns, so that immunisation beco­mes a regular function of the district health system. Behind this is the institutional separation between the polio programme and the wider health system. For years the National and Provincial Emergency Operations Centres have run as a well-funded parallel structure, answerable in large part to international donors, which mobilises large resources for campaign days while the provincial health departments beside them remain short of funds and bound by procedure. The dedicated funding, staffing, surveillance, and management arrangements that let the polio programme operate at scale belong inside the permanent health infrastructure of the provinces. Communities also need channels for their wider concerns, through local administration and elected representatives, and those channels have to work independently of vaccination campaigns. Otherwise the government teaches communities that withholding children from vaccination is an effective way to secure electricity, water, or roads. The responsibility rests with the Ministry of National Health Services, Regulations and Coordination, with the provincial governments, above all in KP, and with international donors, who should measure success by the strength of district health services as well as by the number of children reached in each round. Eradication depends on the campaigns, with better trained and more representative teams, and on what the campaigns cannot supply: a health system that protects children between rounds. When a vaccination team arrives at a door, the family should see it as part of a state that also protects their children and listens to them. Until that is so, Pakistan may go on running polio campaigns, and the conditions that make eradication so difficult will stay as they are. Mineral wealth

PAKISTAN’S mineral wealth offers an opportunity to attract investment, earn foreign exchange and support long-term economic growth. But turning this potential into reality will require more than promises. At the Pakistan Minerals Investment Forum 2026, Deputy PM Ishaq Dar has promised predictable policies and institutional support to investors interested in the country’s mineral resources. Such assurances are welcome. However, investors will need more than that. The country’s experience with large mining projects has not always inspired confidence. The Reko Diq dispute remains an important reminder of what can happen when agreements and government commitments become contentious. We cannot afford another dispute that damages the country’s credibility. Policy consistency must therefore extend beyond individual governments. That said, mining rights must be awarded through transparent processes, with clear contractual obligations and effective regulatory oversight. Moreover, mineral development must benefit local populations. Communities should receive their share of employment, as well as better public services and improved infrastructure. Without local support, even commercially viable projects can falter. Additionally, Islamabad must avoid becoming merely a supplier of raw materials to the world. Exporting unprocessed minerals will bring some foreign exchange, but it will not deliver wider economic gains. The real opportunity lies in transfer of technology for refining minerals and producing finished goods locally. This would require investment in processing facilities and related industries. The aim must be to build an industrial base around mineral resources rather than simply dig them out and ship them abroad. The state should also resist the temptation to present minerals as a solution to Pakistan’s long-term economic difficulties. They can contribute significantly to sustainable growth and help diversify exports. But no country has ever developed on mineral wealth or exports alone. Natural resources can provide capital for development, but they cannot replace the hard work of building productive industries. Mineral wealth must become part of a wider industrialisation strategy. ‘A moral red line’? World on cusp of autonomous weapons revolution

Lethal autonomous weapons powered by artificial intelligence are set to revolutionise warfare, but there is no proper regulatory or legal framework governing the new technology. A conference starting Monday at the United Nations in Geneva will discuss how to establish curbs and prohibitions on such weapons, called killer robots by some. But some analysts doubt the discussions at the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) will keep pace with the fast-changing weapons. AFP looks at three key questions ahead of the conference. What are LAWS? Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) “can identify, select, and engage a target without the intervention of a human operator in the execution of these tasks,” said Jean-Marc Rickli, head of global and emerging risks at the Geneva Centre for Security Policy. Autonomous weapons have long existed. An anti-personnel mine “is an autonomous weapon in the sense that it explodes when someone steps on it,” said Laure de Roucy-Rochegonde of the Centre for Geopolitics of Technology at the French Institute of International Relations (IFRI). But with the proliferation of AI-enhanced drones, such weapons are now “capable of selecting and engaging a target that was not predetermined, adapting to their environment without human intervention,” said Rickli. So the critical issue for potential regulators lies in determining the level of human control over the decision to employ such a weapon. The United States refers to an “appropriate level of human judgement” in the use of autonomous weapons.
