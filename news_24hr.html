Fact check: Viral image of Saudi defence minister kissing Iranian president's hand is AI-generated

Several social media users, including pro-Houthi accounts based on their past posts, have been sharing an image since October 26, on social media platforms Facebook and X, allegedly showing the Saudi defence minister kissing the hand of the Iranian president amid a meeting in Tehran involving the Makkah Defence Alliance. However, the image is old and edited using AI. Iran hosted senior security officials from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkiye on Oct 26 for talks with Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian. Pezeshkian called for regional unity and a coordinated stance among Islamic countries, urging them to strengthen cooperation and oppose actions by the United States and Israel. The meeting focused on regional security, the consequences of US-Israeli attacks on Iran and efforts to de-escalate tensions. Iranian officials said the four countries agreed to continue talks on regional peace and security, while Saudi Arabia said the visit aimed to promote dialogue and support mediation efforts by Qatar and Pakistan. On Oct 26, a Houthi supporter, based on their past posts, shared an image on X allegedly showing Saudi Defence Minister Prince Khalid bin Salman kissing the hand of the Iranian president. The post gained 63,000 views. A pro-PTI user also shared the same image in a similar context with the following caption: “In fact, this is the path to peace. Come to Tehran and pledge allegiance. Welcome, my beloved.” The post attracted 27,000 views on X. Several other pro-Houthi accounts further reposted the same image on Facebook and X, as seen here, here, here, here, here and here. A keyword search was conducted to determine whether any credible Saudi, Iranian, or international media outlets had published the image or reported on an incident in which the Saudi defence minister allegedly kissed the Iranian president’s hand, but yielded no reports. Investigating the image also found several visual discrepancies, such as deformities around the Saudi official’s fingers and hand as he kisses the Iranian president’s hand. There were also slight deformities and distortions on the Iranian president’s cheek and neck as well, hinting that the image was likely edited through AI. The image was then analysed using multiple AI-detection tools to assess whether it had been artificially generated or manipulated. Hive Detect reported a 99 per cent likelihood of AI generation, while DeepAI and TruthScan also indicated a 90pc likelihood that the image was edited using AI. A reverse image search of the viral photo yielded an Arab News report covering the funeral procession of assassinated Iranian Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei on July 3, 2026. The report stated that Saudi Deputy Foreign Minister Waleed El Khereiji attended the ceremony, where he met Iranian officials, including President Masoud Pezeshkian. The featured image in the Arab News report shows a strong resemblance to the viral photo, with the same background and the officials wearing similar attire. However, a comparison of the two images reveals a key difference. The viral image depicts the Saudi official kissing the Iranian president’s hand, whereas the original image shows him simply shaking hands with the president. The same report, featuring the same image, was also published by Saudi news outlet Saudi Gazette and the official Saudi Press Agency. Therefore, the claim that a viral image shows the Saudi defence minister kissing the hand of the Iranian president is false. The image is edited using AI, and the older original picture does not even feature the Saudi defence minister. Chromebook scheme

THE government’s plan to distribute 500,000 Chromebooks to students over two years could help narrow Pakistan’s digital divide. The devices, which will come with MiFi internet connectivity, are meant for students handpicked under the scheme. In a country where access to computers and reliable internet still depends heavily on family income and location, that matters. A student who cannot afford a laptop should not be locked out of online learning, digital skills or job opportunities. But the value of the scheme will depend on how it is carried out. The state should publish clear eligibility criteria, ensure that poorer students are not crowded out by those who already have devices, and explain how the refurbished Chromebooks will be procured, tested and distributed. The total cost should also be disclosed, along with safeguards against political influence in selecting recipients and suppliers. There should also be a plan for repairs, replacement and technical support. A device that stops working after a few months, or cannot connect reliably outside major cities, will do little to close the gap it was meant to address. More importantly, access to hardware is only one part of Pakistan’s education problem. A Chromebook cannot compensate for weak teaching, poor literacy, overcrowded classrooms or schools without dependable electricity and internet access. Nor will handing out devices automatically improve learning. Teachers need training to use digital tools well, students need access to useful content, and schools need systems that can blend technology with ordinary classroom instruction. The government should therefore set measurable goals for the scheme: whether students use the devices regularly for learning, whether their digital skills improve, and whether this leads to better educational outcomes. Independent evaluation would help determine if the programme deserves expansion. There is also a case for giving students in remote and low-income areas greater weight, since they stand to benefit most from access they currently lack. Pakistan has launched laptop schemes before; what matters now is whether this one produces more than a distribution ceremony. At UNGA debate, Pakistan warns against adding more permanent members to Security Council, extending veto privileges

Pakistan has warned that adding more permanent members to the United Nations Security Council (UNSC) and extending veto privileges would “deepen existing inequalities, perpetuate historical advantages and increase the risk of Council paralysis”. The counsellor at the Pakistan Mission at UN, Gul Qaiser Sarwani, presented these views at the UN General Assembly during a debate on the use of the veto. He said Pakistan called for “comprehensive, democratic and equitable reform of the UN Security Council” and stressed that the misuse of veto power undermined UNSC’s effectiveness, weakened the credibility of the UN and perpetuated inequalities within the international system. “Strategic rivalries and narrow interests among the Council’s permanent members had repeatedly impeded collective action on international peace and security,” he said, emphasising the “unique legitimacy” of the UNGA, whose universal membership and democratic decision-making enabled it to respond when the UNSC unable to act, including due to the exercise of the veto. The Pakistani delegate opposed the creation of any new permanent seats in an expanded Security Council. On proposals to regulate the use of the veto, he said Pakistan favoured its abolition or, at a minimum, severe restrictions on its use. He emphasised that the veto must not be expanded and reaffirmed Pakistan’s support for General Assembly resolution 76/262, which provides for a UNGA debate following the exercise of a veto to promote greater accountability. Sarwani stressed that any measures to restrain the veto must remain consistent with the UN Charter. He called for a balanced approach combining restrictions on veto use with enhanced representation and a stronger role for elected members of the UNSC, making its decision-making “more democratic and providing an effective check on the exercise of veto power”. The delegate further emphasised that the question of the veto must be addressed alongside the other four interrelated clusters under the Intergovernmental Negotiations on Security Council reform, as part of a comprehensive reform package. Why the world still needs World Trade Organisation

The World Trade Organisation has spent much of its recent life being told it is obsolete. In its World Trade Report 2026 launched last month, the Geneva-based body tells us what would happen if the rules are simply allowed to ‘wear away.’ It says the outcome would be quite expensive for the global economy. It models a world that breaks into rival geopolitical blocs and finds global GDP would be 5.1 per cent lower by 2050 than it would otherwise be, with exports dropping by 18.6pc. In a harsher scenario, where multilateral cooperation collapses, GDP falls 6.9pc and exports by nearly 27pc. Set against that, a reinforced system could add about 2.9pc to global output and lift exports by almost 18pc. That is roughly three trillion dollars. The report is the WTO’s flagship study and looks back over 80 years of trade rules. It is also a warning: reform the system or risk a slide into fragmentation. WTO Chief Economist Rob Staiger has told Reuters that the trading system is at a “critical juncture”. The report names four pressures: economic power is spread more widely than when the rules were written; governments intervene in their economies far more; digitalisation and global value chains have changed what trade actually is; and political friction between major powers keeps rising. Weaker WTO means riskier world for trade None of this is new to anyone who follows trade. What the report adds is a price tag, and some uncomfortable detail. About 72pc of global merchandise trade still moves on WTO most-favoured-nation (MFN) terms, meaning without special preferences for particular partners. Four years ago the figure was roughly 80pc. Staiger called the slide a “worrisome trend”, and it is the sort of change that does not announce itself. Nobody declares the end of a rules-based order. Trade simply migrates, one exemption and one bilateral deal at a time, until the exception becomes the norm. The WTO says trade policy uncertainty has reached unprecedented levels as governments reach for tariffs, subsidies, export controls and industrial policy. Regional and sector-specific deals are multiplying in response. Without a strong multilateral framework, such arrangements divert trade towards preferred partners rather than the most efficient producers, and encourage blocs to raise barriers against outsiders. The WTO rules restrain discrimination against non-members and shape how free trade agreements are built. Take that discipline away and the incentive to build walls grows. The Trump factor: Erosion of the trading order in recent years cannot be discussed without naming the biggest single force behind it. The protectionism led by the United States under two non-consecutive Trump administrations has been a decisive rupture with the multilateralism that shaped the economy in the last eight decades. What began in 2018 as retaliation against steel and aluminium imports into the US has hardened into a tariff doctrine, revived and widened in Trump’s second term from 2025. It is wrapped in the language of national security and economic sovereignty. However, the logic is strained. Tariffs on allies’ steel were not a response to a security threat, and blanket duties on nearly everything cannot be defended as one. The language of security has become a licence for unilateralism, and it undermines the assumption on which the WTO rests: integration and reciprocity would discipline the strong as well as the weak. The costs are not hypothetical. Studies by the IMF and the US Federal Reserve in the last few years have documented real income losses, disrupted supply chains and weaker investment from earlier rounds of tariffs. Costs for US consumers and manufacturers who depend on imported components have been substantial. The 2025 programme with its blanket tariffs and punitive duties on Chinese goods and electric vehicles, moves US policy from rules-based liberalism towards transactional nationalism, in which access to the American market is a favour to be bargained for. Even at home, the approach has run into legal limits. In May 2025, the US Court of International Trade ruled that the president had exceeded his authority under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act when imposing a universal 10pc tariff; the appeals court upheld that decision. The rulings are a reminder that policy built on emergency powers is fragile, and that trading partners are being asked to reorder their economies around measures that may not survive scrutiny. When the largest economy treats the rulebook it helped write as optional, others retaliate. Some hedge by building their own blocs and some conclude the rules were only ever meant for others. The report’s most striking finding is about distribution. Least-developed countries would gain the most from stronger multilateral cooperation, and lose the most from fragmentation. Pakistan has experienced this. Its exporters have had to absorb tariff shocks and renegotiate access. Rules do not guarantee fairness, but they make the terms of competition predictable, and predictability is what small economies cannot afford to lose. WTO director-general Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala struck a more hopeful note at the report’s launch. The system has been repaired and refreshed before, she said, and it can happen again. “Let’s not be fearful,” she said. The report does not argue that the system is working as designed. Its argument is that the alternative to imperfect rules is worse, and that the choice is not between the old order and a better one, but between repair and decline. The bottom line is plain. Trade rules are not an administrative distraction. The choice between renewing them and letting them lapse is worth trillions of dollars, especially for countries with the least say in whether the rules survive.
