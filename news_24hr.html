Countries open path towards 'killer robots' pact

Countries agreed on Saturday to open a path towards finding an international agreement on regulating lethal autonomous weapons systems, though campaigners seeking to stop so-called killer robots were disappointed by the “watered-down” outcome. After five days of gruelling talks at the United Nations, which ran into overtime, states agreed to open the door to building some form of pact over the next two years. However, with warfare undergoing a radical transformation, spurred by artificial intelligence, non-governmental organisations wanted far swifter, legally binding rules on handing power over human life and death to autonomous machines. Talks at the United Nations’ Review Conference of the Convention on Conventional Weapons (CCW) in Geneva stretched well into the early hours of Saturday. The 128 countries party to the CCW “agreed to launch the elaboration of an international instrument on lethal autonomous weapons systems, which should be conducted over the next two years”, conference president Julien Thoni told reporters afterwards. The process to get there had been tricky, with countries at odds and full consensus required for any decision. While a majority of states went in favour of a legally binding agreement, countries that have the technology, including the United States and Russia, are reluctant to have their hands tied. The process ahead should flesh out “which weapons can be forbidden according to international humanitarian law, and which is not forbidden but can be regulated”, Thoni told AFP. The Swiss diplomat said some countries had objected to the word negotiate, but had settled on elaborate instead. Campaign groups said the outcome fell well short of expectations. “States adopted a watered-down mandate that fails to chart a clear path towards a legally binding instrument,” said the Stop Killer Robots (SKR) coalition. “Autonomous weapons technology, including AI in the targeting cycle, is being deployed on the battlefield,” said Nicole van Rooijen, SKR’s executive director. “Delaying the creation of a legally binding instrument puts civilians and humanity at risk,” Verity Coyle, the deputy director for arms at Human Rights Watch, branded the outcome “disappointing”. “It does not provide the urgent action needed to agree new rules — but the technology is racing ahead,” she said.

Submarine cables are a critical part of international telecommunications infrastructure, carrying data between countries and continents. Before we design new provinces

THE interior minister’s call for a referendum on creating new provinces, followed by the information minister’s press conference, seemingly puts the cart before the horse. Consider a citizen told that a new province will bring government closer to their home. What does that mean to them? Will their land dispute be heard more expeditiously or decided more justly? Who will collect their taxes, and how will those revenues be spent? What does this vision of new provinces look and feel like? What will the new administrative arrangements cost, and who will pay? So far, the answers to these questions seem to vary depending on each province. Without defining what new provinces would actually mean for everyday citizens, a binary question posed nationally can’t provide meaningful insight into the change Pakistanis want. When developers market a new housing scheme, they show prospective buyers what they intend to build. Here, it seems, citizens may be asked to buy into something before knowing what they are buying. Citizens may be asked to buy into something before knowing what they are buying. The Constitution already prescribes how provincial boundaries may be altered. Under Articles 238 and 239, a constitutional amendment requires a two-thirds majority of the total membership of each House of Parliament. Article 239(4) further requires any bill altering a province’s limits to secure a two-thirds majority of that provincial assembly’s total membership before presidential assent. A national majority through referendum may favour a change whose costs fall chiefly on the people of one particular province. This safeguard gives the people most directly affected, through their elected representatives, a decisive voice. It is, in effect, a constitutional veto. Article 48(6) permits a referendum on a matter of national importance, following approval by a joint sitting of parliament, while Article 48(7) provides for legislation governing its procedure. Yet neither parliamentary approval for a referendum nor its result can substitute for the legislative majorities mandated by Article 239. The question remains: can a referendum, by itself, effect the extensive constitutional changes that creating new provinces would entail? The case for a new province should begin with the grievances arising from existing arrangements. If citizens travel hours to reach hospitals or government offices, publish the travel times and service gaps. If districts complain of unequal investment, disclose what was allocated and spent. If communities seek greater control over decisions affecting them, identify the powers they require. Article 140A already obliges provinces to devolve political, administrative and financial responsibility and authority to elected local governments. Proponents must therefore demonstrate what a new province could achieve that meaningful devolution and stronger district administration cannot. Every proposal should be accompanied by an administrative map and five- and 10-year financial projections. Which districts and divisions would be regrouped? Where would headquarters be located? How many officials would be required, and what would their offices cost? Who would collect provincial revenues, and within which jurisdictions? How would water resources and gas allocations be determined? Do we have answers ready for these vital questions? The judicial implications deserve equal attention. What high court facilities, district courts and subordinate courts would need to be established or reorganised? What would buildings, judges, staff and operations cost? Where would litigants go, and what would become of pending cases? Surely, access to justice must be among the measures by which any promise of bringing government closer to citizens is judged. Financial projections must establish what each administration could realistically raise, the federal transfers it would require, and the consequences for National and Provincial Finance Commission arrangements. Water entitlements, mineral and energy revenues, public assets, debts and pension liabilities cannot be left unresolved. Nor can the implications for employment, investment and public services, or the question of who bears the cost of transition, and keeps services running. An independent commission could examine these matters, publish its evidence and assumptions, and conduct hearings across affected districts. A referendum confined to a proposed province would exclude others whose finances and institutions may be altered. Conversely, a province-wide tally could obscure substantial differences between districts. Hearings and forums would allow communities on both sides of any proposed boundary, including minorities and those distant from seats of government, to share what they want and why. If the evidence supports creating a new province, a clearly defined proposal should then be placed before the legislatures whose consent the Constitution requires. Elected representatives must vote publicly and remain answerable to their constituents for the boundaries, costs and commitments they approve. Muslim populations

BY 2035, Muslims will overtake Christians to become the largest religious group in the world, according to the Pew Research Centre’s report The Future of World Religions: Population Growth Projections, 2020-2070. The report also projects that, at the current rate of population growth, by 2070, there will be 3.6 billion Muslims and slightly less than 3bn Christians globally. And Pakistan will have the largest Muslim population. Presently, the average fertility rate for Muslim women is 3.1 and 2.5 for Christians. At 24, Muslims also have a younger median age than Christians, whose median age is 31. On the face of it, many would consider overtaking Christianity to become the largest religious group cause for celebration. But what might temper the sentiment is that power and sway in world affairs largely elude the Muslim world. The Muslim population in both South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa is growing. Likewise, the fertility rate for Christians in sub-Saharan Africa is much greater than it is in Europe. Moreover, the Democratic Republic of Congo is expected to surpass the US as the country with the largest Christian population. At the same time, Pakistan will overtake Indonesia as the largest Muslim country. Both Pakistan and DRC are struggling nations, and the lives of their inhabitants will not be made any better as populations grow. In Pakistan, high population growth is causing what demographers call the ‘youth bulge’, with millions of young people without jobs. Official data in Pakistan shows that last year alone more than 762,000 — mostly young — people left the country to work abroad. This situation is likely to become complicated in the coming years. While the majority of migrants find work in Arab countries — that have their own labour restrictions — those going to Europe and the US must increasingly contend with social and cultural barriers. Islamophobia is on the rise in large parts of Europe and the US, where Muslims are treated with suspicion. In a sense, the Pew study raises more questions than it answers. The situation in richer or more educated Muslim countries is quite different. For instance, fertility rates in Iran, Turkiye, Indonesia, Tunisia, and the UAE have dropped to below replacement levels. But this will pose different problems — similar to those in the West, where ageing populations challenge the state through diminishing human labour and high healthcare and other costs for older people. So, while some countries like Pakistan must figure out what to do with their surplus of young people, others will have to figure out how to handle ageing generations. Each situation also carries political risks. In Turkiye, falling fertility rates threaten the ethnic balance — with parts of the country having higher fertility rates than others. This is likely to create problems for a country where the Turkish identity tends to subsume other groups numerically, politically, and culturally. Meanwhile, recent revolts led by the Gen Z cohort in South Asia reveal the peril that restless and frustrated young people pose to regional states. No matter what tactics repressive regimes in South Asia employ to quell the protests, the dissatisfaction felt by young people is real and cannot be ignored. Pakistan may not have seen the kind of momentous Gen Z movements that led to the collapse of governments in Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and Nepal. But its youth bul­ge continues to grow, and in the coming years, un­­less the state ta­­k­es steps to satisfy the demands of young people, the threat of revolutionary moveme­nts will only increase — a situation that radical Islamist groups can exploit by tapping into the religious sentiments of angry youth. Keeping that threat in mind, South Asia must work not only to create more employment opportunities for its young but also to promote the tolerance associated with Sufi traditions. A change in numbers does not always predict a change in global power dynamics. But in this case, the figures do provide justifiable grounds for pointing out the inequity that exists between Muslims and the adherents of other dominant faiths. Pakistan will soon be the country with the most Muslims. The pitfalls are obvious as resources shrink for a growing population. The silver lining is that, if the state ensures better opportunities for its youth, it will have a greater chance of producing future leaders in world affairs. Bilawal noted that the prime minister had not been “given the chance” to focus on the economic issues so far. “What can he do? Whenever he [PM Shehbaz] makes a plan, some federal minister objects and presents their own agenda to divert the topic at hand.” The PPP chairman insisted he wanted the premier to complete his term as he foresaw difficulties ahead for the country. “I am saying this because I know that there is no consideration for the people of Pakistan. Whether one province is created or a 1,000, the only worry the public has is having access to meals twice a day,” Bilawal said, recalling his party’s slogan of ‘roti, kapra, makan’ as primary concerns for the public.
