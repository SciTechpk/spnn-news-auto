Supreme Court terms lawyers' vigilance 'foundational' to protecting clients' cases from dismissal

ISLAMABAD: Emphasising the importance of diligent case presentation, the Supreme Court on Saturday reiterated that an advocate’s absolute vigilance was a “foundational requirement” for protecting a client’s case from dismissal in default. This principle is also “reverberated across the world with one voice by dint of a legal maxim ‘Vigilantibus et non dormientibus jura subveniunt’, thereby implying that the law assists the vigilant, not those who sleep over their rights”, observed Justice Muhammad Ali Mazhar in an eight-page judgment issued on Saturday, which restored two civil revisions that were dismissed for non-prosecution after the petitioner’s counsel could not appear due to Coronavirus infection. A three-member bench, headed by Justice Mazhar and including Justices Musarrat Hilali and Justice Shahid Bilal Hassan, allowed the civil petitions by converting them into appeals and setting aside the Feb 23, 2026 Lahore High Court’s (LHC) rejection of the restoration applications. Last September as well, the SC, while rejecting three civil miscellaneous applications seeking the restoration of review petitions, regretted that litigants and their counsel sometimes treat the court’s benevolence and leniency as a mere procedural formality, disregarding the solemnity of its categorical directives. The present issue arose when the petitioner, Safdar Ali Butt, filed a case before the LHC, but on Feb 2, 2026, both revisions were dismissed for non-prosecution because the petitioner’s counsel, Shahid Azeem Bajwa, could not reach the bench despite being present at the court as he was suffering from Coronavirus. Upon being called, the counsel rushed inside but could not approach the bench due to the crowd in the courtroom. Demonstrating diligence, the petitioner then filed restoration applications the very next day on Feb 3, but these were also dismissed by the LHC on Feb 23, prompting the present appeal to the SC. On Saturday, Justice Mazhar emphasised that, if an advocate lacks a sense of his professional duty, the ultimate sufferer was the client who engaged the advocate on reposing confidence in him. At the same time, Justice Mazhar highlighted that the client should also be vigilant and pursue his case diligently without leaving everything to the lawyer and should not opt to put out of sight or play hide-and-seek tactics based on an advocate’s inattention or negligence. “To ensure effective performance of professional ethics and further to avoid allegations of professional misconduct or violation of canons of practice, the lawyers ought to properly track fixation dates, cause lists and court benches to avoid missing cases due to human error. “They should also make all best possible efforts to handle every facet and dimension of the lis to protect his client’s interests,” the judgement emphasised. The lawyer should also keep his client updated on case progress, risks and even the settlement offers, if any and also avoid conflicts of interest, Justice Mazhar reminded. “In case of overlapping and simultaneous commitments in different courts of law, advocates ought to make alternate arrangements for holding brief even for adjournment requests or must file proper application for adjournment in advance with prior information to the other side’s counsel as a matter of professional ethics,” Justice Mazhar explained. “Sometimes the court dismisses the lawsuit when, despite various opportunities afforded by the court to proceed with the case or producing evidence, the party and his counsel persistently and calculatingly disappear, either due to lack of interest or to drag the proceedings to avoid well-timed disposal on merits,” regretted the judgment. Dismissal in default of a case on the negligence of an advocate without well-grounded sanity is one of the most serious blemishes which disparage and tarnish the professional image and reputation, the judgment highlighted. “Universally, a dismissal due to non-prosecution is considered a plainspoken ineptitude of the lawyers and also a breach of clients’ trust, which also sets the stage for filing complaints against the lawyers alleging professional misconduct to the Bar Councils under the Legal Practitioners and Bar Councils Act, 1973,” Justice Mazhar emphasised. At the same time, Justice Mazhar also observed that courts cannot turn a blind eye to the well-settled doctrine of restoration of a case, which gives carte blanche to courts to resuscitate the lawsuit dismissed in default if sufficient cause is put on record and the demands of justice so demand. “If the sufficient cause is pleaded through a promptly filed application for restoration within the limitation period and satisfactorily explains the absence on the date of hearing, the eventual consensus of the courts across the board is to lean toward compassion,” Justice Mazhar said. “The all-encompassing philosophy of justice articulates that lawsuits must be decided on merits rather than technical knockouts or due to procedural oversights or inadvertences.” Albeit the benefit of a lenient approach cannot be regarded as absolute, he added. Justice Mazhar emphasised that the “scale of justice neither tilts towards automatic dismissal nor automatic restoration, but it should be with a balanced frame of mind and also subject to the law of limitation to ward off and protect a genuine litigant from suffering due to counsel’s blunders, while delineating a strict dividing line against premeditated recklessness and genuine and honest grounds pleaded for restoration of the case to its original position”. He observed that force majeure circumstances or unexpected situations, including physical incapacitation or medical emergencies, are ordinarily acknowledged as convincing grounds to exercise inherent or discretionary powers by courts for restoring cases to “meet the ends of justice with or without imposing costs”. This was because the “indispensable purpose of a court is to determine the actual disputes as an elementary virtue of the administration of civil and criminal justice”. “The rules of procedure are meant to serve justice and not obstruct it,” the judgment concluded.

Making the most of America’s midterm tsunami

Imagine, for a moment, that the US midterm elections had not delivered Democratic wins at a generational scale, but merely a trickle of flipped seats—enough, perhaps, to change control of the House of Representatives but not enough for a Senate majority. Is there any doubt that President Donald Trump would embrace such results as a good-enough validation of his often lawless and brutal administration? Would it not now be doubling down, striving to cast the polls as an imprimatur for its excesses? Instead, American democracy skirted a disabling blow. Yet it would be a grave mistake to take a further step by concluding that voters’ overwhelming rejection of Trump and his Republican Party marks a full-throated public re-embrace of democracy. The election was a disaster averted, but it was only an opening to assess and fix the damage caused by two years of a rogue administration acting without congressional oversight. The Democrats’ landslide victory could set America’s now clearly imperfect democracy on the difficult path of repair and improvement. But this opportunity could slip away unless seized swiftly. Conventional wisdom about the midterm results is already resurrecting James Carville’s old adage: “It’s the economy, stupid.” There is, indeed, a kernel of truth to this. The Associated Press exit poll conducted on election day flagged economic discontent as voters’ top concern. Likewise, a Gallup poll from a week beforehand also found voters ranking inflation and health care as the most important issues. Against this backdrop, the bizarre optics of Trump’s midterm campaigning—Look at my reflecting pool! My beautiful ballroom! What about Jon Husted’s great butt!—underscore Trump’s surreal detachment from everyday voters’ woes. So potent is this story that most influential media outlets are offering inflation and affordability as catch-all explanations for the Democrats’ sweep through states Republicans had held for decades. And it is easy to see this simple story becoming a guiding light for anti-Trump figures, including among Republicans, for the next two years. This emerging consensus captures an important part of the story, but also overlooks an important finding. According to the AP exit poll, almost one-third of voters named the health of democracy as their top concern—just six points behind the economy—while the Gallup poll appears not to have asked respondents about the issue at all. Concerns over democracy and the rule of law also help explain the massive swings among Hispanic and Black voters. A UnidosUS survey of Hispanics found that immigration “overreach”—that is, flagrant disregard for the rule of law—was a leading concern. Anecdotal evidence suggests that Black turnout was driven by anger at the dismantling of the landmark 1965 Voting Rights Act—that is, by outrage at right-wing judges’ frontal assault on multiracial democracy. The affordability narrative risks overshadowing this strong public demand for democracy and even erasing it from the shared public memory of this election. That understanding of history creates a trap for Democrats, leaving the new House and Senate majorities a hostage to fortune, or worse. While control of the House and the Senate offers them a few policy tools with which to address inflation and affordability, those tools may not work quickly, and Trump retains broad control over trade policy and war-making. Indeed, because Trump can still make things worse, it is reasonable to think that there will be scant improvement in economic sentiment two years from now. No doubt, the Republican presidential nominee in 2028 will pin the blame on the Democrats. At a minimum, the picture will be muddy. Despite this risk, the affordability narrative is seductive to many Democrats, because it means they don’t need to think about the structural problems with American democracy. Consider here rampant electoral gerrymandering, the Senate’s malapportionment favoring rural populations, and the egregious inflation of judicial power. This time, the size of the Democratic wave overcame those barriers. But it would be reckless to count on that in the future, particularly as Trump retains powerful tools to erode democratic norms further. The Democrats, then, would be wise to lean into the democracy message by pressing for more structural change—not only because it is the principled thing to do, but also because it is in their political interest. This does not mean simply focusing on the Trump family’s venality. Highlighting corruption can be politically effective, as the midterm results showed. But unless the strong linkage between corruption and a weakening of democracy is made explicit—how the scramble for spoils overcomes representation of voters and their interests—scandalous revelations can also breed disabling cynicism and even voter apathy. The US midterm election results are an opportunity and an invitation. Voters’ demand for the rule of law and democracy has helped destroy Trump’s MAGA movement. That demand must not be forgotten or suppressed. The Democrats have opportunities, in Congress and also in newly-won state capitals, to show voters they understand that only democracy will be responsive on the economic front. Affordability may rank first, but addressing it depends on the quality of democratic institutions. Americans have given the Democrats the chance to show why that is true. They must not squander it. LAHORE: The Lahore High Court (LHC) has dismissed a public interest petition seeking directions to the federal and provincial governments to legislate on marital rape, holding that a constitutional court cannot compel the legislature to enact a particular law or create a new offence. Justice Abher Gul Khan clarified that the ruling neither endorses violence or abuse within marriage, nor prevents the Parliament or any provincial assembly from taking up the matter. Areej Sohail Bhutta, a lawyer, filed the petition, stating that she had encountered women clients who complained of non-consensual sexual conduct within their marriages. She argued that the law lacked a distinct statutory framework for sexual violence within marriage. She sought directions for legislative measures, including amendments to sections 375 and 376 of the Pakistan Penal Code. A court can’t force the legislature to enact a law or create an offence, observes the judge Justice Khan observed that the Constitution divides powers among the legislature, the executive and the judiciary, and that judicial review does not authorise courts to assume legislative functions. She said Article 199 empowers the high court to issue directions for the enforcement of fundamental rights. However, she maintained, there is a distinction between directing an authority to perform an existing constitutional or statutory obligation and compelling the legislature to enact a particular law or create a new offence. The judge noted that articles 9, 14 and 25 protect the security, dignity and equality of a person, while articles 25(3) and 34 allow special protections for women and promote their participation in national life. She said Article 35, which requires the state to protect marriage and the family, does not provide a licence for violence against a spouse. Justice Khan observed that foreign laws can provide comparative guidance, but cannot override Pakistan’s Constitution or replace the judgment of its legislature. She said any proposed legal framework also has to be examined in the light of the Constitution, existing laws and the injunctions of Islam. The judge noted that under Article 142(b), the Parliament and provincial assemblies are competent to legislate on criminal law, procedure and evidence. “The question whether an additional offence, a separate statutory provision or a further protective mechanism is required, is essentially a matter of legislative policy,” the judge observed. Citing Supreme Court judgments, including Tika Iqbal Muhammad Khan versus Pervez Musharraf (PLJ 2008 SC 500), the judge observed, “A court cannot issue a mandamus compelling the legislature to enact a particular law.” The judge noted that the petitioner had neither challenged a specific statutory provision as unconstitutional nor identified an authority’s failure to perform an existing legal duty. She said the relief sought would require the court to direct legislation according to a model proposed by the petitioner, amounting to interference in the legislative process. When home is hell

ON an ordinary March afternoon this year, the children of a household in Lahore’s Choong district gathered for their uncle’s small promise of treats. Azan had reportedly been tortured and eventually killed with an eight-inch-long knife by his own father, allegedly in a calculated bid to implicate a third party. Azan is just one of several children who are killed every month by people known to them. Moving back, in October, a 13-year-old in Mianwali was shot dead by her cousin for not wearing a hijab. In September, a three-year-old in Islamabad was kidnapped, raped, and murdered by her brother-in-law. In August, an 18-month-old in Karachi died after being raped by her 17-year-old cousin, while in July, a six-year-old boy was beaten up, raped, and strangled with a wire by his neighbour, and a four-year-old girl in Gujranwala was set on fire by her mother on suspicion of being ‘possessed’. In May, a father in Vehari repeatedly raped his three daughters aged 14, 11, and nine for six consecutive months; while three children aged six, four, and three were killed and found hanging from the fans in their home in Multan. A month earlier, in April, a mother in Lahore brutally slit the throats of her three children, luring them through a sadistic ‘game’ of hide-and-seek. Occurring within the safe confines of the home, these tragedies shocked and enraged us more than others, but our protest ended in quiet resignation, as it did in 2022 when an eight-year-old was tortured and poisoned by her grandparents for failing at household chores, and in 2015 when a 12-year-old was beaten to death by her father for not making properly round rotis. Homes repeatedly turn into hell for children as our criminal justice system is unequipped to intercept and prevent attacks within the home. Statutes such as the ICT and Sindh Child Protection Acts create institutional bodies that address minors’ welfare, but they lack the authority to adopt anticipatory, prompt, and intrusive measures to ensure child safety. Our laws are reactive rather than anticipatory. Even the landmark Zainab Alert, Response, and Recovery Act, 2020, creates a protection mechanism for missing children that is enforceable only after the abduction is reported. As a result, attacks on children in public are not easily prevented. Our criminal justice system is unequipped to intercept and prevent attacks within the home. Recently, a five-year-old in Haripur was raped and left to die after being lured out of her home to see her father; an eight-year-old in Lahore was sexually harassed in a grocery store by the owner, and an eight-year-old in Sargodha was raped and murdered in a grocery store. Even when these victims were seen being accompanied or coaxed by a group of men or being eyed by strangers, the palpable threat of attack was ignored by those present at the scene. It was also in the presence of bystanders in Multan that 15-year-old Wasim was tortured and then run over by his employer recently. Whether it was their indifference or ignorance, they abandoned the weakest, most vulnerable members of society — our children. Being voiceless, defenceless and weak, children are considered dispensable. It is, therefore, easy to ignore injustices against them. We tolerate inadequate child protection laws and mechanisms, ignore the proliferation of pornographic content, disregard paedophilic customs such as trading minor boys, and permit appalling lectures advocating marriage with girls as young as four. Our complicity and apathy have normalised child abuse and allowed it to persist, putting Pakistan third on the global map for child abuse. Around 2,000 cases of child abuse were reported in just the first six months of the current year. Sahil reported over 3,600 in 2025. In 2024, 21 children were being abused daily. Reducing such horrifying figures demands enforceable protection under the law. Justice Jawad Hasan in 2023 (Zafar Khizer vs the Federation of Pakistan)initiated a shift in discourse from viewing children as ordinary citizens to viewing them as vulnerable citizens categorised as ‘child citizens’ and entitled to a higher degree of protection and concern under the law. Justice Usman G. Rashid last month said that the child citizen is entitled to fundamental rights in their independent capacity that cannot be displaced by any parental authority. While the concept of the child citizen as an independent rights holder is evolving through jurisprudence, it is insufficient without effective implementation and accountability. Mandatory reporting regimes should be introduced for designated professionals such as healthcare providers, instructors, and adults in child-related roles or positions of trust, as under Australian law and England’s Crime and Policing Act, 2026. Additional measures for prevention through reporting suspected child abuse and conducting child-sensitive investigations and follow-ups in such cases must be made compulsory in line with the spirit of Article 19 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child. These obligations must be rooted not merely in law, but in a shared sense of humanity and a collective duty to protect the vulnerable because, as lawyer and human rights activist Asma Jahangir said, a society that fails to protect its children has, in fact, failed humanity’s most basic test.
