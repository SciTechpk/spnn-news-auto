Bumpy road to export-led growth

In an unusual public expression of dissatisfaction, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif has voiced displeasure with exporters’ performance despite what he described as ‘several incentives’ extended to them in the last federal budget. His remarks raise questions not only about the effectiveness of government support but also about the commitments, if any, made by beneficiary industries to increase exports in return. The problem reflects a persistent failure to distinguish between export promotion, export-led growth and export-led industrial transformation. The first seeks to increase foreign sales of existing products, the second makes exports a driver of economic expansion, while the third requires sustained investment in technology, productivity and higher value manufacturing. Export-led growth is a favourite phrase for politicians to use in their policy statements without consistently translating it into industrial development. Official data and reports showed Pakistan’s policies have largely focused on export promotion through subsidies and concessions to established industries. The Commerce Ministry’s Rs25 billion subsidy package for rice exports and similar demands from the textile sector illustrate this approach. The history is full of rebates, concessions and cash subsidies to suppress prices of commodities. The underlying question is whether economic policymakers and influential industrial groups have prioritised short-term export incentives over investment in competitive industries. Despite merchandise exports averaging around $2.5bn a month, Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal announced targets of $60bn in May 2025 and $63bn in August 2026, both to be achieved within five years. Since Independence, industrial development has relied heavily on import substitution, encouraging production for a protected domestic market Official statements have also envisaged $100bn in exports by 2035. However, annual merchandise exports have remained between $30bn and $32bn in recent years, exposing the gap between official ambitions and productive capacity. The World Bank’s October 2026 Pakistan Development Update, titled Attaining Escape Velocity, calls for continued tariff liberalisation, improved export standards and greater access to export financing to strengthen competitiveness. The bank’s historical data shows that exports of goods and services declined from a peak of 17.3pc of GDP in 1992 to around 10pc in 2025, indicating that exports have failed to keep pace with economic expansion. Former commerce minister Humayun Akhtar Khan attributed this declining share to a shrinking industrial base and stagnant productivity caused by inadequate technological upgrading. He said Pakistan’s competitors were producing cheaper, more competitive goods by adopting modern technology, while domestic industries continued to lag behind. “The exchange rate no longer has any meaningful link with export growth,” Mr Khan said, calling for a focused industrial policy to reverse de-industrialisation and expand production capacity. The IMF’s 2024 Article IV consultation report provides further evidence of limited industrial diversification. It noted that Pakistan ranked 85th on the Economic Complexity Index in 2022, unchanged from its position in 2000, with exports heavily concentrated in textiles and agricultural products. The ranking suggests limited progress in developing more sophisticated export products. The experience of East Asian economies offers a contrast. According to the World Bank’s 1993 study, The East Asian Miracle, countries such as South Korea and Taiwan combined export expansion with industrial investment, technological upgrading and productivity growth. Their experience illustrates how export promotion can contribute to industrial transformation rather than merely increasing foreign sales of existing products. Pakistan followed a different course. Since independence, industrial development has relied heavily on import substitution, encouraging production for a protected domestic market. Subsequent export promotion measures emphasised subsidies without consistently improving industrial productivity, while investment increasingly favoured services, particularly real estate. The Planning Commission’s Annual Plan 2024–25 highlighted this imbalance, reporting that manufacturing accounted for only 10pc of private investment, compared with 21pc for real estate activities and 30pc for agriculture. It also observed that manufacturing’s investment share was declining. The trend continued in FY25. According to the State Bank of Pakistan’s Annual Report 2025, private investment in manufacturing declined for the fourth consecutive year, while overall private investment increased modestly, mainly in services. The bank also reported that increased fixed investment financing largely supported efficiency improvements and infrastructure upgrades, with relatively few industries borrowing to expand production capacity. Compounding these weaknesses is the absence of a coherent national industrial policy. The Ministry of Industries and Production has largely pursued sector-specific policies for automobiles, mobile phones and other industries, encouraging domestic production under substantial tariff protection. Former ambassador to the World Trade Organisation Dr Manzoor Ahmed identified excessive tariff protection as a major constraint on export competitiveness. However, he considered the government’s new five-year tariff policy a step towards encouraging industrialisation and exports. Citing the automobile industry, Dr Ahmed said Suzuki had closed its manufacturing plants in Thailand as the country moved towards electric vehicles, while Pakistan’s three major automobile manufacturers continued to enjoy excessive protection. He urged Pakistan to learn from Indonesia and Thailand, which had moved away from such protection, and adopted technological upgrading to remain competitive in international markets. Dr Ahmed stressed that technological upgrading was essential for export competitiveness. Pakistan, he said, needed to follow changes in global manufacturing and technology rather than remain dependent on protected domestic industries. Pakistan’s export difficulties, therefore, extend beyond high energy costs, exchange rate instability, taxation and market access. Unlike East Asian economies, which linked industrial support to productivity and export performance, Pakistan has continued to protect established industries without consistently requiring technological advancement or international competitiveness. Unless industrial policy and investment incentives are linked to measurable improvements in productivity, domestic value addition, technological upgrading and export performance, ambitious targets may continue to outpace the economy’s capacity to deliver. A shifting cycle

PAKISTAN’S 2024 election results marked the end of a political cycle that began in the early 2000s. Less often noted is that this cycle expired at roughly the same moment as the global order which framed it. Signs of political and cultural realignment are now fully emergent, and their trajectory passes through events of the past 25 years — the ‘war on terror’ and its effects on Muslim societies, the financial crisis alongside growth in Asian economies, and increased migration to Western countries. The Pakistani domestic cycle is best characterised as an unsuccessful attempt to achieve a political settlement, under the garb of federalised (and electoral) constitutional rule. Its participants were the traditional parties PML-N and PPP, and their core constituents, sections of liberal civil society and private news media, and some segments of the judiciary. Its opposition came from entities excluded from or antagonised by that brief lived system of patronage: the PTI, the upwardly mobile urban classes, and the military establishment and its aligned factions among the media. The latter coalition won out over the last 10 years. In winning, it stumbled upon its own internal contradictions, which are now playing out as the present crisis. This cycle, however, was always nested inside something larger. Its vocabulary of rights, its civil society, and much of its international support came from a global dispensation; NGO work here was intellectually and financially sustained by Western aid largesse in the context of the war on terror. Once the external scaffolding weakened, the undoing at home proved swift. That scaffolding is what we commonly dub the neoliberal world order. Characterised by deregulation in markets, and trade and financial liberalisation, these prescriptions were macroeconomic orthodoxy for nearly four decades. They represented the response of the capitalist class to the stagflation crisis of the 1970s in Western economies, dismantling the political power of labour by making offshoring easier, and restoring the power of capital through financialisation. The recipe would remain incomplete without access to new labour, product, and capital markets, which is why it came packaged as IMF-run ‘structural adjustment’ for countries in the Global South. The Pakistani domestic cycle is best characterised as an unsuccessful attempt to achieve a political settlement. What did these four decades of liberalisation deliver? Growth for some countries (East Asia, parts of Latin America), stagnation and deindustrialisation for most others, astronomical returns for owners of finance capital, and heightened inequality nearly everywhere. Since 2008, populations in the West have rightly soured on this misfiring orthodoxy, and that resentment has gradually made its way into how decision-makers think about their economies. Free markets are no longer considered orthodoxy (at least in rhetoric). The unravelling of the old order extends beyond economics. Liberal democracy was presented as a core value, supposedly maintained by the US globally. Its use as a cynical foreign policy stick had already made many in the world wary. Elsewhere, the appeal of some authoritarian systems is growing. Sociologist Cihan Tugal points to Turkiye under Erdogan and the AKP, which has used state welfare, economic intervention, and centralised politics to contain opposition over two decades. Key political and establishment figures in countries like Pakistan now routinely view limits on democracy as necessary for economic progress. Does this reordering then offer Pakistan an opening? Statist nationalists see the country’s expanded role in regional geopolitical affairs as both a vindication of its military-led governing structure and a route to its destined status as a regional power. On the face of it, both shifts work in the country’s favour. As a state with strong military capabilities, both nuclear and conventional, Pakistan can extract material concessions from regional powers looking for a security patron after the US winds down its position. And for a country long locked into IMF programmes, escaping some elements of neoliberal economic orthodoxy is a necessity. The real question is: can it? Here the answer goes back to how the global is refracted into the local via the domestic arrangements that actually exist on the ground. Can the rentier/ patronage economy be turned productive? Can those with outsized claims on the exchequer be reined in? The task rests with a state overwhelmed by a military-bureaucratic establishment that has turned its primary logic into one of extraction via authoritarian decree rather than service delivery. More importantly, this is a state of viceregal origin, incapable of the mass mobilisation and disciplining that engendered effective capacity in Korea or China. It is held together instead by the distribution of rents, internally to its cadres and kin, and externally to a narrow sliver of the population. The traditional parties that might have steered this moment have proven completely unable to do so. Their mode of politics rested on narrow patronage relations with socially dominant segments in Punjab and Sindh, and in exchange for temporary respite they outsourced Fata, Balochistan, judicial independence, electoral competition, and even large parts of governance to the militarised logic of establishment control. Most of all, they have ignored the emergent reality of the upwardly mobile, digitally resourceful urban middle classes and inflation-weary masses, leaving the field wide open for multiple types of resentment. However, resentment on its own is insufficient to lead society anywhere. The arduous task for those still committed to broad-based material uplift lies in reworking the state into something attentive to the collective good and capable of mobilising society for it. This is not a reworking that will be handed to progressives with any ease. In fact, in the absence of viable progressive movements to project some notion of universal justice, the militarisation of the region and the turn to narrow, sectional resentment is a recipe for further marginalisation of the already vulnerable. Flight from reality

PAKISTAN’S ruling elites devote inordinate time and attention to foreign engagements — at the expense of tackling domestic challenges. The country’s media also tends to focus more on foreign policy and international events than on fundamental problems confronting the country. Switch on any TV channel and most talk shows are endlessly discussing Pakistan’s overseas forays, defence agreements, and other global developments. Some of this is understandable as a war in the neighbourhood and regional and international dynamics do have implications for Pakistan that merit comment. But domestic issues more consequential to the country’s fate and future rarely receive the same attention. There may be several reasons for this. For ruling elites, foreign engagements are a way to access and collect geopolitical rents to keep the economy afloat, enabling them to avoid undertaking domestic reform. Their notion of ‘success’ rests on foreign alignments that can deliver these rents without having to expend political energy solving structural economic problems at home. Hence, the almost obsessive focus on foreign pursuits and lack of reform at home. For governments resting on dubious electoral mandates, it is also the means to acquire international legitimacy to compensate for its absence at home. This is more so for authoritarian and hybrid regimes. There is also more cachet in taking overseas trips and having photo-ops with world leaders than trying to get ministries to do their job. That is why ministers often spend more time abroad than in the country, with prime ministers exceeding their predecessors’ record of foreign visits. Fixing the country’s problems is much harder than flying off to, say, Davos. A cynical explanation is that the country’s problems — poverty, hunger, lack of education, and health facilities, malnutrition, growing population numbers — don’t affect the power elite, so if they fester there is no skin off its back. Certainly, it sees no urgency in addressing them. Pakistan’s future will be determined at home, not abroad. The excessive attention that much of Pakistan’s broadcast media devotes to foreign policy and external developments is explained by other factors. The obvious one is the electronic media simply covers what the country’s leadership is doing. But it also means it is content to play follow-my-leader and not set its own agenda that takes public issues into account. Discussing foreign trips and events involves far less effort than delving into complex domestic issues. Confrontations between the government and opposition do get plenty of coverage. But when a report on an aspect of Pakistan’s economic and social situation is published, the coverage and discussion are fleeting and shallow, with no analysis or follow-up. There are occasional exceptions but an in-depth analysis or investigative documentary on a consequential socioeconomic issue is hard to come by. Above all, TV channels seem to believe serving up formulaic talk shows is enough for viewers. Yet issues that warrant the attention of primarily the government, that can do something about them, and media, which can encourage authorities to pay attention, have become even more urgent. Improving the living conditions of people is the government’s most important task. That it has not lived up to this task is apparent from the fact that poverty, for example, has risen significantly in recent years. According to the World Bank, Pakistan’s national poverty rate rose from 22 per cent in 2018-19 to 29pc in 2024-25. The WB also estimates that Pakistan accounts for almost half of the poor in the 23 countries of the MENAAP region (Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan, and Pakistan). UNDP estimates 38.3pc of Pakistan’s population face multidimensional poverty, which measures multiple, overlapping deprivations in education, health, and standard of living that go beyond a purely monetary standard of poverty. So why is the government unable to deal with the increasing number of people living below the poverty line? It is argued that the government is constrained by the macroeconomic situation and an IMF programme that is contractionary, and has to focus on stabilising the economy. But that should not prevent it from addressing poverty. There is much that can be done to reduce poverty that has nothing to do with the spending cycle. After all, the IMF hardly prevents the government from allocating resources judiciously, mobilising revenue, pursuing non-tax filers, and curbing electricity theft. Stabilising the economy has nothing to do with vanity projects undertaken at the expense of helping the poor. Poverty is also reflected in the alarming state of hunger in the country. Pakistan is among the world’s 10 countries where millions face acute hunger, according to an international humanitarian organisation, Action Against Hunger. Another recent report, No Security Without Food Security, finds Pakistan is worst hit by hunger in South Asia. Deteriorating food insecurity is attributed to economic instability, inflation, and climate shocks. Malnutrition associated with poverty is also responsible for child stunting. Around 40pc of Pakistani children under five are stunted, which condemns them to a life of physical disability, poverty, and lifelong deprivation. No issue matters more to the country’s future than education. Yet it barely gets priority attention from the government. Pakistan still has the world’s second highest number of out-of-school children, around 25m (aged five to 16) — over half of them girls. This is a national scandal. Literacy remains stagnant, with 37pc of people illiterate. No country can make economic progress with that level of illiteracy. Without investment in people, economic growth will remain elusive. The education deficit is all the more serious given the youth bulge. Over two-thirds of the population are below age 30. The young will determine the country’s destiny. But if they are uneducated and unemployable, they face a hopeless future. This jeopardises the country’s prospects. Also disturbing is the share of youth (15-29 years) who are not in education, employment, and training, known as the NEET rate, which is 37pc overall and 62pc for women, according to a Gallup report. Human capital and human development, crucial for economic growth, have suffered from government indifference and deteriorated over time. Foreign engagements count for little when there is no improvement in the condition of the majority of people. Nor does the ‘glamour’ of foreign trips obscure the grim reality of the economic and social deprivations people face across the country. Nation-building at home should have priority. Hitler’s ‘retreat’ sparks debate over Germany’s past and present

PERCHED on a ridge high in the German Alps lie the burned-out ruins of a mountain house frequented by Adolf Hitler, which is now at the heart of an intense debate about Germany’s Nazi past. Called The Eagle’s Nest, or Kehlsteinhaus in German, the retreat was built by the Nazis in 1938. After World War II it was classified as a historical monument. But after it was gutted in a suspected arson attack last month, some believe it is time for the site to be completely destroyed, so it can shed the mantle of its Nazi past, BBC News reported. Others, however, want it either partially or completely rebuilt, wary of the risk of turning it into a lightning rod for neo-Nazi elements at a time when Germany is witnessing the resurgence of the far-right. Before it was burned down on Oct 23, the Eagle’s Nest had been a popular tourist attraction, owned by the state of Bavaria and operating for many years as a restaurant. Visitors would take a bus and then ride the original brass and mirror-panelled lift through the mountain to the Kehlsteinhaus to savour the magnificent views above the town of Berchtesgaden. Unlike Hitler’s nearby summer residence, the Berghof, which was bombed in World War II and demolished in 1952, the Eagle’s Nest survived the war. In the 1990s, amid concerns that the site did not do enough to confront the crimes of the Nazi past, a museum, the Obersalzberg Documentation Centre, was opened close by. Although the Eagle’s Nest itself was “largely destroyed” by the fire, according to police, the documentation centre survived unscathed. The authorities have not commented on a possible motive, but a 33-year-old man with a history of mental illness has been detained on suspicion of arson. According to BBC News, some feel that this is an opportune moment to allow the site to fade into history. “It’s been torched now. It’s been destroyed. I think they should just clear away the rubble and leave the mountain as it should be in its natural state,” said Prof Sir Richard J. Evans, a specialist in Nazi Germany at the University of Cambridge. That has happened with “quite a few other Nazi sites”, he argued, including the bunker in Berlin where Hitler killed himself. But Bavaria’s authorities take a different view about the site’s future. Both the state’s interior minister, Joachim Herrmann, and its minister for the arts, Markus Blume, have spoken out in favour of rebuilding the Kehlsteinhaus. Germany and Austria have long struggled to deal with the fraught legacy of buildings and sites connected to the Nazi era. With the recent rise of the far-right AfD and the Austrian Freedom Party, the debate over Erinnerungskultur (Culture of Remembrance) has taken on a new urgency. A row broke out recently in Germany when AfD and conservative CDU councillors in a town in Saxony decided to ban “stumbling stones” memorials – embedded small brass plaques in the streets - to victims of the Nazis. In Austria over the summer, a police station was opened in the house where Hitler was born in Braunau am Inn. It followed years of controversy about how the house should be used. The Austrian government said the police presence would help prevent neo-Nazi ‘pilgrims’. But others want the site to become a centre of responsibility, actively confronting the Nazi past. Antalya agenda

Geopolitical tensions, an energy crisis, and strained finances in rich countries have dampened hopes of a breakthrough. The absence of an official US delegation after Washington’s withdrawal from the Paris Agreement has worsened matters. Meanwhile, the climate crisis is advancing faster than efforts to contain it. A new UN report warns that even if countries fulfil existing climate pledges, global temperatures could rise by 2.6°C this century. For Pakistan, already exposed to floods, extreme heat, and erratic rainfall, the question is what it can realistically secure from the talks. At COP29, developed countries committed to mobilising $300bn annually for developing nations by 2035. COP30 called for progress towards a broader goal of $1.3tr a year and for adaptation finance to be tripled by 2035. But promises of more money mean little if vulnerable states cannot access it. For Pakistan, the priority should be to secure easier access to climate funds, more grants rather than debt-creating loans, and clear deadlines for financial contributions. It should seek support for practical measures to protect homes from flooding, secure water supplies, and improve early warning systems. By joining forces with other vulnerable countries, Pakistan could strengthen calls for better glacier monitoring, flood protection, and affordable climate technology. Its latest climate plan aims to cut projected emissions by 50pc by 2035, with 17 percentage points unconditional and the remaining 33 reliant on international support. Pakistan must also address problems that international aid alone cannot solve, including construction on floodplains and poor enforcement of environmental rules. Heatwaves in Punjab threaten agriculture, food security: UN

• Report says fruit yields plunge 50pc; heat stress spreads livestock disease, cuts milk output • Shrinking incomes, food spoilage limit rural families’ healthy diets • Women bear brunt of crisis due to heavy outdoor labour; preterm birth risk jumps 26pc in severe weather ISLAMABAD: Increasingly frequent and severe heatwaves in Punjab’s rain-fed rural areas are disrupting agricultural production, reducing household incomes and worsening food insecurity, particularly among women and vulnerable communities, according to a report by United Nations (UN) agencies. The assessment “Extreme Heat and Agriculture”, was published jointly by the UN Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) and the World Meteorological Organization (WMO). It warns that rising temperatures have emerged as one of the most serious and acute hazards facing global food production, threatening the livelihoods of billions of people while inflicting immediate economic and nutritional damage on vulnerable farming communities. “The effects of extreme heat on production are stark, as heat wav­es have led to significant declines in crop yields,” the report said. In the high-rainfall zones of Punjab, fruit productivity has fallen by as much as 50 per cent, the agencies found. Livestock operations across the region are also severely affected. Large ruminants produce less milk under heat stress, and small ruminants face increased outbreaks of foot-and-mouth disease, the report said. For rural families dependent on farming, the loss of agricultural output directly translates into reduced household income and diminishes their ability to purchase basic food staples. The crisis is compounded by the accelerated spoilage of vegetables, dairy products and other perishable goods in extreme temperatures. That widespread spoilage further reduces local food availability and drives up market prices. As a result, rural families face a double burden. They produce less diverse and nutritious food for their own consumption while simultaneously losing the agricultural income they rely on to purchase food from markets. “This double burden severely limits their access to healthy diets,” the report said. Although entire communities are affected, women generally bear a disproportionate share of the burden, the UN agencies found. Women in Punjab play an essential role in agricultural activities, including harvesting, collecting water and carrying out physically demanding household responsibilities. This exposes them to high temperatures for extended periods, increasing their risk of heat-related illnesses and other health complications. Those health risks are particularly acute for pregnant and lactating women, who have greater nutritional and dietary needs that are often the first to be compromised during periods of household food scarcity. Research cited in the report indicates that each 1°C rise in temperature is associated with a 4pc increase in the likelihood of preterm birth. During heat waves, the risk of preterm birth jumps to 26pc, according to the report. Additional social inequalities exacerbate women’s vulnerabilities to extreme heat. Despite their critical role in farming, women often lack ownership of land and other assets, necessary to access credit and financial services that could aid adaptation. With limited access to climate information and early warning systems, they are often less prepared to respond to heat events. Rural women’s coping strategies, such as reducing their own food intake to prioritise other family members, which can further compromise their health and resilience, reflect broader patterns of gender discrimination that amplify their vulnerability in times of climate crisis, the report said. Beyond the localised crisis in Punjab, the FAO and the WMO warned that extreme heat is acting as a powerful risk multiplier with direct and indirect impacts across all agricultural subsectors worldwide. Rising temperatures amplify existing environmental hazards such as drought, heighten the risk of destructive wildfires and create complex compound impacts. Those converging threats endanger not only global agricultural production, but also the physical health of agricultural workers who labour on the front lines of the growing climate threat. The fingerprints of extreme heat on agriculture are already visible worldwide, and the analysis of scientific evidence confirms that heat is driving significant productivity losses. Under high-emission scenarios, nearly half the world’s cattle could be exposed to dangerous heat by 2100, with annual losses nearing $40bn, although under a low-emission scenario, impacts from livestock exposure to extreme heat are reduced by nearly two-thirds. In aquatic systems, marine heatwaves have already caused repeated mass mortality events and are forcing entire fish stocks to migrate in search of cooler water. Fruit and nut trees and natural forests are also subject to production losses and the growing risk of more frequent and intense wildfires. Together, those global agricultural losses create a dangerous environmental feedback loop, the report concluded. When heat-driven shortfalls in crop and livestock production prompt farmers to clear additional land for agricultural expansion to compensate for lost yields, the resulting land-use changes increase greenhouse gas emissions — fuelling further climate change.
