14 infants killed in fire at Pims' gynae ward in Islamabad

ISLAMABAD: Fourteen infants were killed in a fire that broke out in the gynaecology ward of the Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences (Pims) early on Wednesday. What has happened so far: 14 babies die after fire erupts in ward; initial reports say spark erupted from AC, blaze spread due to oxygen points Bereaved families decry rescue teams arrived “too late”; witnesses report locked doors Health secretary removed; inquiry panel formed on PM’s orders; separate fact-finding committee constituted Health Minister Mustafa Kamal vows accountability; Tallal Chaudhry defends district administration Condolences from across the nation, other countries pour in Footage showed rescue workers using ladders to reach broken windows to access the affected area and grief-stricken women sitting on the ground outside the hospital. An AFP reporter saw a woman in tears and a man clear glass from two broken windows, as security guards stood at the entrance to the medical facility. Several witnesses, their hands and faces blackened from the smoke, told AFP they waited hours for help from the emergency services. They were forced to smash through windows to try and get to the neonatal intensive care unit. Others said they came across locked doors and alleged guards prevented those inside the building from leaving as the fire raged. However, the hospital disputed this in a statement issued later in the evening. “All emergency exits were fully open and operational,” it asserted. In a statement, the Islamabad administration said that the fire broke out on the third floor of the Mother and Child Hospital’s ward. It said that rescue teams rushed to the scene, with six fire brigade vehicles and four ambulances taking part in the rescue operation. “The fire was immediately brought under control and the children were immediately shifted to the intensive care unit,” it said, adding that 14 children “were completely burnt”. Pims Spokesperson Dr Aneeza Jalil told Dawn that 15 babies were in the ward. “At least one baby was saved by a doctor,” she said. Health Minister Mustafa Kamal, who reached Islamabad from Karachi in the afternoon, vowed that “no one responsible for negligence, including me, will be spared”. “The tragic fire incident at Pims MCH Centre, resulting in the loss of precious neonatal lives, is deeply heartbreaking,” the public hospital said. Citing a preliminary report, the Pims statement said the incident was “initially caused by a spark inside nursery at 06:38am and within seconds a massive fire erupted due to oxygen points at each incubator”. It said two doctors and two nurses on duty inside the nursery responded “immediately and called security staff, who joined in and tried to get in twice [to bring] babies out until 06:39am, when a huge blast occurred and ceilings fell down”. The statement said the duty staff managed to rescue a baby in the nursery and four in an adjacent room, along with three mothers. “Besides immediate response, staff managed rescue of 73 other admitted patients in the adjacent Gynae ward on the same floor within minutes,” it added. The hospital asserted that “available fire safety equipment installed, including 35 fire extinguishers, were all utilised by the staff to contain the situation”. Health minister vows accountability Speaking to reporters in Islamabad, the health minister said he will present himself to his superiors for accountability. Providing details of the incident, Kamal said: “At 6:38am, it can be seen in the CCTV footage that there was a spark in the AC, then a fireball could be seen erupting out of it and landing on the floor of the ward. In one minute, at 6:39am, four people, including two nurses and two doctors, could be seen rushing for help and around the same time, the whole room was engulfed in flames”. The health minister said the incident was being investigated by “experts, different institutions including us”, adding that “nothing will remain hidden and all facts will be unearthed”. He continued: “Apparently, it seems that 14 out of the 15 infants were connected to oxygen tubes and it was that oxygen which caught flame.” Kamal reiterated that the “heart-wrenching” incident will be investigated and warned that “if there was any negligence, then as I said, we will not spare them, including myself”. The minister further said that Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif had instructed him “not to speak about certain issues”. He said he would be meeting the premier and that the two had been in touch. “Everyone is on board, and the findings will be presented,” he said. On being asked if he was going to tender his resignation, the health minister said, “As I have said before, whoever is responsible [..] they will not be spared. I have said what I can, and I wish I could say more, but the prime minister is my boss, and whatever his orders will be, I can only say that.” The federal minister was also questioned about fire safety standards at Pims, with a reporter pointing out that the hospital had been issued notices for the past eight years. Kamal claimed that the hospital had ensured compliance each time a notice had been issued in the past, pointing out that the blaze was contained with “26 fire extinguishers available in the building”. When a reporter asked about his early morning statement over the lack of adequate facilities at Pims and “why in his 2-2.5 years as a health minister, no improvement had been made”, the minister, getting visibly frustrated, said, “If it has been 2-2.5 years, then feel free to shoot me” and clarified that he had only been at his position for one year and two months. “What I said in the morning has to be seen within a certain parameter. Noting that such an incident should not have happened, he said: “Will get to its root cause and ensure prevention in the future.” The minister was also asked about reports that the door of the ward was locked, to which he said that access to the ward was limited and guarded due to “instances of people stealing newborn babies a couple of years back”. “You would not see that anywhere else in the world, but we have the issue, so the door had to be manned with a guard,” Kamal explained. Massive Nepal-Tibet flood kills 160, leaves hundreds missing

Nepali police said in an operational update on Wednesday they had recovered 157 bodies after a terrifying flood tore through the Tibet border region, taking the overall death toll to at least 160, with several hundred missing. Hundreds of tourists were still out of contact hours after the disaster hit. A torrent of mud and debris burst from the banks of a river between China and Nepal, burying buildings and triggering a frantic search for survivors. Chinese authorities said three people died and 265 others were missing after the mudslide, state media reported, citing preliminary figures from local officials. It added that President Xi Jinping ordered an “all-out” rescue effort. It was not immediately clear if there was any overlap between the Chinese figures and those announced by the Nepalese, with state broadcaster CCTV saying specific casualty figures were still being verified. “I got a call from [my brother-in-law] this morning, and they were crying and said their entire house had been swept away. US designates UK-based Palestine Action as foreign 'terrorist group'

The United States on Wednesday named the UK-based Palestine Action group, which has been banned by the British government, as a “specially designated global terrorist” organisation. The US also issued sanctions against the group, claiming the move was for supporting “an act of terrorism”. Set up in 2020, Palestine Action is appealing against the UK government ban, which has led to thousands of arrests. Rights groups have decried the ban, saying it was aimed at criminalising dissent. Palestine Action had increasingly targeted Israel-linked defence companies in Britain, especially Israel’s largest defence firm Elbit Systems. Responding to the US move on Wednesday, Huda Ammori, co-founder of Palestine Action, said the group’s activities “have always been about saving lives by disrupting the Israeli war machine”. “The fact that Trump is now taking inspiration from Britain’s repression of the movement for Palestinian freedom exposes just how dangerous this ban is and should be a wake-up call to anyone who cares about free speech and civil liberties,” Ammori said in a statement to Reuters. Palestine Action’s stated goal on its website is to end “global participation in Israel’s genocidal and apartheid regime”. It gained visibility after Israel launched a devastating military onslaught on Gaza following the Oct 7, 2023 attack by fighters from Palestinian group Hamas in southern Israel. Since coming to power for his second term, US President Donald Trump has issued orders for his government to designate what it terms “far-left” groups as terrorist organisations. Pakistan's amateur radios are tuned in for the apocalypse

When the earthquake struck in 2005, two men in Islamabad loaded their bags and headed for Azad Kashmir. They made it as far as Kohala bridge where the road got cut off. From there on, they made it by foot and hitchhiked on motorcycles and the back of pick-up trucks to Muzaffarabad. One of the few offices still functioning there was Rescue 15 whose building had half collapsed. The men asked the surprised police officials inside if they had a car battery. The officials led them to a crushed Suzuki Mehran. The men got to work. A radio transceiver was plugged into the battery and they started transmitting messages to Islamabad since telephone landlines had collapsed. In the next few days they sent nearly 3,000 messages to Islamabad by radio, which their affiliates spread throughout Pakistan by phone. Most messages were confirmations of survivals and deaths. These men belonged to the voluntary Pakistan Amateur Radio Society (PARS) which has been around since 1970. It is made up of licensed professionals who operate amateur or ham radio, which keep people connected when all else fails. Decades later, the society is still going strong, and since its fledgling foray into Kashmir has increasingly taken up the cause of rescue and relief work when humanitarian disaster strikes in Pakistan. Ham radios come to Pakistan Ham radios emerged at the end of the 19th century with licensing starting from the US around 1912. One theory suggests that it was the initials of a group of early adopters—Hyman, Almy, and Murray at Harvard University’s radio club—that shortened first into HYALMU and then the name HAM. A more popular theory is that in the early days professionals looked down at the amateurs as ‘hams,’ as in ham-fisted or unskilled. Despite the pejorative etymology, the name stuck and is used proudly today. Amateur radio operators have operated here since before Partition. The oldest membership card was issued in November 1947 and a Pakistan Amateur Radio Society one from 1950 carries the call sign AP5B. Records show that one was active in Karachi in 1949 and from Quetta’s Chiltan Hotel in 1953. Photos via PARS Though these records appear in 1950, former PARS President Nasir Khan (call sign AP2NK) said in a 2019 interview that the society was formed in 1970. (This discrepancy could be explained by a change in registration rules after which the society began anew). According to Nasir Khan, many of the initial members, including him, were associated with the telecommunications industry which is why their professional interests led them to ham radio, and ultimately to create the society. When Nasir Khan passed away in 2025, PARS announced that AP2NK was now a ‘silent key’. Ham radio work is driven by enthusiasm and charitable spirit but it is still governed by regulations which say a licensed operator has to be a member of PARS to legally function. Licenses were first issued by the Post and Telegraph department, followed by the Telephone and Telegraph department, and since 1996, the Pakistan Telecommunication Authority (PTA) which now has a dedicated Radio-Based Services section. PTA records thus date back to 1962-63 even though files and folders have moved around between departments. Radio operators in Pakistan must be at least 16 years old. They join PARS as a ‘short-wave listener’ (SWL) for at least one year (like a driving learner’s license) before earning a full license (two years if you are under 18). SWLs can listen but not speak on the radio and use this time to learn mannerisms and techniques. Applications require details of equipment and operators are subjected to security verification which takes three months or more. A written test and interview follow at the PTA. Each newly licensed operator is then assigned a unique call sign, which in Pakistan begin with AP2 (or 5 for organisations) followed by the person’s initials. Radio communication takes place with phonetic codes, so AP2 will become “Alpha Papa 2”, and universally recognised Q codes, such as QTH relating to location and QSL relating to receipt of transmission. Codes such as “5 and 9”, meaning readability and signal strength respectively are at their highest, are also used. Audio of communication between hams in Islamabad and Lahore, a distance of over 283 kilometres. They announce their call signs, and confirm readability and signal strength by saying “5 and 9”. PARS helps applicants prepare and for several years, tests were conducted in coordination with the PTA. The license fee is Rs2,000 and has to be renewed every five years. Members pay the Society Rs2,000 a year, students Rs1,500 for three years and SWLs Rs4,000 for a three-year term. People over 65 years are granted lifetime licenses. Licenses can be revoked legally but according to the PTA no such case has arisen so far. Licenses usually end when holders pass away or fail to renew. The PTA does not allow mobile radio stations in its law. Exceptions can, however, be made for temporary periods in times of emergency and special need. This provision, in particular, is central to how the Society has worked in rescue efforts. ‘It’s not a hobby’ Today the group has around 600 active members of whom around 160 are amateur radio operators and the rest are SWLs or others. Most members have full-time jobs or businesses. One works in the oil and gas industry, some are telecom engineers, another is a cardiologist and several are serving members of the armed forces. Former Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Governor Amir Gulistan Janjua (AP2AGJ) was also a member. There is nothing ‘amateur’ about PARS. During initial interviews, my use of the word ‘hobby’ was met with polite clarification that every member was a professional and the operations are considered a service. The power division said the procurement inefficiencies were identified during a series of meetings presided over by the power minister, where officials reviewed actual data, contractual arrangements and market practices. “Pakistan has a significant fleet of coal-fired power plants with a combined capacity of approximately 5,280 megawatts that rely wholly or partly on imported coal. These include three major 1,320MW plants at Port Qasim, Hub Power and Sahiwal, as well as the Lucky and Jamshoro plants which also have the capability to use imported coal,” it said. The division said coal import prices for IPPs were linked to internationally recognised benchmarks such as the API-4 index, but the final price paid by a plant also depended on the discount negotiated with the supplier.
