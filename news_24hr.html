Acute hunger in Afghanistan reaches highest levels ever recorded: monitor

Acute malnutrition and hunger in Afghanistan have reached the highest levels ever recorded, with 15.9 million people facing acute food insecurity and conditions set to worsen in the coming months, according to a global hunger monitor report released on Thursday. UN agency heads have called for emergency support to stem the crisis, as a separate report details serious human rights violations from public floggings to a new ban on demonstrations. Record acute malnutrition levels for the Aug-Oct period have seen hunger levels grow for millions of Afghans according to the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), a global initiative aimed at informing decisions based on five phases of food security. Economic pressures are straining households nationwide, contributing to more than 32 per cent of the population facing crisis-level acute food insecurity phase three or above, including 3.4m people experiencing emergency phase four conditions. The current deterioration is driven by disease outbreaks and inadequate water, sanitation and hygiene conditions, compounded by sharply reduced access to nutrition services, the report found. The situation is expected to worsen from November to April during the winter lean season, when nearly 19.2m people are projected to face phase three or above, including 5m people in emergency food insecurity conditions. Acute malnutrition is also set to grow significantly, with 4.04m children under the age of five projected to need treatment between January and December 2027. Although improved rainfall and better agricultural production have supported food availability, declining humanitarian assistance is reducing households ability to cope, the report warned. The World Food Programme (WFP) and Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) issued a stark message on Thursday, calling for urgent funding to scale up food assistance, emergency agricultural support and malnutrition prevention and treatment. We still have a window to act, but it is closing fast, WFP country director John Aylieff said. With urgent support, families can get through the winter with dignity. Without it, many will face impossible choices no parent should have to make. FAO representative Richard Trenchard said families need opportunities to earn a dependable living that helps them withstand the next shock and support to get through this winter. Erosion of human rights The UN Assistance Mission in Afghanistan (UNAMA) on Thursday reported the continued erosion of basic human rights across the country between July and September, with women and girls bearing the brunt of restrictions imposed by Taliban authorities. Girls remain barred from formal education beyond grade six. Asked about the ban, a Taliban spokesperson said that problems and considerations had to be taken into account from the perspective of Sharia law. UNAMA documented more than 50 cases related to the hijab, mostly in Herat and Kabul. In one case, a 13-year-old girl was held overnight. Women were also blocked from entering health clinics and markets without a mehram. The mission recorded at least 443 cases and 97 incidents of ill-treatment by virtue and vice inspectors over offences including hijab violations, trimmed beards, skipped prayers and listening to music. While the de facto Supreme Court stopped publicly announcing such cases on its social media account and website in April, UNAMA documented public lashings of 22 men and six women on charges including theft and adultery, and the arrest of 14 former security force members, with at least four others killed. Several outlets were suspended without clear reasons or appeal procedures. A journalist arrested in July 2025 remains in custody without conviction. A new police law, published in August, bans all demonstrations.

Minorities in Pakistan continue to face rights violations despite legislative measures to protect them: HRCP

LAHORE: The Human Rights Commission of Pakistan (HRCP) on Thursday concluded that religious minorities continue to face violations of their fundamental rights — including through the misuse of blasphemy laws, forced marriages and conversions, hate speech and discriminatory practices — despite recent legislative measures aimed at protecting them. In its seventh annual report on freedom of religion, titled “Sanctuary Denied: Freedom of Religion or Belief in 2025/26”, and an accompanying press release, the HRCP highlighted that scores of individuals accused of blasphemy remained in legal limbo, while alleged perpetrators of an “organised entrapment network” had yet to be investigated. The report documented attacks on the Ahmadi community, including two attacks in Chenab Nagar in October 2025 and June 2026, and pointed out “increasingly overt attempts to exclude Ahmadis” from public and commercial life. Girls belonging to Christian and Hindu communities continued to face abductions and forced marriages, compounded by poverty and inadequate police responses, the report said. Discriminatory employment practices, controversial changes to school curricula and the marginalisation of minority concerns in mainstream media further undermined the principle of equal citizenship, it added. At the report’s launch, HRCP Director Farah Zia warned that the state’s approach to religious minorities was becoming increasingly exclusionary, according to the press release issued by the commission. Minority rights defender Nabila Feroz Bhatti highlighted the case of Kamran Gill, a Christian medical practitioner from Sheikhupura, whose dispensary was set ablaze by a mob on Oct 9 following allegations that he had shared blasphemous content on social media. HRCP Vice-Chair in Punjab Raja Ashraf said blasphemy accusations had become a “means of exploiting and endangering vulnerable people”. Arafat Mazhar, director of research and advocacy collective Shehri Pakistan, urged the public to consult lawyers representing people accused in blasphemy cases and allow courts to determine the appropriate legal procedures, rather than turning accused individuals into symbols of the broader debate over blasphemy laws. Former HRCP chairperson Hina Jilani highlighted the institutional nature of discrimination against minorities, saying: “Democracy should have no concept of ‘minority’ and ‘majority’.” On a positive note, the HRCP welcomed the passage of the National Commission for Minorities Act 2025, as well as laws in Punjab and Balochistan that raised the minimum marriageable age to 18. However, it noted that this minorities commission had yet to be constituted months after the law’s passage, raising questions about the government’s commitment to implementing the legislation. Recommendations In its recommendations, the HRCP called on the federal government to implement the National Commission for Minorities Act without further delay by constituting the commission, framing its rules and appointing its chairperson and members in accordance with the law. It urged federal and provincial governments to publish annual, disaggregated data on blasphemy cases, mob violence, forced conversion complaints and prison conditions to improve transparency and enable independent scrutiny of official claims. The HRCP also called on the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa government to enact legislation setting the minimum marriageable age at 18 for both men and women, bringing the province in line with the laws recently adopted in Punjab and Balochistan. It recommended prompt legal action against individuals and groups inciting violence, mandatory training for police and investigative agencies, including the Federal Investigation Agency, on freedom of religion or belief, and stronger safeguards for human rights defenders, lawyers and journalists working on minority rights. The report called for an end to discriminatory recruitment practices that confine many religious minorities to sanitation work, and urged an overhaul of the Evacuee Trust Property Board — a government department that administers evacuee properties — to make it more transparent, accountable and representative of minority communities. It further called for regular monitoring of seminaries linked to forced conversions and underage marriages, and recommended that religious conversions “take place in court or in the presence of a government official”. Emphasising the need to curb hate speech, the HRCP urged authorities to take legal action against incitement to violence, particularly on social media, while respecting the freedom of speech guaranteed under Article 19 of the Constitution. It called for improvements in police intelligence gathering, crowd control and community policing to prevent mob violence, as well as effective departmental accountability mechanisms to address corruption within law enforcement agencies. The report recommended reviewing school curricula to promote inclusivity and pluralism, operationalising provincial research centres envisaged under countering violent extremism laws and keeping interfaith committees active throughout the year. It also urged authorities to prevent pressure from bar associations and religious groups from influencing police and district administrations to act against particular sects or minority communities. The HRCP called for the full implementation of the Supreme Court’s 2014 judgment on religious freedoms, protection of minority places of worship and the reopening of closed places of worship. Given the severity of blasphemy charges, it recommended that investigations under Section 295-C of the Pakistan Penal Code be conducted by senior-ranking officials. It also called for public awareness campaigns explaining sensitive laws carrying capital punishment, the methods used in alleged entrapment schemes in the digital age and developments in cyber laws. The report stressed that mainstream media should be able to represent all segments of society, particularly marginalised groups and stated that no place of worship should be demolished without a clear court order issued after hearing both sides. It added that those attempting to vandalise places of worship should face legal prosecution.

The increase is expected to bring in around Rs33bn for the Power Division, which said in a statement that the additional FCA could have been significantly higher, by around Rs3.44 per unit, but targeted load shedding helped contain the increase. “The Ministry of Energy (Power Division) has averted a potential Rs5-6 per unit hike in the September fuel price adjustment (FPA) through timely measures, including targeted load management, limiting the projected adjustment to Rs2.56 per unit despite rising international fuel prices and constraints on LNG supplies,” the statement said. “Targeted load management and other measures helped limit the impact of expensive fuels used for power generation, saving consumers from an additional burden of up to Rs3.44 per unit,” the Power Division said. It added that targeted load management for approximately two to two-and-a-half hours during September helped reduce reliance on costly alternative fuels, including furnace oil and high-speed diesel, and contained the anticipated increase in the FPA. The division maintained the provision of imported re-gasified liquefied natural gas (RLNG) for power generation at domestic gas prices had further eased the burden on consumers by 83 paisa per unit. However, it noted that international fuel prices had risen sharply amid the Middle East conflict and disruptions to fuel supply chains following the closure of the Strait of Hormuz. Against this backdrop, despite Qatar’s declaration of force majeure, Pakistan continued efforts to meet its RLNG requirements through available contracted supplies and additional spot cargoes, wherever necessary, the statement said. The Power Division claimed that the combined impact of fuel price adjustments and quarterly tariff adjustments during Jan-Aug had been limited to 65 paisa per unit, equivalent to an increase of only 1.9 per cent over the base electricity tariff. It attributed this outcome partly to tariff rationalisation measures and an additional electricity consumption package for industrial and agricultural consumers, which encouraged greater use of electricity from the national grid. According to the Power Division, the September FPA is expected to be Rs1.45 per unit higher than that of August’s Rs1.11 per unit FCA, with an estimated financial impact of Rs32.7bn on consumers’ electricity bills in November. The Power Division said it was continuing to monitor developments in international fuel markets and supply chains while taking measures to minimise the impact of external price shocks on electricity consumers. On the other hand, industrial consumers are contesting the purported positive impact of industrial support package on incremental consumption and claim that Rs22.96 per unit marginal cost rate was helping a select few while the actual marginal cost of Rs34 per unit was resulting in Rs11 per unit feeding into the average consumer tariff, becoming a burden for all consumers.
