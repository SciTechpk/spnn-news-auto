Mir Raza’s grave is not a silent one

There is a particular cruelty in a name being spoken twice by death. Once when it is buried. And once when it is dug up again, because the truth could not rest with it. Mir Raza, the son of Mir Hassan Ali Khan, and Wafflix business owner, was laid into the earth of Rehorania Masjid graveyard on the 29th of July. Eleven days later, a board of doctors, a police surgeon, a magistrate, and a family that refused to let silence be the final word stood over that same soil and opened it again. I have read the report. I do not think I will ever be able to unread it. It is written in the flat, careful language that medicine keeps for what it cannot otherwise bear to say. Somewhere inside that distance is a twenty-five-year-old man who once had a face his mother could recognise without being told his name. What the doctors found confirmed what the family had feared all along, that Mir Raza did not simply die. He was taken from them. And when asked, plainly, whether his wounds came before death, someone circled a single word on the page: Yes. That word has not left me since. What stays with me most is not the injury itself, but the effort that surrounded it. The care taken. The insistence, even now, that this body still deserved to be handled gently, named properly, treated as someone rather than something. Dr Summaiya Syed, Police Surgeon of Karachi, stood at that graveside and did what very few of us are ever asked to do. She looked directly at what injustice leaves behind, and she did not turn away. There is a particular kind of courage in choosing, on an ordinary working day, to be the last honest witness a murdered man will ever have. It is not the courage of headlines. It is quieter than that, and heavier, and it asks everything of the stomach and the soul, and gives back only the satisfaction of having told the truth when it would have been so much easier not to. And yet, the final word on how Mir Raza died has still not been given. Not because there is doubt. The findings speak clearly enough. It has simply not been said out loud, in the way that matters, in the place that matters. Twelve days passed, the doctors believe, between his death and the moment anyone was able to examine him. Twelve days in which a family did not know. And then more days still, in which even his grave had to be argued for, before the truth was allowed to be looked at a second time. We spend our lives pretending we are not what that grave revealed. We make plans, we argue over small things, we behave as though we will always have tomorrow to fix what we said today. But strip away the years, and every one of us ends the same way Mir Raza did. Plain. Unrecognisable, even to those who loved us most. If that is where we are all headed, then what exactly are we fighting for when we fight for justice? I do not think it is the body. The earth takes that regardless. I think it is the truth of what was done to someone while they were still here to feel it. That is the only part of us that does not decay. It is the only inheritance a murdered man can leave behind that no grave is deep enough to bury. Karachi did not let this one go quiet. Somewhere between a graveyard and a courtroom, ordinary people, neighbours, strangers, a city that has buried too many of its sons without asking enough questions, decided that Mir Raza’s silence was not going to be mistaken for peace. They spoke. They kept speaking, until an order was signed and a grave that should have been the end of his story became instead the place where it finally began to be told properly. That is not a small thing. In a country where so many cases are allowed to fade as quietly as a body returns to the earth, the refusal of ordinary citizens to look away is very often the only justice the dead will ever be given. So I find myself asking the only question I have left. If the truth has already been found, plainly, and written down, and confirmed by a circled word on a page, then what are we still waiting for? Where does justice go, in the space between a death and its acknowledgment? And where, in a city that loved this man loudly enough to bring him back up into the light so his story could be told rightly, does our humanity actually stand today? Not in the vigils. Not in the slogans. But in the quiet, unglamorous work of finishing what we started for Mir Raza. I do not have that answer yet. I am not sure any of us do. But I know this much. A city that will not let its dead be forgotten has not yet forgotten itself. And whatever else remains unresolved, that much still holds true: somewhere beneath all our exhaustion and all our grief, we still believe a man’s name is worth saying twice. Judicial probe launched into death of 2 girls in Lahore police custody

LAHORE: A judicial magistrate has initiated an inquiry under Section 176 of the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC) into the suspicious death of two underage girls in Lahore police custody on Aug 4, as mystery continues to surround the horrific, tragic incident. The judicial inquiry was launched after the families of the victims – Amina and Anmol – demanded an impartial investigation into what happened to the girls in the police custody for well over two hours – from the time of their arrest to calling a Rescue 1122 team to the Township police station on Aug 4. Section 176 of the CrPC empowers a magistrate to inquire into the cause of death when a person dies in police custody or under some suspicious circumstances. The law also empowers the magistrate to order exhumation of a body for the postmortem examination, besides recording statements and examining witnesses on oath. Dawn spoke to police officers familiar with the matter and attempted to map the sequence of events from the girls’ arrest to their deaths. According to an official, around 2:54pm on Aug 4 an emergency call was received on police helpline 15 from complainant, Zeshan Latif, who alleged that two girls entered his house and fraudulently stole gold jewellery and cash belonging to his wife. A sub-inspector (SI) of the Township police, Ghulam Ghous, reached the scene at 3:11pm and brought the two girls to the police station, where they were handed over to the duty clerk (Muharrar), Tasnim Arif, at 3:26pm. Mr Latif subsequently submitted an application, which was e-tagged at 4:40pm, and an FIR was registered at 4:50pm against the two girls. The Township police station then recorded a report at 5:20pm regarding the transfer of the girls’ custody from the operations wing to the investigation staff. According to the official, the operations wing’s muharrar claimed that, following registration of the FIR, he handed over the girls to the investigation wing Muharrar Bilal, in accordance with the prescribed procedure. The situation took a grim turn shortly afterwards when, at 6:03pm, the central office of Rescue 1122 received an emergency call from the Township police regarding the deteriorating condition of both girls. A Rescue 1122 team reached the police station at 6:14pm and found one of the girls “almost dead”, while the other was unconscious, according to the official. Both were shifted to Jinnah Hospital, where doctors pronounced the unconscious girl dead. The other girl, who was reportedly in critical condition, also died shortly after in the hospital. As news of the girls’ death spread, senior police officers from the Saddar Division’s operations and investigation wings reached the Township police station at around 9:30pm and sensing gravity of the situation apprised higher authorities about the incident. The official says that incident triggered a row between the operations and investigation wings, with officers from both sides reportedly shifting the responsibility for the girls’ custody onto each other. The time between 3:11pm, when the girls were taken into custody, and 6:03pm, when Rescue 1122 was called, is therefore crucial in the judicial inquiry, he adds. Video footage that surfaced after the incident shows the girls apparently in a relatively stable condition when they were brought to the police station. In one recording, a police official can be seen questioning them. Another video recorded by a camera installed in the duty clerk’s room shows Amina and Anmol sitting on chairs inside the police station. They appear conscious and are seen interacting with the duty clerk before leaving the room. Police officials, however, say that details of the girls’ interaction and interrogation are not covered by the station’s security cameras. Immediately after the incident, the investigation police claimed that drug overdose was the likely cause of the girls’ death. The claim, however, has yet to be conclusively established through the postmortem reports, which are crucial evidence in determining the death cause. Police were still awaiting the final postmortem reports when Dawn spoke to officials. Investigation SSP Mohammad Naveed strongly rejected allegations that the girls had been tortured in custody. “Though I am not aware whether the final postmortem reports have been issued, I can assure you that the allegations of custodial torture are absolutely false and misleading,” he claimed. The SSP alleges that both girls were chronic drug users and had apparently consumed a strong addictive substance before being brought to the police station. “During their custody at the police station, they fainted, probably because of the impact of the drugs they had consumed, and later died of the same cause,” he claimed. Mr Naveed maintains that as a major portion of the girls interrogation had been captured by surveillance cameras installed at the police station, it shows girls were not maltreated in custody. He says the judicial inquiry had been initiated at the request of senior police officers under Section 176 of the CrPC and that its findings would bring out the circumstances of the deaths. Asked why the case had not been referred to the Federal Investigation Agency (FIA), the SSP said the agency is usually asked to investigate the cases involving allegations that a suspect had died as a result of custodial torture or killed in a police encounter. “This case is different, as it concerns the deaths of two suspects in custody, and there was no need for an FIA investigation,” he insisted. The judicial inquiry is expected to examine the circumstances surrounding the girls’ detention, the transfer of their custody between the operations and investigation wings, the available CCTV footage, statements of police officials and witnesses, and the postmortem reports’ findings to dig out the facts. Genuine governance

ON the surface, the Punjab cabinet’s decision to allocate funds for local government elections is a welcome step towards restoring a democratic tier of governance that has remained absent for more than four years — in fact, for much longer in practical terms. After years of premature dissolution, court-mandated reinstatement, repeated delays and legal and administrative manoeuvring, the allocation suggests that Punjab is finally preparing to hold polls that were constitutionally due by April 2022. The timing, however, is difficult to ignore. The announcement comes only days after the interior minister, with the public endorsement of DG ISPR, argued that creating new provinces or administrative units could improve governance. The sudden will to revive LGs may well be aimed at demonstrating that devolution to the grassroots can improve governance and that LGs offer an alternative to redrawing provincial boundaries as proposed by certain quarters. That argument would have carried weight had Punjab chosen to establish a genuinely empowered LG system. Unfortunately, it has done the opposite. The existing Punjab LG law is devolution only in name. While it provides for elected councils, it concentrates meaningful administrative and financial authority in the hands of the provincial government and bureaucracy, making elected local representatives dependent on the goodwill of officials for even routine decisions. Such an arrangement defeats the very purpose of LGs, which is to bring decision-making closer to the citizens. Punjab’s legislation compares unfavourably even with that of other provinces where LGs enjoy relatively greater governance space. The country’s largest province has opted for perhaps the most centralised model of local governance. The experience of the past six decades illustrates why merely holding elections is not enough. Successive governments have dissolved local bodies and amended or replaced LG laws at their whim. Thus a constitutional obligation has been repeatedly ignored and grassroots democracy treated as an optional exercise rather than a permanent feature of governance. Meaningful devolution requires much more than elected councillors. A truly functional third tier of government must have constitutionally protected administrative and financial autonomy. LGs should be able to raise, allocate and spend resources without provincial interference. The local bureaucracy and police should be accountable to elected local representatives rather than exclusively to the province. Without such authority, elected councils are little more than advisory bodies with limited capacity to address citizens’ issues. The governance challenges of Punjab — and of other provinces — stem from the excessive centralisation of power. Holding polls under a law that denies genuine autonomy to LGs may satisfy a constitutional formality. Tick, tick ... boom

IN hindsight, revolutions are easy to predict. Years, decades and even centuries after such events, scholars will write volumes explaining how prevailing conditions made such violent action inevitable, with the most common preconditions being extreme economic inequality and injustice along with an oppressive state which is not only prone to overreaction but is also so wedded to using force that it has forgotten all other means of conflict reduction and resolution. What is next to impossible to predict is the single spark that sets off the explosion, the final straw that breaks the back of the ancient regime. And all too often, that spark is so small as to be insignificant and the straw is almost weightless compared to the existing burden accumulated over years. In Bangladesh, the issue of job reservations led to the swift end of Hasina Wajed’s 15-year reign and resulted in her fleeing the country, but how many jobs were actually reserved this way? According to most calculations, they were no more than 1,000 at most and given Bangladesh’s population and relative economic prosperity one would imagine that this would have been a relatively minor issue. Certainly, Hasina Wajed — with the overconfidence and isolation that absolute power breeds — thought it would blow over. Instead, it blew her regime away. Similarly, in Nepal, while anger over widespread political corruption, high unemployment and extreme economic inequality and frustration was the norm, the spark that led ‘Gen Z’ to riot was the government’s September 2025 ban on 26 social media platforms. While the government framed this as a regulatory measure, the protesters were convinced that the real reason for the ban was to block a social media trend that focused on the privileges and wealth of the children and close relatives of Nepal’s ruling class. The economic divide had always been a reality, but now, for the first time, a poor unemployed youngster in Nepal could see the sons and daughters of ministers live it up on Instagram, spending more on a single purchase than that youth could hope to earn in a lifetime. By the time states get around to addressing the spark, the fire has spread. In Bangladesh, Hasina Wajed got the supreme court to reduce the reservation quota to seven per cent but it was too little, too late; the fuse had already been lit and the protests had become an uprising against authoritarianism and the loss of life during the government crackdown. Ensconced in her palace, one imagines that Hasina simply didn’t understand what had hit her. A single spark can set off an explosion. In Nepal the government lifted the social media ban just a few days after imposing it but by that time several dozen protesters had been killed and government buildings set on fire. Four days after the ban was imposed, and one day after it was lifted, the prime minister had to resign. India’s ‘cockroach’ movement was milder in comparison. However, it represented not just anger at exam leaks but a deeper frustration with a system seemingly fully captured by the Sangh agenda and networks. Despite the state’s surprise that time-tested heavy-handed tactics and media smears no longer seemed to work, it acted with far more foresight than its neighbours by addressing the central demands before the movement spread out of control and by not resorting to deadly force. The final fate of the movement remains to be decided, but for now, it seems the crisis has passed even though it exposed the chinks in Narendra Modi’s hitherto impenetrable armour. Often, state overreaction to a movement or protest causes that protest to balloon into rebellion and revolution even if that was not the original intention of the demonstrators. Case in point: a large but mostly peaceful procession of peasant workers led by Orthodox priest Father Georgy Gapon on Jan 9, 1905. Gapon was no revolutionary and nor were revolutionary elements prominent in the crowd. In fact, anti-tsar parties like the Bolsheviks had disapproved of the march due to its lack of political demands. Carrying icons and portraits of the tsar, the crowd regarded Nicholas II as their benevolent ‘little father’ whom, they hoped, would listen to their pleas and save them from exploitation. Instead, the crowd was fired upon by panicked imperial troops resulting in hundreds of deaths. Distraught and dismayed, surrounded by the dead and wounded, the previously pro-tsar Gapon was heard to shout: “There is no God any longer. There is no tsar.” And from here on, the days of imperial Russia were numbered. Here then are lessons for all states, inc­luding our own: address concerns before they metastasize beyond treatment and just because a strategy has worked in the past there is no guarantee that it will not backfire in the present. Capacity payments, circular debt and costly contractual obligations have emerged among the largest structural drivers of the country’s fiscal stress. The defining feature of many Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) is the capacity payment mechanism. Under these contracts, power producers receive fixed payments to recover capital costs, debt servicing and fixed operating expenses regardless of whether electricity is generated. The economy has reached a stage where structural reforms can no longer be postponed Consumers therefore pay not only for electricity consumed but also for maintaining power plants that may remain idle for years. As installed capacity expanded faster than electricity demand, these fixed obligations became one of the largest components of electricity tariffs. The financial burden is immense. During FY25, Pakistani consumers paid approximately Rs 1.81tr in capacity charges — around Rs 14.3 per unit — while actual energy costs amounted to only Rs9 per unit. Capacity payments accounted for nearly 61pc of the total electricity bill of Rs2.94tr. Projections for 2026 indicate capacity charges of around Rs 1.7tr, equivalent to roughly Rs17 per unit. The burden is compounded by dollar-indexed returns embedded in many early Independent Power Producers (IPP) contracts. Although these guarantees attracted much-needed private investment, they also exposed Pakistan to exchange-rate risk, with every depreciation of the rupee automatically increasing government payment obligations. Projects developed under the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC) illustrate this vulnerability. These contractual obligations have become a major driver of Pakistan’s circular debt. When distribution companies fail to recover the full cost of electricity because of transmission losses, theft, poor recoveries and delayed subsidies, payments to power producers are postponed. The resulting liabilities accumulate across the supply chain, forcing repeated government borrowing simply to keep the sector operational. Although the government reduced circular debt from Rs2.39tr to Rs1.61tr during FY25 through extraordinary financial interventions, the improvement proved temporary. By the close of FY26, circular debt had climbed back to approximately Rs1.84tr, well above the IMF target. Recognising the severity of the challenge, the government renegotiated contracts with fourteen IPPs in January 2025, replacing parts of the traditional “take-or-pay” model with arrangements more closely linked to actual electricity generation. These revisions are expected to generate lifetime savings of approximately Rs1.4tr, while settling returns on equity in Pakistani rupees instead of open-ended dollar indexation marks another important reform. However, the largest challenge remains unresolved. CPEC-related IPPs still account for roughly Rs1.4tr in outstanding receivables and continue to dominate the circular debt equation. Until these liabilities are comprehensively addressed, durable fiscal relief is likely to remain elusive. Pakistan’s challenge is therefore not the existence of IPPs but the design of contracts that balance investor confidence with fiscal sustainability. Without a comprehensive overhaul of power-sector governance and future PPAs, the IPP model will continue to weigh heavily on public finances. Yet energy-sector reform alone cannot restore long-term fiscal sustainability. Our broader fiscal challenge also stems from weaknesses in the intergovernmental fiscal framework, making federal-provincial resource sharing and fiscal governance the next critical area for structural reform. Pakistan’s fiscal architecture has reached a stage where structural reforms can no longer be postponed. Following the report’s release, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif constituted a high-level committee to review its recommendations in consultation with provincial governments and other stakeholders. The report does not advocate reversing devolution; rather, it proposes reforms to make Pakistan’s federal system more efficient, accountable and development-oriented.
