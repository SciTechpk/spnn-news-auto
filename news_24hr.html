It typically takes place every two to seven years and lasts around nine to 12 months. Conditions oscillate between El Nino and its opposite La Nina, with neutral conditions in between. El Nino ‘strengthening rapidly’ “Sea-surface temperatures in the central and eastern tropical Pacific are forecast to increase to record levels during October-November-December 2026, reflecting the strengthening of the El Nino event which will drive weather patterns and impact economies, ecosystems and communities across the globe,” the WMO said. The forecast is based on consensus across 13 leading national and international meteorological centres. “El Nino is firmly established and strengthening rapidly,” said WMO chief Celeste Saulo. “WMO forecasts indicate a near-100 per cent likelihood that it will persist through February 2027, potentially being one of the strongest since at least 1950.” Saulo explained that El Nino does not add new heat to the planet, but releases heat being stored in the ocean. “The ocean it’s drawing from is the warmest we’ve ever measured it. There is simply more heat available to release,” she said. Sea temperatures record The index which measures sea surface temperature anomalies across the central-eastern equatorial Pacific — the region at the heart of El Nino — increased from 1.6°C above normal in June to 2.5°C above normal in August.
