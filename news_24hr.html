How the US controls Iraq's oil revenues

US forces are set to withdraw on Wednesday from their last bases in Iraq, a departure celebrated as a victory by Iran and its allies, which have deep influence in the country where 4,500 Americans died during more than two decades of war. Still, since its 2003 invasion of Iraq, the US has held effective control of the country’s oil revenue dollars, giving Washington extraordinary leverage over Baghdad’s affairs, with implications for regional dynamics involving Iran. How does the US control Iraq’s oil revenues? This stems primarily from the management of Iraq’s oil income through the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. After the 2003 invasion, the Coalition Provisional Authority (CPA), led by the US, set up the Development Fund for Iraq (DFI), held at the New York Fed, and designed to collect the oil revenues for use in reconstruction and development efforts. A US Marine watches a woman and her child during a series of raids to hunt arms and guerrilla suspects south of Baghdad, Iraq on December 1, 2004. —Reuters/File The Fund also aimed to protect the Iraqi oil revenues from lawsuits and claims relating to Saddam Hussein’s rule. An executive order by then president George W. Bush to set up the arrangement has been renewed by every president since. The DFI eventually became an account of the Central Bank of Iraq at the New York Federal Reserve, as it remains today. What leverage does this give the US over Iraq? Oil is Iraq’s most important revenue source, making up about 90 per cent of the state budget. This gives Washington significant sway over the country’s economic and political stability. When the Iraqi government asked US troops to leave in 2020, Washington reportedly threatened to cut Iraq’s access to the New York Federal Reserve funds, with Baghdad ultimately backing down. Why has the arrangement endured for so long? The system helped anchor Iraq’s financial stability and safeguards state finances, government officials told Reuters on condition of anonymity. It boosts international confidence in the management of oil income, eases access to US dollars needed for trade and imports, and protects revenues from external claims and financial shocks, including claims by creditors and lawsuits, they said. It also allows the government to push back against some actors, such as Iran-allied groups, who seek fewer curbs on dollar access. Last year, the US imposed sanctions on Iraqi banks and individuals it accused of laundering money for Iran. How has this arrangement affected Iraq? The heavy curbs on US dollar supply led to a parallel, informal market for dollars, resulting in a price spread between the official exchange rate set by the central bank and that on the black market. The price difference is essentially a risk premium for dealing outside the formal system. In his second term, President Donald Trump has pursued a campaign of maximum pressure on Iran, with Iraq often caught in the crossfire as Tehran has used it as a vital economic lung. The war he launched with Israel against Iran on February 28 added further pressure on Baghdad. What is the status of Iraq’s oil revenue management? Iraqi oil revenues remain in the custody of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. CBI has historically used dollar auctions, formally known as the foreign currency window, as the main mechanism to supply dollars. Private banks and exchange houses could bid daily to purchase US dollars using Iraqi dinars. Iraq formally ended the auction system at the start of 2025 after significant US pressure, part of a broad crackdown on alleged siphoning of dollars to sanctioned parties, especially Iran. Bangladesh’s 2024 student-led uprising was fuelled by a demand that coveted government jobs be awarded on merit, not through quotas. In 2024, it was proposed to reinstate quotas, sparking student protests. That escalated into a mass uprising, and Hasina was overthrown. Today, 93pc are filled on merit. Swedish rights prize honours Pakistani human rights lawyer Jalila Haider

Human rights lawyer and activist Jalila Haider on Wednesday received the Right Livelihood prize, dubbed an “alternative Nobel”. The Sweden-based Right Livelihood Foundation honoured Haider “for breaking barriers, as the first female lawyer from her Hazara community, to challenge ethnic, state and patriarchal persecution, guided by the conviction that no human being is illegal.” According to the foundation’s website, Haider in 2018 “led a seven-day hunger strike against the targeted killing of Hazaras” that led to direct negotiations between the army chief and Hazara women, “resulting in a sustained decrease in attacks against the community”. It added that Haider had combined legal work with street activism and community empowerment, securing “justice for thousands targeted or ignored by those in power, specifically ethnic, gender and religious minorities, as well as refugees”. “Her law firm has also helped restore freedom of movement for more than 600 political activists and human rights defenders,” the Right Livelihood Foundation said. Haider is a founding member of the Women’s Democratic Front as well, the foundation said, adding that she “helped pioneer Balochistan’s first Women’s March, a street mobilisation that sparked discussions on feminism across the deeply patriarchal region”. Through her organisation ‘We the Humans’, Haider has “helped over 400 widows of terrorism victims gain financial independence”, the foundation said. “Despite detention, cyber-harassment and death threats, she continues to work towards a world where, in her words, ‘no human being is illegal’,” the foundation said. The Right Livelihood Award was established in 1980, when Swedish-German stamp collector Jakob von Uexkull sold part of his collection to fund it, after the foundation behind the Nobel Prizes refused to create new awards in the fields of environmental protection and international development. A prominent US artificial intelligence researcher and two women-led rights groups were also recognised. The jury said in a statement it had chosen US artificial intelligence researcher Timnit Gebru “for challenging concentrated power in artificial intelligence and pioneering digital technology rooted in justice, lived experience and human agency.” Gebru is known for her work examining how biases influence AI and in 2017, Gebru co-founded Black in AI. The foundation also gave the nod to the Rural Women’s Assembly — a grassroots movement of more than 178,000 small-scale farmers across 11 Southern African nations. The Georgian Young Lawyers’ Association (GYLA), which the jury described as “Georgia’s oldest and most prominent human rights organisation,” was also honoured.

After more than a decade, the US-led coalition is ending its mission in Iraq, having helped save the country from militant groups while causing persistent friction. Coalition troops were deployed to Iraq and Syria in 2014 to fight the Islamic State (IS) group, which had seized large swathes of both countries and committed massacres and atrocities. The group was defeated in Iraq in 2017 and in Syria in 2019, but coalition forces stayed on to battle the group’s remnants. Baghdad and Washington agreed in 2024 to wind down the coalition’s mission, and its troops withdrew gradually from bases across the country, restricting their presence to the autonomous northern Kurdistan region. Their final withdrawal from there is scheduled for Wednesday. The Iraqi government believes the coalition’s withdrawal removes the excuse powerful militant factions have used to retain their weapons. Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi declared that October 1, the day following the coalition’s departure, should be celebrated as Sovereignty Day. It is “a national occasion that affirms the completion of Iraq’s path toward sovereignty and consolidates the presence of the state and its institutions”, the heads of the executive, legislative and judicial branches said on Monday. They added that “the presence of weapons outside state authority must be addressed… in line with timelines set by the government, in a manner that strengthens state sovereignty”. For the good of its people Zaidi, who came to power this year with the US’s blessing, has vowed to reinforce the state’s monopoly on arms and ensure that armed factions hand over their weapons to the state. Makkah moment

THERE is no disputing that the coming together of Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye comes at a critical juncture for West Asia, even if several aspects around the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement’s precise operationalisation remain unclear. There are good reasons for Islamabad to view this as a significant strategic opportunity. The arrangement, forged in the shadow of the US and Israeli war on Iran, responds to a growing imperative for regional stakeholders to exercise greater control in stabilising a region fractured by conflict. It also builds on long-standing relationships between three important middle powers whose security is bound up in regional outcomes. Pakistan and Saudi Arabia have decades of military and diplomatic cooperation behind them. Turkiye has important extensive defence ties with both. With US retrenchment from the region increasingly appearing inevitable, institutionalising these relationships offers some much-needed strategic coherence to a region beset by complex challenges. But for Pakistan to make the most of this moment — and for it to translate the compact into lasting strategic advantage — several caveats deserve consideration. First, for this ‘non-directional’ arrangement to truly be regionally tenable, it must avoid becoming a source of further division in the Middle East. For Pakistan, the present crisis involving Ansarullah (the Houthis) in the Red Sea offers an early test of whether the alliance can resist being drawn into the region’s existing rivalries. There is no disputing the fact that the stability of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia matters enormously to Pakistan: it is an important source of energy and financial support, and home to a large Pakistani diaspora whose remittances contribute significantly to foreign exchange earnings. The strategic opportunity comes with caveats. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has also dramatically increased Pakistan’s dependency on the flow of oil through the Red Sea. But direct military involvement against the Houthis does risk drawing the alliance deeper into existing regional rivalries, risking Islamabad’s position with Tehran, and testing Pakistan’s stated policy commitment to avoiding camp politics. Ultimately, Pakistan’s usefulness to the alliance might lie in its ability to exploit the diplomatic space it retains with both Tehran and Riyadh, using those channels to encourage dialogue and de-escalation. Second, in terms of objectives, military coordination, defence-industrial cooperation and, ultimately, collective defence are not incompatible with one another. But it is that final commitment, however, that will require the most careful thinking about where Pakistan’s obligations to the other two countries begin and end. A decade ago, in 2015, Pakistan’s parliament played an important role in the decision not to commit Pakistani troops to the Saudi-led war in Yemen. While the intervening decade has arguably seen an erosion of Pakistan’s democratic institutions, the country’s leaders must still remember that major commitments abroad still require political dialogue and consensus at home, not least given the country’s demographic diversity and the ability of external security choices to reverberate internally. Finally, and relatedly, those shaping Pakistan’s strategic direction would be wise to recognise that Pakistan’s expanding strategic horizons cannot be divorced from the pressures it confronts at home and that while there is no arguing that regional influence can be an asset, it is important to build the domestic capacity to sustain. At present, Pakistan confronts a serious trifecta of terrorism, political instability and persistent economic fr­­agility. This year has been among Pakis­tan’s deadliest for terrorism in recent memory. Fraught go­­vernance challe­nges in KP and Ba­­lochistan add to that insecurity. For a country already contending with multiple sources of instability at home, expanding commitments abroad without thought to improved governance and/or a corresponding domestic reset at home risks weakening the very foundations on which any newfound regional influence must ultimately rest. None of these caveats detracts from the rare opportunity Makkah presents. In the shadow of looming threats from India and Afghanistan, and general instability unleashed by America’s war in Iran, the arrangement offers Pakistan the chance to do several things: to extract strategic currency from its military capacity, to expand defence-industrial cooperation with important partners, and to eventually be a consequential voice in shaping whatever security architecture emerges from the ashes of the US war against Iran. But these things also require more than aspiration. For Islamabad, that means navigating existing regional fault lines and developing a better political compact at home.
