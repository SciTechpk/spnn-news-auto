Pakistan has Portugal’s “full support” for the European Union’s (EU) GSP+ status, Portuguese Minister of State and Foreign Affairs Paulo Rangel said on Monday during Pakistan-Portugal delegation-level talks held in Islamabad. Pakistan’s current GSP+ status is set to expire in 2027, after which Islamabad will seek a renewal. GSP+ status is a special incentive awarded to developing countries to “pursue sustainable development and good governance” in exchange for cutting import duties to zero on two-thirds of the tariff lines of its exports. The status was granted to Pakistan in 2014, resulting in a 108 per cent increase in Pakistani textile exports to the EU due to concessional tariffs. In October 2023, the European Parliament unanimously voted to extend the GSP+ status for another four years until 2027 for developing countries, including Pakistan. The upcoming GSP+ monitoring mission will scrutinise Pakistan’s record on 32 international conventions tied to the trade scheme. Maintaining GSP+ status is critical for Pakistan to keep export costs low. Rangel, in his remarks, stressed that both Pakistan and the EU would mutually benefit from cooperation, adding that “we should not hesitate on developing these bilateral platforms”. The Pakistani delegation was led by Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar. Capitalising on Middle East conflict

Rarely does a government in Pakistan respond swiftly and purposefully to a crisis by bringing public and private stakeholders together, building consensus and implementing corrective measures. The US-Iran conflict presented such an opportunity, forcing Pakistan to protect its maritime supply chains while opening new possibilities for its ports and shipping industry. The prime minister constituted two committees to address the emerging challenges. This article focuses on the committee headed by Federal Minister for Maritime Affairs Muhammad Junaid Anwar Chaudhry, of which the writer was a member. Its mandate is to protect maritime trade flows and energy supplies while identifying opportunities arising from disruptions to regional shipping. The committee met regularly, sometimes daily, to address operational bottlenecks and develop a coordinated response. It helped maintain oil supplies and secure some liquefied natural gas (LNG) cargoes from Qatar despite force majeure following attacks on its production facilities and disruption of the Strait of Hormuz. One of its earliest initiatives followed an approach by the Pakistan Ship’s Agents Association (PSAA), which pointed out that disruption at major regional transshipment hubs offered Pakistan an opportunity to attract cargo previously routed through competing ports. Despite having a transshipment policy for more than two decades, Pakistan had failed to attract significant business because of restrictive customs procedures and uncompetitive port charges. US-Iran conflict is pushing Pakistan to protect its maritime supply chains and opening new possibilities for its ports and shipping industry The committee engaged customs authorities to remove these impediments. Rules were amended to allow transshipment cargo to be stored at off-dock terminals, easing pressure on congested on-dock facilities. The requirement for mandatory scanning of all containers moving to off-dock terminals was also replaced with a risk-based non-intrusive inspection system. Customs procedures were further revised to accommodate non-containerised break-bulk and roll-on/roll-off cargo. New feeder shipping services to Gulf and Red Sea ports were also encouraged to maintain outlets for Pakistani imports and exports amid disruption to established shipping routes. Another significant achievement came on July 29, when the Joint War Committee of Lloyd’s London formally stated that war-risk insurance premiums for vessels calling at Pakistani ports would no longer be automatically triggered by their classification under listed areas. This is expected to reduce shipping costs and improve the commercial attractiveness of Karachi, Port Qasim and Gwadar, removing a reputational disadvantage that had affected Pakistan’s ports for more than a decade. The committee also addressed the longstanding problem of auctioned containers occupying valuable port space. Customs authorities allowed these containers to be shifted to licensed off-dock facilities, freeing terminal capacity, facilitating faster cargo handling and enabling shipping lines to redeploy their containers for fresh business. Another breakthrough was permission for less-than-container-load (LCL) transshipment. This involves importing smaller consignments, consolidating or deconsolidating cargo, undertaking value addition and re-exporting it. Pakistan has long sought to develop this business along the lines of Jebel Ali. One major US-based shipping company has already selected Pakistan over India for its LCL transshipment operations. Gwadar Port also witnessed a notable increase in activity, receiving more than a dozen vessels carrying around 200,000 tonnes of transshipment cargo, including steel plates, machinery and fertiliser, within three months. This exceeded the vessel and cargo activity recorded during the preceding three years. Five additional vessels conducted anchorage operations. To improve competitiveness, reduced port and terminal tariffs were introduced for transshipment cargo, with concessions linked to the proportion of such cargo handled. A Far Eastern shipping company has consequently expressed interest in shifting its transshipment operations from India’s Mundra Port to Karachi. The committee also took up complaints about excessive and retrospective war-risk and emergency conflict surcharges imposed by shipping companies, including on cargo that had sailed before the conflict began. Customs authorities directed shipping lines and their agents to ensure transparency, disclose charges in advance and refrain from unjustified retrospective levies. A mechanism was established for affected importers and exporters to submit documentary evidence for scrutiny. Importantly, revised customs procedures will allow consignees to choose between on-dock and off-dock terminals for cargo clearance. This will enable cargo to be moved directly to off-dock facilities, where shipping containers can be emptied and returned promptly, reducing detention and demurrage costs while improving port turnaround times. The committee’s recommendations were subsequently submitted to the prime minister, who constituted the National Coordination and Management Council to coordinate decisions concerning the economic, trade and energy consequences of the Middle East conflict. The next phase offers further opportunities. The Ministry of Maritime Affairs and Port Qasim Authority are working on a proposed automobile import and re-export zone where used vehicles could be imported, refurbished and exported. Much of this business is currently conducted in Dubai and Sharjah, often by Pakistani operators. A proposed energy city at Port Qasim or another suitable location could also provide strategic petroleum storage outside the Strait of Hormuz. International energy companies have reportedly expressed interest. The potential economic gains extend beyond port revenues to shipping, logistics, storage, transportation and employment. Each transshipment container handled in Pakistan could generate an estimated $150 in associated economic activity. An additional throughput of 500,000 twenty-foot equivalent units (TEUs) is achievable within one to three years, rising to one million within five years. The crisis has demonstrated that trade facilitation is essential to Pakistan’s economic competitiveness. A trade facilitation board is expected to examine remaining bottlenecks in cargo clearance and logistics costs. Sustaining these gains, however, will require consistent policies, institutional coordination and a lasting commitment to removing regulatory friction. ADB invests in Pakistan-based B2B fintech company Neem

ISLAMABAD: The Asian Development Bank (ADB) on Monday said it invested an undisclosed amount in Neem, a Pakistan-based B2B fintech company that helps businesses receive, manage, and pay out money digitally. In a statement, the Manila-based lending agency said its investment will flow through ADB Ventures, the venture capital arm of the ADB. The seed investment will support Neem’s product development, help expand its team, and accelerate the onboarding of enterprise and merchant clients across Pakistan, the ADB said. Pakistan has made strong progress in digitising consumer payments, with the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP)’s instant payment system, Raast, and mobile wallets bringing millions of people onto digital rails. Business payments, however, have not kept pace. According to SBP, an estimated 71 per cent of retail transaction value is still settled in cash, and companies looking to go digital must integrate separately with banks, wallets, payroll providers and reconciliation tools, a process that can be slow, costly, and hard to scale. Neem aims to close this gap by giving businesses a single platform to manage how money comes in, how it is tracked, and how it is paid out. Its API-first system combines collections, disbursements, a programmable ledger and finance workflows, and connects directly with a company’s existing operating systems to automate settlement and reconciliation in real time. Neem currently serves more than 70 enterprise clients across logistics, utilities, insurance, manufacturing and retail. “Pakistan is laying a strong foundation for digital payments, and the next step is ensuring that businesses of all sizes can fully benefit from this progress,” said Emma Fan, ADB Country Director for Pakistan. “Neem is helping enterprises move away from cash and fragmented systems toward payment solutions that are faster, more transparent and easier to manage.” The impact extends well beyond the enterprises Neem serves directly. As businesses digitise their payments, the suppliers, small merchants and gig workers they pay are brought into the formal financial system. Neem’s solutions are built to meet the payment needs of businesses of every size, including women-owned businesses and micro-entrepreneurs, so access to modern financial infrastructure is not limited to large enterprises. Limited access to finance is often presented as a key challenge preventing businesses, and the economy, from growing. Last year, the State Bank governor argued that weak investment is “directly linked” to low savings. The argument has serious consequences. Taken at face value, governments and policymakers should focus on promoting higher savings, encouraging foreign money to enter the economy, and decreasing the cost of financing. No doubt, successive governments have gone to great lengths to make elaborate foreign trips, sign memoranda of understanding, offer incentives and fast-track approvals through ad hoc arrangements in their attempts to attract foreign investment. Yet, the overall story remains that of a failure. Foreign direct investment has averaged just under one per cent of GDP between 2006 and 2025, among the lowest in the world. The world average is 2.6pc. At home, governments have pushed for greater access to financial instruments to increase household savings, promoted Islamic finance to respond to people’s religious preferences, and launched schemes to decrease the cost of borrowing for businesses. However, between 2005 and 2025, investment averaged just 15.7pc of GDP, against an average of 29.8pc for lower-middle-income countries. Only eight of 168 countries invested less. Private investment has been even lower, at close to 10pc of GDP in recent years. What if the problem has been misdiagnosed and access to finance is not the central problem facing the country? To see this, consider what the largest businesses – non-financial corporations listed on the stock market – do with the internal finance they already have in the form of profits. Data published by the State Bank of Pakistan show that, between 2005 and 2025, these firms earned, in today’s prices, Rs20.7 trillion after tax and paid out Rs10.7tr, more than half, as cash dividends. This result is not driven by a high payout in a few years. Instead, firms paid out more than half of their profits in dividends in 15 of the 21 years, and paid out Rs500 billion in FY25 alone. The share is higher still in food (63pc), autos (59pc), other manufacturing (59pc) and chemicals (57pc). Put simply, Pakistan’s largest firms are not short of money. Instead, they are short of reasons to invest it. Policy measures must focus on reducing uncertainty for firms by providing a stable macro environment, consistent enforcement of regulation, and the rule of law, which ensures trust in the judiciary The high dividend payout ratio is not driven by weak firms. The fifth of privately owned firms with the highest returns on assets earned 83pc of all private firms’ profits and paid out 54pc of them. The payout is even higher in the case of multinational subsidiaries, which paid out 68pc of their profits. In other words, while governments court new foreign investors, those already in the country send much of their profits home. Paying dividends is not undesirable in itself. However, how much firms pay out also depends on the opportunities they have to invest. In a study published in 2000 in The Journal of Finance, La Porta and co-authors examine 4,000 firms across 33 countries and find that, in common-law countries, faster-growing firms pay lower dividends. Pakistan’s high payouts are consistent with the explanation that firms see few opportunities to invest at home. High dividends are, therefore, a symptom, not the problem. What, then, is missing? Investment in plants and machinery takes years to pay back and is difficult to reverse. As a result, firms invest only when returns are predictable and contracts can be enforced. Pakistan performs poorly on both counts. First, the economy swings from boom to bust every few years, and the current IMF programme is the country’s 24th. Such uncertainty makes firms delay investment. Second, institutions are weak. The World Justice Project’s 2025 Rule of Law Index ranks Pakistan 130th of 143 countries, 129th for civil justice and 127th for regulatory enforcement. This matters because complex production, which relies on contractual relationships with many suppliers, develops mainly when courts can be trusted and contracts are expected to be enforced. Together the evidence points to a different diagnosis. While access to finance still matters, particularly for smaller firms, it cannot explain why the largest and most profitable ones pay out so much of what they earn. Another scheme focusing on providing cheap credit or another investment conference aimed at foreign investors is unlikely to change what firms do with their profits. Instead, policy measures must focus on reducing uncertainty for firms by providing a stable macro environment, consistent enforcement of regulation, and the rule of law, which ensures trust in the judiciary. The test of success is straightforward: whether Pakistan’s most profitable firms start reinvesting their own profits at home. Recent policy, however, points the other way. Rather than strengthening the predictable rules and institutions that encourage investment, the government has increasingly relied on discretionary arrangements. The Special Investment Facilitation Council (SIFC), set up in 2023, can recommend relaxing regulations for individual projects, replacing rules that apply to all with case-by-case decisions.
