No court without trust

LAST month, at its 49th meeting, the Law and Justice Commission of Pakistan, chaired by the chief justice, recommended establishing an ‘International Commercial Court of Pakistan’ through a new Article 212A in the Constitution. Its stated aims — swift resolution of commercial disputes, stronger enforcement of arbitral awar­­ds, investor confidence, and Pakistan as a credible destination for capital — are exactly right, and I welcome the ambition. But as one who has spent years close to this work, I must say plainly that the court as proposed cannot fly. It will not fail for want of a fine building or good rules, but for three reasons that lie beneath it. The first is that our courts no longer command the legitimacy such a forum requires. The second is that the arbitration law it would sit above is archaic and unworkable. The third is that we have already built an overlapping forum, and it has failed. First: a court is only as trusted as the system around it. A court draws its power not from the statute that creates it but from the confidence of those who use it — the belief that its judges are independent and its outcomes cannot be arranged by whoever holds power outside the courtroom. That confidence has been shaken. The 26th Amend­ment of 2024 ended the convention by which the senior-most judge became chief justice and left judges a minority on the body that appoints them; the 27th Amendment of 2025 created a Federal Constitutional Court that absorbed the Supreme Court’s core jurisdiction. It is now widely percei­v­­ed, at home and abroad, that our courts have be­­come managed — and perception is the very thing on which investment turns. The foreign investor senses that absence long before a citizen does; his lawyers price it in and rank us low. He wants a neutral, swift forum; today we offer neither. A court draws its power not from the statute that creates it but from the confidence of those who use it. Consider who has understood this best — not the mature democracies but the Gulf monarchies. Dubai, Abu Dhabi and Qatar each walled their commercial courts off from the local order and handed the bench to eminent judges from across the common-law world. If absolute monarchies can guarantee total independence to their commercial courts, a common-law republic that will not has made a choice, and a poor one. I do not dismiss the grievance at home: our judges have not always been good, and our courts have too often failed to inspire confidence. But the cure for a court that strays is reform — accountability, transparency, openness — not capture. What has been done in the name of fixing the judiciary has set the country back many years. You cannot amend your way to credibility — least of all by another amendment, when it was amendment that did the damage. Second: the law it would sit above is a relic. When parties agree to arbitrate, they also choose a legal system to supervise them — the law of the seat. Choose London, Singapore or Dubai, and a modern, arbitration-friendly regime applies. Choose Pakistan, and you have chosen the Arbitration Act of 1940, a colonial statute enacted before this country existed. Under it an award is not final until a court makes it a ‘rule of the court’, which the court may modify, remit, or set aside on broad grounds; arbitration becomes litigation wrapped around it at both ends, slower and costlier than the lawsuit it was meant to replace. Nor does the international system save us: foreign awards, too, are enforced back through the same slow, unprioritised courts. And a commercial court succeeds everywhere by working hand in glove with arbitration; above a broken arbitration law, it opens onto a walled corridor. The remedy already exists. In April 2023 the then chief justice constituted an Arbitration Law Review Committee, which I chaired; within a year we produced a complete draft — the Arbitration Act, 2024 — modelled on the UNCITRAL Model Law. On May 2, 2024, we handed it to the Federal Minister for Law and Justice, urging its enactment. Two years on, it gathers dust while we live under the Act of 1940. Meanwhile the world has moved: Rwanda modernised its arbitration law in 2008, joined the New York Convention, built the Kigali arbitration centre, and climbed near the top of Africa’s ease-of-doing-business rankings; Nigeria and Mauritius did likewise. They grasped that the foundation must be laid before anything is built upon it. Third: we have already tried this — and it failed. We need not imagine what happens when we build on the wrong foundation, because we have done it. In 2023 the government created the Trade Dispute Resolution Commission — well funded and well housed — and it has produced next to nothing, because its own statute chains its arbitrations to the 1940 Act and its autonomy was folded into the Ministry of Commerce. There is even grave doubt whether it is constitutional at all, for it exercises plainly judicial power as an executive-controlled body, without the independence Article 175(3) demands. The new Inter­national Mediation and Arbitration Centre is no different — signboards that have decided nothing of consequence, and the world has ignored them. Budgets, buildings and hollow announcements do not make a forum; an independent ecosystem does. An international commercial court added to this landscape would be a third empty vessel, costlier than the first two and of no use. The order of repair So the order of repair is everything. There are two tasks, not of equal rank: the first and fundamental is the legitimacy of the courts; the second is the arbitration law. Put legitimacy right, and the reform finally works; pass that reform, or raise a gleaming new court, into a system the world distrusts, and you will have modernised a forum no one will enter. Restore the Supreme Court to its place; disband the constitutional court a common-law country does not need; enact the Arbitration Act, 2024; and protect judicial independence rather than buying it. Neutrality and the rule of law are not the ornaments of a democracy; they are its engine, and the reason capital feels safe. Until legitimacy is regained, the International Commercial Court will not fly. Let us first get our foundations right. Let us not fool ourselves. In 2007, the so-called ‘Lawyers’ Movement’ in Pakistan mobilised immense pressure against then military ruler Gen Pervez Musharraf to reinstate Chief Justice Iftikhar Chaudhry, whom Musharraf had arbitrarily dismissed. Backed by opposition parties, these protests catalysed a somewhat ‘fair’ 2008 general election, which brought the Pakistan Peoples Party (PPP) to power, closely followed by the Pakistan Muslim League-Nawaz (PML-N). He complied, signalling a victory for the protest movement. However, this success was short-lived. What followed was the reinstatement of a judge who began to practice overt judicial populism and hound the fragile PPP ruling coalition, alongside a military establishment led by Gen Pervez Kayani. In fact, some political commentators have suggested that Kayani had quietly allowed the Lawyers’ Movement to mushroom as a way to force Musharraf to hand over the post of army chief to him. The PPP coalition faced severe judicial populism from Chaudhry, constant bullying from Kayani’s military establishment, unprecedented terror attacks from Islamist militants and an opposition that jumped on these bandwagons. It was a chaotic period. This episode illustrates a classic example of how ‘horizontal movements’ are capable of removing a powerful political figure and even a government, yet almost always fail to produce structural change. Musharraf’s fall actually worsened matters because the problematic political and economic structures not only remained intact but deepened. Decentralised movements can mobilise millions without a central command, but their greatest strength can also become their greatest weakness. Once the common enemy disappears, what holds the movement together? Horizontal movements are decentralised forms of social and political activism that operate without a traditional hierarchical leadership. Unlike top-down organisations with a central command system, horizontal movements rely on spontaneous mobilisation, peer-to-peer collaboration and consensus-based decision-making. These movements often bring together diverse groups, ranging from professional associations and student outfits to ordinary citizens and opposition parties, united by a common grievance against an authority figure or regime. Their lack of a single leader makes them difficult to suppress, as there is no single figurehead to arrest or negotiate with. But according to the American sociologists F. Fox Piven and Richard Cloward, while horizontal movements can become effective catalysts for challenging authority and dismantling regimes, their decentralised nature introduces several disadvantages. These vulnerabilities often explain why such movements succeed in disruption but fail in reconstruction. In 2017, the political theorists Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri wrote that horizontal movements suffer from a lack of unified vision. Due to the fact that participants unite against a common grievance rather than a shared ideology, internal cracks quickly emerge once the shared enemy is removed, leaving groups deeply divided on how to govern. Such movements lack institutional depth. Mainstream political parties have chains of command and administrative experience, whereas horizontal movements struggle to transition from street-level protest to sustainable governance. This vacuum is often exploited by entrenched state institutions and governments. Yet, ever since the 1990s, horizontal movements have overtaken the concept of ‘vertical movements’ that rely on hierarchies, central party leadership and top-down command structures to effect social and political change. Historically, vertical movements have a better record of enforcing structural changes. Some general examples in this regard include anti-colonial independence struggles led by disciplined party structures and organised labour movements backed by union hierarchies. These demonstrated a much stronger capacity to build institutional machinery, seize state power and enforce lasting structural and legal changes.. Illustration by Abro Most political scientists posit that vertical movements have largely fallen out of favour due to a widespread disillusionment with traditional political parties, governments and labour unions, making people deeply sceptical of top-down leadership. The Spanish sociologist Manuel Castells believes that the rise of the internet, social media and encrypted communication platforms enables swift, decentralised coordination without requiring a central committee or formal organisation. Recent research suggests that horizontal structures are valued for being faster to form and more welcoming to diverse groups, as participants do not need to submit to party discipline or ideological doctrines. The barrier to participation is lower, making it much easier for individuals to join a spontaneous, decentralised protest or online campaign than to commit to the long-term, disciplined membership required by traditional vertical structures. Over the past two decades, nearly all major uprisings have been organised along horizontal lines. On the surface, these movements are frequently deemed successful and their impact receives sensationalist coverage in both mainstream and social media. Yet, the failure of horizontally organised movements to enact structural change is rarely scrutinised. While these movements have succeeded in removing unpopular leaders and, in some cases, entire governments, these victories merely create vacuums that are swiftly filled by entrenched powers, particularly when these powers successfully replicate and co-opt the tenor, slogans and even participants of the original movements. The Arab Spring uprisings are a case in point. Across several countries in the Middle East and North Africa, decentralised horizontal networks successfully toppled long-standing authoritarian rulers, such as in Tunisia and Egypt. However, because these movements lacked well-thought-out alternatives, apart from abstract demands and slogans, the ensuing political vacuums were initially filled by organised Islamist groups and then by entrenched military institutions that co-opted the rhetoric of the uprisings. In the United States, the left-leaning ‘Occupy Wall Street’ movement captured global attention and mainstream media coverage regarding economic inequality following the 2008 financial crisis. According to the American political scientist John Ehrenberg, since the movement was horizontal, it struggled to translate street-level momentum into lasting legislative policy, leaving existing financial and political structures entirely intact, while mainstream political parties later absorbed parts of its populist rhetoric for electoral campaigns. In the early 2010s, the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) in India and the Pakistan Tehreek-i-Insaf (PTI) in Pakistan also did exactly that. In developing countries, the expansion of the middle class is another factor that has popularised the growth of horizontal movements. Rising educational attainment, urbanisation and access to digital technology have created a population with higher expectations for governance, transparency and personal autonomy. Unlike traditional working-class movements tied to union hierarchies or rural-agrarian networks, this growing urban middle class in developing countries often feels alienated by supposedly fossilised political parties and state institutions. They prefer flexible, decentralised networks that reflect their modern interconnected lifestyles and allow them to mobilise around specific grievances without submitting to traditional party discipline. Their initiatives in this regard are as much political endeavours as they are performative and aesthetic. While these movements can, and often do, succeed in removing ministers and governments, when their participants are basking in the glow of victory, entrenched state institutions are often already filling the vacuum left unaddressed by horizontal movements. Structural and sustained institutional reconstruction demands a lot more energy and (offline) effort.  Middle-class horizontal movements seem to be hesitant in this regard.
