Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif on Wednesday arrived at the COP31 Antalya Leaders’ Summit in Turkiye, where he is set to highlight Pakistan’s climate priorities, along with other world leaders. Delegates from nearly 200 countries are meeting in the Mediterranean resort city of Antalya as global greenhouse gas emissions show little sign of falling and wars, inflation and other crises compete for political attention. The world leaders will begin their speeches in the afternoon and continue through the day on Thursday, according to the COP31 schedule. Ahead of the conference, PM Shehbaz arrived at the venue where he was received by Turkiye President Recep Tayyip Erdogan and UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres, Pakistan TV reported. World leaders, including PM Shehbaz, gathered for a group photograph at the summit, it added. Wednesday’s speakers are also expected to include Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, who was re-elected at the end of ​October, and whose country hosted last year’s climate talks. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, Chinese Vice President Han Zheng, and Marshall Islands ​President Hilda Heine will also speak on Wednesday. The United States, the world’s largest historical emitter, has skipped the talks for the second year in a ‌row after ⁠President Donald Trump — who has called global warming a hoax — withdrew the US from climate cooperation following his return to office in 2025. For his part, PM Shehbaz will highlight Pakistan’s climate priorities and emphasise the urgent need for enhanced global climate action and strengthened international cooperation to mitigate the adverse impacts of climate change, the Prime Minister’s Office (PMO) said following the premier’s arrival in Turkiye on Tuesday night. The Foreign Office (FO) has said the prime minister will also highlight “Pakistan’s steps towards rapid solarisation and expansion of its renewable energy capacity, as part of its transition towards a cleaner and more resilient economy, along with its efforts to strengthen disaster preparedness and early-warning systems”. He will call for predictable, adequate and accessible climate finance for developing countries to strengthen their resilience and pursue sustainable development, it added. PM Shehbaz will also address a high-level dialogue on clean energy transition and electrification convened by the COP31 presidency, titled “35 by 35: Powering the Future” and scheduled for 3pm local time (5pm Pakistan time). On the sidelines of the summit, the prime minister is scheduled to hold bilateral meetings with Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan and other world leaders attending COP31 to discuss matters of mutual interest and explore avenues for strengthening bilateral cooperation. According to the PMO, a “Pakistan Pavilion” has been established at the COP31 venue where 54 events will be hosted on various themes. ‘Difficult start’ Ahead of the conference, negotiators had been locked in discussions late on Sunday over demands by developing nations to include items on climate finance in the official agenda. “This has already been a difficult start for COP31,” said Jasper Inventor, head of Greenpeace’s COP31 delegation. Under UN climate agreements, developed countries, which are historically responsible for most greenhouse gas emissions, have provided financial support to help developing nations adapt to climate impacts and transition to cleaner energy. But poorer countries say the sums remain insufficient. Inventor said developed countries were “taking a hard line” over whether to include a platform, agreed at COP30 in Brazil last year, aimed at discussing how wealthy nations should provide funds to developing nations. African countries are also pushing for the agenda to include discussions on the tripling of adaptation finance, which aims to help poorer nations pay for measures such as building sea walls or planting drought-resistant crops. Observers say few major breakthroughs are expected at COP31 as countries grapple with an oil price shock and geopolitical tensions fuelled by the US war on Iran and Russia’s conflict in Ukraine. As negotiators tussled in Antalya, the UN’s World Meteorological Organisation released a report indicating that 2026 has continued a streak of exceptional global temperatures, melting ice and rising sea levels. The EU’s climate monitor warned last week that 2026 would most likely be the second-hottest year ever recorded, while scientists say 2027 could top the chart, fuelled by El Niño.

Asian Development Bank approves $500m loan to help Pakistan strengthen disaster preparedness

ISLAMABAD: The Asian Development Bank (ADB) on Wednesday approved contingent disaster financing (CDF) facilities up to $500 million for Pakistan to withstand disasters and facilitate disaster response. In a statement, the Manila-based lending agency said the funds will provide prompt budget support following eligible disaster events to help reduce the resulting economic and fiscal impacts. “Floods, earthquakes, and other disasters cause Pakistan an estimated $2 billion in annual average losses,” ADB noted, further recalling, “2022 floods alone caused $14.9bn in direct damage and $15.2bn in economic losses, pushing as many as $9.1m people into poverty.” “Poor and rural communities have less capacity to recover, while women and girls are disproportionately affected.” The lending agency said the second subprogramme of the Climate and Disaster Resilience Enhancement Programme supported reforms aimed at strengthening disaster preparedness and risk management, as well as integrating resilience measures into Pakistan’s development planning and investment, to reduce the impact of future disasters. “The programme also strengthens Pakistan’s disaster risk financing framework and improves the availability of financing following disaster events,” the statement added. “When disaster strikes, it is often the most vulnerable communities that are hit hardest. Investing in preparedness can help protect lives and livelihoods, reduce economic losses, and safeguard the development gains Pakistan has worked hard to achieve,” ADB Country Director for Pakistan Emma Fan was quoted as saying. “This programme will support the country in implementing critical reforms to ensure a more resilient future.” It added that the programme was in accordance with “Pakistan’s priorities for managing disaster risks and with ADB’s commitment to supporting resilience initiatives across Asia and the Pacific.” In 2024, ADB had also approved a $500m policy-based loan to support climate change and disaster risk reduction and resilience in Pakistan. In May, a report compiled by the think tank Jinnah Institute highlighted that persistent governance failures and weak community-level systems continued to undermine Pakistan’s ability to prepare for and respond to climate disasters and called for urgent reforms to build long-term resilience across vulnerable districts. The findings came in the wake of devastating 2025 floods, which submerged entire districts, displaced millions, and wiped out livelihoods, exposing critical gaps in preparedness despite years of policy frameworks, institutional reforms, and international commitments. The report argued that reliance on reactive disaster management has proven insufficient in the face of intensifying climate stress.

India pollution monitors overwhelmed by toxic smog

India’s air pollution monitors were overwhelmed this week by toxic smog so severe that concentrations exceeded their upper measurement limit, equivalent to 66 times the World Health Organisation’s recommended daily maximum, regulators said. The admission comes after opposition politicians accused authorities of deliberately allowing gaps in official readings for several hours overnight Sunday into Monday, just as pollution surged with a barrage of fireworks for Diwali, the Hindu festival of lights. New Delhi is one of the world’s most polluted capitals, where winter smog from vehicle emissions, farm fires and industry already chokes millions. But the missing readings meant official data failed to capture the full extent of the pollution at its worst. “The government which shuts down air quality monitoring stations on the night of Diwali, the government that does data fraud, how will you trust such a government?” Aam Aadmi Party (AAP) Delhi president Saurabh Bharadwaj said in a video posted on X. “If Diwali night’s PM2.5 (toxic particulates) is not in the record, who will answer for that air?” senior Congress party lawmaker Ajay Maken said in a social media post. The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) rejected accusations of deliberate under-reporting, saying the gaps reflected “differences in measurement of pollutant concentration” rather than an attempt to conceal pollution. ‘Extreme episodes’ Particulate matter analysers, installed when the National Air Quality Index (NAQI) was introduced in 2015, were calibrated to measure concentrations of up to 1,000 micrograms per cubic metre. The WHO recommends a daily maximum of 15 micrograms per cubic metre for PM2.5, fine particles small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs and linked to cancer. “The designed system of automated data validation treats hourly readings beyond the calibrated range as outside range, and such readings are not used in the computation,” the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) said in a statement late Tuesday. “During extreme episodes, which are occasional like Diwali firecracker bursting, this may appear as a data gap at the particular station concerned”, it added. Although choking clouds filled the streets early Monday, the scale of the pollution was only captured when monitors resumed reporting, around eight hours after the fireworks peaked. Readings then hit 879 micrograms per cubic metre in the Mandir Marg locality, according to monitoring organisation IQAir, which cited data from a city government station. That is more than 58 times the WHO daily limits. “There is one thing the people of Delhi can be certain of: the fact that the data has gone missing doesn’t mean the pollution has disappeared too,” Congress party lawmaker Pawan Khera added. “The poisonous smoke we have been inhaling… will continue to choke our lungs, threaten our lives and shorten our life expectancy”.

Hidden cause of economic imbalances

GROWING inequality is a serious economic and social issue. Internally, we tend to treat poverty and inequality as separate problems. They are closely connected but not identical. Poverty is about deprivation; inequality is about how income, wealth, opportunities, and economic power are dis­­tributed. An economy can grow and even red­u­­ce poverty temporarily while allowing wealth and privilege to become concentrated. Household data underlines the problem. The consumption-based Gini coefficient increased from 28.4 in 2018 –19 to 32.7 in 2024 –25. This figure does not capture the full extent of inequality in income and wealth, be­­cause household surveys tend to underrepresent the assets and incomes of the richest households. The distribution of income and wealth influences what people consume, what businesses produce, where investment flows, and how much a country imports. In Pakistan, a relatively small segment commands a disproportionately large share of purchasing power and assets, while much of the population struggles to meet basic needs. A relatively small segment commands a disproportionately large share of purchasing power and assets. The benefits of economic growth have been distributed unevenly. Expanding sectors such as banking, telecommunications, IT, energy, oil and gas require capital, technology, and specialised skills, favouring those who already possess financial resources or access to quality education. The state’s failure to provide decent education and healthcare has denied much of the population a fair opportunity to participate in these activities. Hence, economic disadvantages are passed from one generation to the next. Equally damaging is the continued protection of selected businesses from competition through high import duties, subsidies, tax exemptions, and special regulatory concessions. Powerful business groups have resisted competition, sometimes forming cartels to influence prices and markets. Profits earned through state protection rather than efficiency reward influence instead of innovation, raise costs for consumers, and weaken competitiveness. The tax system has reinforced these inequalities by treating similar incomes from different sources unequally. Salaried individuals face substantial income taxes, while earnings from property, capital gains, and certain commercial activities have historically enjoyed relatively favourable treatment. The state itself has been a major contributor to wealth concentration. Valuable public land and plots have been given to officials and influential individuals, while discretionary powers over building permissions, zoning regulations, and public contracts have created opportunities for private enrichment. Rising property prices, reinforced by speculative investment and the use of real estate to park undeclared wealth, have further increased the fortunes of existing asset owners. Corruption, smuggling, tax evasion, and manipulation of import and export prices have provided additional avenues for accumulating wealth, often beyond the reach of effective taxation and accountability. Together, these factors constitute a system in which public authority is used to transfer economic opportunities and resources to privileged groups. The consequences extend beyond unfair income distribution; they also distort how the economy allocates and uses its scarce resources. When fortunes can be made through political connections, regulatory concessions, and protection from competition, capital and entrepreneurial talent are diverted towards securing favours rather than productive investment. It becomes more profitable to lobby for higher import duties or special exemptions than to invest in technology, improve quality, or develop export markets. The concentration of purchasing power also shapes the structure of domestic demand and, therefore, what businesses choose to produce. Businesses often respond to this purchasing power by directing investment towards activities serving a narrow market. Meanwhile, the limited purchasing power of the majority constrains demand for mass-consumption goods, discouraging investment in industries that could serve a broader domestic market, create jobs, and compete globally. This distorted pattern of consumption and production also contributes to our recurring external imbalances. The concentration of disposable income generates demand for imported vehicles, electronics, luxury goods, foreign travel, and education abroad, adding to the foreign exchange needed for machinery, fuel, technology, and industrial inputs. Not all imports are undesirable. Many are essential for productivity and exports. However, our consumption and production structures generate substantial demand for foreign exchange without a corresponding capacity to earn it through competitive exports. Remittances from overseas Pakistanis have helped bridge this gap. But however valuable these inflows, they cannot substitute indefinitely for an economy capable of financing its import requirements through its own export earnings. Inequality is certainly not the sole cause of external deficits. Exchange rate policies, tariffs, energy costs, inflation, low productivity, and weak export competitiveness also matter. Still, the concentration of purchasing power helps explain why import demand remains substantial even when we face severe foreign exchange constraints. There is another damaging effect. When influential groups secure tax exemptions, subsidies, and protected markets, government revenues suffer. The burden of financing an oversized, extractive state, with its extensive controls and perks and privileges, consequently falls disproportionately on salaried workers, compliant businesses, and ordinary consumers unable to escape taxation. Public expenditure compounds the problem. Chronic underinvestment in quality education and healthcare has denied millions of children a fair start. Affluent households can purchase good private education and medical care, while poorer families depend on poor and overstretched public services. Their children’s prospects depend on family circumstances. The failure to provide equal opportunities thereby perpetuates inequality across generations. Addressing these disparities does not mean pe­­nalising wealth creation. It means distinguishing wealth earned through enterprise, innovation, and productivity from fortunes accumulated through political connections, monopolies, and state-gra­nted privileges. This requires a fairer tax system. Protection must give way to competition, while discretionary allocations of public land, contracts, and regulatory concessions need to be replaced by transparent, rule-based processes. Foreign aid, remittances, and favourable global developments have provided only temporary relief, enabling successive governments to shelve tough reforms, thereby sustaining an economic structure that rewards privilege over productivity. Unless this system of privileges and unequal opportunities is dismantled, Pakistan will remain trapped in recurring internal and external imbalances, regardless of how many stabilisation programmes it negotiates with the IMF and others. • Says no public money lost in procurement controversy • Claims Rs183 million collected from applicants remains in Nadra’s account ISLAMABAD: Amid allegations of irregularities in vaccine procurement, Federal Health Minister Mustafa Kamal on Tuesday claimed that not a single rupee of public money had been lost in the controversy surrounding the procurement of vaccines for Umrah pilgrims. He said a private company had purchased the vaccines, while the entire amount collected for the purpose, amounting to Rs183 million, remained deposited in a National Database and Registration Authority (Nadra) account. Addressing a press conference, the minister said he wanted to present the facts before the nation following a week-long controversy over the vaccine procurement process. He said the health ministry would upload the inquiry report on its website to ensure transparency. It may be recalled that Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif had ordered an inquiry into the procurement of meningococcal meningitis and influenza vaccines by Border Health Services-Pakistan (BHS) under a public-private partnership (PPP) arrangement for pilgrims and overseas job seekers. It was alleged that the previous vaccination arrangement involved a cost of around Rs1,250 per dose per person, whereas the amount under the new arrangement was approximately Rs4,500 per pilgrim. The prime minister had constituted a five-member committee, headed by the secretary of the Cabinet Division, to examine the procurement process and determine whether it complied with the relevant rules and procedures. Mr Kamal explained that BHS had approached the ministry regarding vaccination requirements for people travelling abroad for Umrah. The ministry constituted a procurement committee on May 9 and also established a grievance committee, headed by the director general of health, to address complaints. However, he claimed that BHS officials subsequently formed their own committees, including procurement and appellate committees, bypassing the ministry’s arrangements and violating prescribed procedures. The minister said the inquiry found that BHS officials had bypassed the Ministry of Health and violated Public Procurement Regulatory Authority (PPRA) rules. Nadra was also brought into the process through a public-private partnership arrangement, with payments deposited into its account. He said the inquiry held BHS officials, members of the procurement committee and a subsequent director responsible for endorsing the process. He added that the ministry had implemented all the recommendations of the inquiry committee and would constitute a new procurement committee. Mr Kamal said the Prime Minister’s Office had also directed the Federal Investigation Agency (FIA) to take action, following which the officials concerned were arrested and sent to jail. Rejecting social media claims of Rs10 billion in corruption, he said the Rs183 million collected from applicants remained in Nadra’s account. Neither the government nor the company supplying the vaccines had received any payment, he added. He claimed that the government had suffered no financial loss, although the vaccine supplier had incurred losses after procuring the vaccines while the collected amount remained deposited in Nadra’s account. Pakistan pushing for diplomacy amid global tensions

• Strength at home and effective diplomacy abroad key to confronting emerging world order, Dar tells Margalla Dialogue • University of Chicago scholar says US can’t win Iran war; claims Washington complicit in Palestinian ‘genocide’ by Israel • Urges Pakistan to capitalise on ties with both US and China, shore up its ‘middle power’ status ISLAMABAD: Calling for stronger diplomacy, dialogue and multilateral cooperation to address growing global tensions, Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar here on Tuesday stressed that Pakistan would continue to work for regional peace, while safeguarding its sovereignty and national interests. Addressing the Margalla Dialogue 2026, organised by the Islamabad Policy Research Institute (IPRI), he highlighted that the shape of the emerging world order remains uncertain. “The choices countries make during this transition will help determine whether it produces greater cooperation or deeper confrontation,” he said. “The international security architecture faces mounting pressure. Intensifying great power rivalries, protracted conflicts and erosion of arms control agreements are increasing the risks of confrontation and miscalculation,” he noted, addressing the theme of this year’s dialogue, ‘Global Order in Transition: Navigating Strategic Uncertainty’. Posing the question “how can we safeguard our security and build a more prosperous future for our people?”, Mr Dar maintained that the answer lies in “strength at home and effective diplomacy abroad”. He noted that the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement with Saudi Arabia and Turkiye, was a defensive arrangement aimed at strengthening collective security and credible deterrence, rather than targeting any particular country. Discussing Pakistan’s international partnerships, he described relations with China as a cornerstone of the country’s foreign policy, while highlighting constructive engagement with the United States and expanding cooperation with Saudi Arabia, Turkiye, Iran, Gulf countries, Europe and Central Asia. He reiterated that Pakistan sought peace but would firmly defend its sovereignty, stressing that a just settlement of the Jammu and Kashmir dispute in accordance with UN Secu­rity Council resolutions was essential for lasting peace in the region. He also warned against Indian attempts to weaponise water, adding that Pakistan would safeguard its rights under the Indus Waters Treaty. Mr Dar also classified terrorism emanating from Afghan territory as a serious security challenge, adding that Pakistan would continue protecting its people while supporting a peaceful, stable and economically integrated Afghanistan. With regard to the Middle East, the foreign minister said Pakistan’s facilitation of the Islamabad talks between the United States and Iran in April had reflected its commitment to resolving conflicts through dialogue. Other panel discussions at the event also featured noted national and international speakers. In his keynote address during a discussion on ‘Geopolitics and the Realignment of Global Power’, Prof John J. He categorically said that Israel was committing genocide in Gaza as a means to driving Palestinians from their own territory. “That is why they are treating the Palest­in­i­ans in Gaza today in the most horrendous ways… and launching pogroms against Palestinians in the (occupied) West Bank,” he said, adding that despite their brutal methods, they had failed to achieve their objective of driving the Palestinians out. He was quite clear that Israel could not have committed this genocide without US assistance, adding that Washington was complicit in this crime, providing diplomatic support to Israel and vetoing six UN Security Council resolutions designed to shut down the genocide. Talking about the contours of great power politics, he said that the world has shifted from a unipolar world into a multipolar world. “There are now three great powers in the world: the US, China and Russia,” he said, adding that the dynamics of a unipolar world were very different from the dynamics of a multipolar world. Noting that nothing is going to change in the relationship between Pakistan and India, Prof Mearsheimer said that India could not become a great power in the near future, as it is plagued by too many issues, though it could become a formidable middle power, like Japan. Pakistan, he said, needed to strive for wealth creation to become a middle power like Germany and Japan. Pakistan, he said, was in an ideal position in the multipolar world, as it has cordial ties with both the US and China, and hasn’t had to choose between the two. As long as the wars in Ukraine and the Middle East persist, the US will continue to pivot away from East Asia, he said, noting how nice Trump is to Xi Jinping because he doesn’t want to antagonise Beijing. Noted Middle East scholar Dr Vali Nasr lauded the resilience and tactics of Iran that had led to a failure of US and Israeli policies. However, he maintained that Israel had an expansionist agenda and the war between Israel and Iran was still unfinished, adding that a military alliance between India, UAE and Israel could pose a threat to Pakistan. He added that US involvement in Iran and Ukraine had led to softening its military presence against China in the Far East, and soon all­ies like Japan and South Korea too will start reconsidering their age-old reliance on the US for security. The actions and statements of President Trump have prompted demands from Iran to quit the Nuclear Non Proliferation Treaty, he said. Former US diplomat Robin Raphel noted that if Iran leaves the NPT, it could trigger exits by other countries too. Second Pakistan-Sri Lanka Test shifted from Lahore to Rawalpindi over smog

The Pakistan Cricket Board (PCB) on Tuesday shifted the second Test between Pakistan and Sri Lanka from Lahore to Rawalpindi amid worsening smog conditions in the Punjab capital. According to an official announcement issued by the PCB, the match, originally scheduled to be played in Lahore from November 17 to 21, will now take place at Rawalpindi Cricket Stadium on the same dates. “The second Test match between Pakistan and Sri Lanka, originally scheduled to be played in Lahore from November 17 to 21, has been relocated to Rawalpindi due to prevailing weather conditions,” PCB spokesperson Amir Mir said in a post on X. He said the decision was taken in view of increasing smog levels in Lahore and concerns over deteriorating air quality. It may be mentioned that Lahore has been grappling with severe smog for the past 10 days, with the city’s Air Quality Index (AQI) reportedly touching 600, placing it among the world’s most polluted cities. The venue change means both Tests of the two-match series will now be played at Rawalpindi Cricket Stadium. The first Test is currently under way at the same venue. Pakistan declared their first innings at 434 for nine on the second day, with Saud Shakeel top-scoring with 93. Sri Lanka were 114 for three in their second innings when bad light stopped play. Sri Lanka have been touring Pakistan since early October. Pakistan won the three-match T20I series 2-1 before claiming the ODI tri-series, which also featured England. The ongoing Test series is part of the ICC World Test Championship 2025-27 cycle.
