To be a Karachi Bugti who yearns for watan and dry wood

Dawn breaks over Ghaggar Phatak on the edge of Karachi. In a cluster of huts stitched together from rough timber, patched canvas and weathered cloth, the morning begins much as it once did hundreds of kilometres away in the hills of Dera Bugti. Inside an otaak, sixty-five-year-old Meerdali Bugti sits on a worn charpai with a faded rilli quilt. Beside the bed stands a recycled cooking-oil tin filled with drinking water, covered with cloth to keep it cool. Everything inside the hut has its place. It is a careful attempt to preserve a way of life. Each morning is a memory. The crowing rooster carries him back to his village of Doi, where dawn arrived over green valleys and clear springs, and where the day began with birdsong instead of diesel engines. Then Karachi stirs around him. Motorcycles rip down the road. Trucks grumble past. Construction machinery pounds. After Fajr prayers, he picks up a shovel and walks to a farm, where he spends the day coaxing water through irrigation channels. The work feeds his family. It does not quiet his thoughts. “There is too much noise here,” he says. “Where I came from, there was peace. The air had its own fragrance. Life flowed through our springs. The mountains spoke to us. The flowers, the leaves, the lakes, everything was alive.” He pauses as another truck rumbles past, overtaking his voice. “Now I am a man without a homeland.” This August marks twenty years since Meerdali left Dera Bugti because Nawab Akbar Khan Bugti was killed in a military operation in their mountains. He believed he would return within days. Instead, those days became years and like thousands of other Bugti tribesmen and their families, he rebuilt his life in scattered settlements across Sindh. The anniversary of August 26, 2006 will revive political debates over the conflict but for Meerdali that date marks something far more personal, the moment they became strangers to their own homeland. The conflict between the center and Baloch nationalists long predates 2006. Since Pakistan’s creation, Balochistan has witnessed successive insurgencies, each ending militarily but leaving behind political grievances. Yet the insurgency that intensified in the early 2000s proved different. It spread across the province, drew in a younger generation of educated Baloch, and displaced entire communities whose stories were rarely recorded. For the people of Dera Bugti, the fighting reached a breaking point in 2005 and 2006. As clashes intensified, Nawab Akbar Khan Bugti left his fort for the surrounding mountains. His departure signaled that ordinary life was no longer possible so families began leaving and villages steadily emptied. When Bugti was killed, thousands had already fled, and many others followed in the months after. A fact-finding mission by the Human Rights Commission of Pakistan estimated that at the height of the fighting, nearly eighty-five per cent of Dera Bugti’s population of around 182,000 people had been displaced. No official record tracks what became of them. There were no displacement camps, registers or programmes to help them rebuild or resettle. Families drifted across Sindh and Punjab, finding work wherever they could, on farms, in quarries, at kilns and factories. Meerdali remembers the day he left. “Dera Bugti was under siege,” he says. “Food had stopped coming in. Shelling was happening everywhere. When the Nawab left for the mountains, ordinary people had no reason to stay.” There was no time to decide what to take. “We left wearing only the clothes on our backs.” Everything else stayed behind. The roads filled before dawn. Children were loaded onto donkeys. Others climbed into stuffed trucks. Many simply started walking, following dry riverbeds and tracks through the hills to get away from the shelling. “We thought we’d return in a few days,” Meerdali says. “Who leaves their home forever?” He smiles faintly at the thought, as though surprised by his own innocence. No one locked their doors. No one wrapped family heirlooms in cloth. For days they travelled south with whatever little flour and dried jujube they could carry. Every rumor of a safe road drew more people onto the same path. Every report of shelling forced them to change direction. A few villages away, in Pir Koh, Mahmood Bugti remembers the same journey differently. Before speaking about the road, he counts what stayed behind. “One hundred and forty sheep and goats,” he says. “Ten cows. Chickens.” He pauses after each number, as though taking stock. These animals had sustained the family for generations with milk, butter and meat. They could be sold when money was scarce. They were inheritance as much as livelihood. “The hardest part wasn’t the journey,” he says. “It was leaving my homeland and my livelihood. Life has brought poverty. It has brought illness. But nothing has hurt me as much as being forced to leave.” Like Meerdali, he walked for two days before finding transport south. The farther they travelled, the more unfamiliar life became. “Back home,” he says, “we hardly needed money. Our land and our animals gave us everything.” Here, he says, everything carries a price. Bread. Water. Medicine. For many, the journey ended wherever work could be found. For Sultan Bugti, it never ended at all. When his family fled Loop Sehra under the protection of a military convoy, they reached Kashmore first. Then Khairpur. Then Jamshoro. Eventually they settled on a rocky hillside in Gadap, on Karachi’s northern edge, where thirteen displaced Bugti families now live among piles of broken stone. Every few years, another move follows. “As long as this mountain gives us stone, we stay,” Sultan says. Men chip away at the rock in the afternoon sun, their hammers ringing in the hillside. “The day it stops, we’ll move somewhere else.” The rhythm of life that revolved around livestock and land has been replaced by the uncertainty of daily wages. Before sunrise, men leave the settlement carrying hammers, shovels and pickaxes. Some know where they will work. Others walk towards the roadside hoping someone will need an extra pair of hands. By evening they return with whatever the day has offered, sometimes a thousand rupees, sometimes nothing at all. A few kilometres away, in Ghaggar Phatak, Alam Deen Bugti prepares for another day behind the wheel of a rented qingchi rickshaw. Before earning anything for himself, he must first hand a thousand rupees to its owner. Fuel comes next. Whatever remains feeds his family. When he arrived in Karachi nearly twenty years ago, he believed the city would offer opportunities that the mountains never could. Instead, it kept asking questions he did not know how to answer. “They asked whether I was educated,” he says. He wasn’t. “They asked whether I had any skills.” He smiles, not with embarrassment but resignation. The only skills he possessed were those his homeland had taught him, raising livestock, farming, reading the land. They had sustained his family for generations. In Karachi, they counted for little. For years he broke stones in quarries scattered across Sindh before renting a qingchi. Now he spends twelve to fourteen hours weaving through Karachi’s traffic, chasing enough passengers to make the day worthwhile. Beyond Karachi’s last paved roads, the settlements almost disappear into the landscape. At Ghaggar Phatak, a handful of huts stand shoulder to shoulder, built from timber, canvas and sheets of plastic held together by rope. Chickens scratch at the ground. Goats wander with indistinguishable purpose. Women bake bread over open fires and the children chase one another in the dust. A pipeline carrying water from Keenjhar Lake runs beside the settlement. The families fetch water from it in plastic containers. In Gadap, the hillside tells a similar story. The huts blend into the colour of the mountain above the quarry where most of the men work. They are built to be dismantled as easily as they are erected. Everyone knows the arrangement is temporary. But one structure stands apart. It is always the otaak. As evening arrives, the men drift towards the otaak without speaking. Dust is washed from hands and faces. Someone pours water into a steel cup and passes it around. Conversation gathers on its own. One man asks another how the day went. That is called Haal-hawal. The answer is never short. Stories move slowly around the otaak. One memory gives way to another. Someone recalls a wedding in Dera Bugti that lasted three days. Another remembers the spring where the village drew its water. Laughter arrives unexpectedly, followed just as quickly by silence. Meerdali remembers a time when the otaak was never empty, when relatives arrived without notice and neighbours stayed long after sunset. Weddings stretched over days, announced by the beat of drums as hereditary Domb musicians walked from village to village. “Back then,” he says, “people belonged to one another.” Memory has become its own geography. The older men remember the taste of the water, the scent of rain on earth, the paths their animals followed through the hills. They argue over distances between villages, laugh about weddings that lasted for days, and recall neighbors by name. That landscape survives because they continue to speak it aloud. Their children will inherit something different. Jan Mir Bugti is one man who cannot remember the day his family left Dera Bugti because he was three years old. His memories are of moving from one stone quarry to another. School was always postponed. Now at 23, he loads crushed stone onto dumper trucks to feed thirteen people. He wants his children to go to school. A few kilometres away, another child carries a different inheritance altogether. Ali Khan Bugti is fourteen and has never seen Dera Bugti. He was born after his parents fled, in Sanghar. But when you ask him where he is from, he answers without hesitation. “Balochistan.” He has never walked beside its springs or climbed its hills. Yet he speaks of returning with the confidence of someone describing a place he has only just left. “One day,” he says, pointing beyond Karachi’s expanding edge, “I will go back.” For Meerdali, home is a place he can still describe in remarkable detail. For Jan Mir, it is something that disappeared before he was old enough to remember. For Ali, it exists almost entirely in imagination. Three generations. Three different relationships with the same place. Yet each speaks of Dera Bugti as though it still waits for them. Not everything the families carried from Dera Bugti could be counted in heads or acres. Nadi Boojala Mangal unwraps a hand-embroidered cloth case with care. Inside rests his Nar-the wooden flute that he carried since he left his village of Kargaz Peshi in 2006. “My land stayed behind,” he says. “My art did not.” The flute travelled with him to Rohri, Sukkur, Sehwan Sharif and finally, Karachi. It belonged to his father, and grandfather before that. As a boy, Nadi learned the Nar-Sur tradition by ear. By playing until the melodies became memory. He raises it to his lips. The first note wafts up. Children stop chasing one another. The men go silent. The melody is a Dadari Sur, neither loud nor dramatic. “There is a saying in Balochi,” Nadi says at last. “Wai watan, hushkein daar.” Alas, homeland and dry wood. All photos by author

4 women from GB successfully summit Spantik

He said weather conditions had been favourable at the peak before Friday, but subsequent deterioration created difficulties for rescue and recovery operations. The expedition also featured Alexandra Visan of Romania, who, despite battling cancer, successfully climbed Spantik and descended safely. Her expedition was organised by Broad Boy Adventures, owned by Pakistani mountaineer Shehroze Kashif. Fixing agriculture

PRIME Minister Shehbaz Sharif’s renewed push to strengthen agricultural research, provide quality seeds to smallholders and medium-scale farmers, and improve crop estimate systems could help address some of the sector’s long-standing weaknesses in farm productivity. But good intentions alone are not enough. The real question is whether the government can turn his directives into a coherent policy and implement it effectively. That would require reforming federal and provincial agriculture policies, markets and institutions that determine what farmers produce, what they pay for inputs and what they earn. Above all, an agricultural policy must be responsive to the needs of small and medium-sized farmers rather than powerful lobbies. Take the example of the sugar sector. Policy has repeatedly been designed around the interests of millers, who have traditionally enjoyed political influence. Decisions on support prices, cane procurement, export, and other interventions aim to protect them. Cotton illustrates another failure of the agricultural system. Farmers often do not receive a fair share of the value generated by their crop, with middlemen and other parts of the supply chain capturing much of the margin. The result is reduced yields over the years. Improving farm productivity means little if farmers remain trapped in inefficient markets and weak bargaining arrangements. The policy would not, however, cover goods subject to international sanctions binding on Pakistan or those listed in the Negative List of the Import Policy Order, 2022. The policy covers domestic sales to oil marketing companies (OMCs) and refineries. Foreign suppliers would be able to maintain bonded inventories for local sale at private and public bonded storage terminals, including dedicated storage terminals, at approved locations such as Port Qasim Authority, KPT/Keamari, Hub and Gwadar Port, as well as any other designated ports, including Mahmood Kot and Machike, Sheikhupura. For imports into bonded storage and re-export, both private and public bonded storage terminals at ports would be approved locations, including those at Port Qasim Authority, KPT/Keamari, Hub and Gwadar Port, as well as any other designated ports, subject to compliance with the relevant laws and regulations of the respective port, marine and other regulatory authorities. Foreign suppliers, through their consignees, would have access to the national petroleum pipeline network to move bonded inventory from approved port-based locations to inland locations — such as Mahmood Kot and Machike, Sheikhupura — for local sale to licensed OMCs and refineries. No duty or tax would be triggered by such bonded pipeline movements, while goods declaration (GD) filing requirements would apply. These guidelines will not affect or alter the existing regime for imports of petroleum products by licensed OMCs and refineries on their own account. The existing OMC import regime will continue unchanged and in parallel with this initiative. Foreign suppliers, through their consignees, will be allowed to maintain inventories of crude oil and other aforementioned products in private and public bonded storage facilities, including dedicated storage terminals. Meanwhile, foreign suppliers or their consignees will have the option to develop their own dedicated storage infrastructure or utilise private or public bonded warehouse facilities and dedicated storage terminals, subject to the relevant regulatory approvals under the Customs Act, 1969 and from the relevant port authorities. The consignee’s bonded storage facility — whether a public bonded warehouse, private bonded warehouse or dedicated storage terminal — will have to be licensed by Customs after fulfilling the requirements for operating as a bonded warehouse. For domestic sales of bonded goods to OMCs, all sales tax obligations — including registration as an importer under the Sales Tax Act, 1990, filing of returns and payment of sales tax — would apply to and rest solely with the OMC or refinery as the importer of record at ex-bonding. The foreign supplier and the consignee would have no sales tax registration requirement, return-filing obligation or liability to pay sales tax in respect of domestic sales. Under the policy guidelines, concessions would be granted to the consignee and the foreign supplier to ensure that they remain tax-neutral in Pakistan in respect of bonded storage, blending, trading and re-export operations. At the time of storing goods received under the scheme, consignees would not be required to file an Electronic Import Form (EIF) with their GD for in-bonding. The consignee may sell bonded goods to licensed purchasers in foreign currency, with the letter of credit or open contract in the name of the foreign supplier and payment made directly into its foreign bank account through an authorised dealer, in accordance with applicable foreign exchange regulations. Moreover, the sale and purchase of goods between the foreign supplier and Pakistani purchasers would be conducted on a purely commercial basis, without any liability on the part of the government. Upon the domestic sale of bonded goods, the consignee shall issue a no-objection certificate for the change of ownership. The OMC or refinery shall file the ex-bond GD, submit the Electronic Import Form through its designated scheduled bank, and pay all applicable customs duty, sales tax and other charges at the rates applicable at the time of ex-bonding. The FBR shall allow the movement of duty-paid products through pipelines or any other mode of transportation and facilitate local purchasers in making input and output tax adjustments. This policy will not restrict or alter the import rights of OMCs and refineries under the Product Review Meeting (PRM) framework. Upon importation into Pakistan and any subsequent movement between bonded warehouses without a sale, the consignee shall file the in-bond GD. Upon sale to a local purchaser, the purchaser shall immediately file the relevant ex-bond GD. Regarding pricing, Ogra-regulated prices will apply solely to the onward sale of petroleum products in the country by purchasers from bonded storage and will not restrict the international oil supplier’s unconditional right to re-export bonded goods at any time. Therefore, pricing obligations under Ogra notifications will apply to local purchasers and not to the foreign supplier or the consignee. Foreign suppliers will retain full pricing flexibility to sell bonded petroleum products to local OMCs and refineries at commercially negotiated prices. Such prices will not be subject to Ogra price notifications. The customs value of imported goods, for the purposes of assessing applicable customs duties, taxes, levies and other statutory charges, shall be the transaction value prevailing at the time of sale to the local purchaser in Pakistan. For the avoidance of doubt, if the sale by the consignee and ex-bonding by the local purchaser occur simultaneously, the transaction value applicable on the date of filing the ex-bond GD shall apply. The government will have the option to requisition bonded products in an emergency, but only where the government has officially declared a formal emergency, including war, armed conflict, a major natural disaster or a complete and documented collapse of domestic supply. Emergency requisitioning would not apply to routine energy shortages, price fluctuations, geopolitical events that do not cause supply disruptions or government procurement preferences. The government will have the right to request access to the consignee’s bonded petroleum stocks physically present at all approved bonded storage locations at the time of the request. In such cases, requisitioned goods shall be compensated at the prevailing international market price on the date of requisition, determined by reference to the weekly average of the published Platts price assessment for the relevant product and delivery point. Payment shall be made in any foreign currency within 15 calendar days of delivery. The government shall submit a formal requisition notice specifying the volume, product and delivery point. ISLAMABAD: Global ratings agency Moody’s on Monday upgraded Pakistan’s sovereign credit rating to B3 from Caa1 and maintained a stable outlook, citing expectations of improvements in governance and easing external and fiscal vulnerabilities. “The upgrade to B3 reflects our expectations that improvements in governance will allow the government to sustain the recent improvements in the country’s external position and strengthen fiscal metrics,” Moody’s said in an announcement. Pakistan’s external vulnerability risks have eased further since its last rating action in August 2025 at Caa1, with foreign exchange reserves building steadily, supported by sustained macroeconomic stabilisation, it said. At the same time, lower domestic financing costs amid monetary easing and an improved fiscal position have driven a material improvement in Pakistan’s debt affordability, the US-based rating firms stated, adding that Pakistan’s strengthening credit profile is also demonstrating greater resilience to external shocks than in previous cycles, including the ongoing Middle East conflict. However, it warned that notwithstanding these improvements, Pakistan’s credit profile remained vulnerable due to a structurally fragile external position, weak debt affordability, a still relatively narrow revenue base and constraints on attracting investment and stimulating high-productivity and economic growth. These credit constraints are embedded in the B3 rating. The stable outlook balances a potentially faster improvement in Pakistan’s credit fundamentals against outstanding risks related to the vulnerabilities above, which, if materialised, could weaken access to foreign-currency financing and further reduce fiscal flexibility. The upgrade to B3 from Caa1 also applies to the backed foreign currency senior unsecured ratings for The Pakistan Global Sukuk Programme Co Ltd, the agency said. The associated payment obligations are direct obligations of the Pakistan’s government. The outlook for Pakistan Global Sukuk Programme Co Ltd remains stable, it said. Concurrent to today’s action, Moody’s also raised Pakistan’s local and foreign currency country ceilings to B1 and B3, from B2 and Caa1 respectively. The two-notch gap between the local currency ceiling and sovereign rating is driven by the government’s relatively large footprint in the economy, weak institutions, and high political and external vulnerability risk. The two-notch gap between the foreign currency ceiling and the local currency ceiling reflects incomplete capital account convertibility and relatively weak policy effectiveness. It also takes into account risks of transfer and convertibility restrictions being imposed. The rating agency noted that foreign exchange reserves increased to about $17 billion at end-July 2026, from $14bn in end-July 2025, sufficient to cover nearly three months of imports. As such, Pakistan’s External Vulnerability Indicator (the ratio of short-term and long-term maturing debt to foreign exchange reserves) has improved to about 145 per cent in 2026, compared to 230pc in 2025. Continued implementation of the IMF-supported reform programme has strengthened policy credibility, maintained macroeconomic stabilisation and underpinned financing from official creditors. Pakistan has also regained gradual access to market financing, including a three-year, $750 million Eurobond issued in April 2026 and a CNY 1.75bn (about $250m) debut Panda bond in May 2026. Together, these developments have enabled an accumulation of reserves while allowing Pakistan to meet all of its external obligations in FY26. Moody’s expected foreign exchange reserves to rise to about $19-20bn at the end of fiscal 2027 and $20-21bn in FY28, subject to the government’s sustained progress on the IMF programme, enabling timely disbursements from official partners and continued gradual access to market financing. In turn, continued adherence to the International Monetary Fund (IMF) programme would allow Pakistan to meet its external financing needs of about $21bn in fiscal 2027 and around $30bn in fiscal 2028, according to IMF estimates, while supporting continued reserves accumulation. About $7bn and $12bn of financing requirements in FY27 and FY28, respectively, comprise existing bilateral deposits, expected to be rolled over. Yet, Pakistan’s external position remains vulnerable to shocks, particularly given still-large external financing requirements, it noted. However, the projected reserve accumulation provides a larger buffer than in recent years against adverse market or commodity-price developments, including the elevated oil prices associated with the ongoing Middle East conflict, the statement said. Pakistan’s debt affordability has also improved materially, from very weak levels. Interest payments absorbed about 35pc of government revenue in fiscal 2026, down sharply from 49pc in fiscal 2025. The rating agency expected the recent improvement in Pakistan’s debt affordability to be durable, underpinned by sustained macroeconomic stability. While inflation remains sensitive to exchange-rate movements and external shocks, improved external buffers, a more stable macroeconomic environment and the authorities’ commitment to fiscal consolidation should help contain inflationary pressures and support debt affordability gains. Although higher global energy prices stemming from geopolitical tensions pose upside risks to inflation, Moody’s expected Pakistan’s strengthened policy framework and improved economic resilience to mitigate their impact.

In meeting with IMO chief, Dar calls for 'urgent intervention' for return of Pakistani sailors held by Somali pirates

Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar met the International Maritime Organisation’s head and called for “urgent and effective institutional intervention” for the return of 10 Pakistani sailors held captive by Somali pirates, according to a statement issued by the Foreign Office (FO). Dar met IMO Secretary General Arsenio Dominguez in London, where he is on a four-day visit. He also thanked Dominguez for his support for the ongoing efforts to secure the safe release of the 10 Pakistani seafarers, the FO statement said. The seafarers were among the crew of MT Honour 25 — a Palau-flagged oil tanker — that was hijacked by armed pirates off the coast of Puntland, Somalia on April 21, 2026. Four months later, there has been no progress either in negotiations or the payment of the ransom, said to be around $3 million, for the recovery of the hostages, despite concerns abounding about their declining health. Since the hijacking, the crew has made several emotional videos and audio appeals, while reporting that they have run out of clean water, food and medicine. The hostages are victims of an “ownership crisis”, as it is not clearly established who owns the ship, and the man previously thought to be the owner was later revealed to be more likely a middleman who hired them. Besides 10 Pakistanis, the crew members also include seven Indonesians, an Indian and a Sri Lankan. Dar reaffirms Pakistan commitment to IMO In his meeting with Dominguez, Dar also reaffirmed Pakistan’s “longstanding commitment” to IMO and appreciated its role as the principal international regulatory body for safe, secure and efficient international shipping, the FO statement said. He highlighted Pakistan’s continued efforts to strengthen maritime governance, implement international maritime standards, and further develop its blue economy, the statement added. The two sides also discussed further strengthening Pakistan-IMO cooperation in maritime safety, security, regulatory reforms, and the development of Pakistan’s maritime sector, according to the FO. Dar reached the United Kingdom on Sunday, ahead of a four-day official visit that began today. FO said that during the visit, Dar would meet UK Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband and Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) Minister of State for Pakistan Stephen Doughty, as well as other senior members of the government. He is also expected to meet British parliamentarians and representatives of the Pakistani diaspora in the UK. The visit comes after the two countries agreed to intensify bilateral trade engagement and strengthen business-to-business cooperation in July. The two sides had explored upgrading an existing Trade Dialogue into a more structured economic and trade framework.
