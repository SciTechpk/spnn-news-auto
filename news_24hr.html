Pakistan greenlights $200m ADB loan despite questions over digitalisation project

ISLAMABAD: Pakistan on Friday greenlit another $200 million (Rs57bn) loan from the Asian Development Bank (ADB) for digitalisation, despite serious questions raised by the Planning Commission. The project was one of seven projects worth Rs116bn cleared by the Central Development Working Party (CDWP) at a meeting presided over by Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal. He conditionally cleared the Rs57bn Transforming and Digitalising Revenue Administration (TADRA) project, which aims to increase the tax-to-GDP ratio to 13.5pc by 2029 from 11.1pc at present. The approval comes as the country has already utilised more than $4.7bn in foreign loans aimed at improving revenue collection, while the tax-to-GDP ratio continues to hover around 11pc. The planning minister “recommended the project to the Executive Committee of the National Economic Council (Ecnec), with the condition that the Pakistan Institute of Development Economics (PIDE), a state-owned think tank, thoroughly review its business model”, an official statement said. It was placed on record that PIDE had already conducted a study on loans taken by the government to reform the energy sector and tax administration, “which indicates that approximately $4.7bn has been obtained from development partners to reform Pakistan’s tax system. “Therefore, the Revenue Division should conduct an impact assessment of previous reform interventions undertaken under the Tax Administration Reforms Programme (TARP), Pakistan Single Window (PSW), Integrated Transit Trade Management System (ITTMS) and Pakistan Raises Revenue Project (PRRP) — the four major loan programmes for the Federal Board of Revenue — to determine the impact of the reforms and value for money.” The project’s clearance was necessary to secure a $200m loan that the ADB’s management had already committed and which required formal approval by its board. The loan is part of the ADB’s Investment Project Financing (IPF) under its Ordinary Capital Resources (OCR) concessional window, or Asian Development Fund, and carries a standard concessional term of 25 years, including a five-year grace period, at an annual interest rate of 1.5pc to 2pc. Based on objections raised by various analytical wings of the Planning Commission and views expressed by PIDE, Iqbal “stressed the need for clearly defined and measurable outcomes, particularly in terms of revenue generation, improvement in the tax-to-GDP ratio and expansion of the taxpayer base”, an official statement said. It added that the FBR had assured that the loan would contribute to increasing the tax-to-GDP ratio to 13.5pc by 2029 and bringing more taxpayers into the tax net. The Planning Commission demanded that, before approving the project and the loan, the FBR should first seek concept clearance on whether the project was synchronised with the FBR’s overall Rs350bn Transformation Plan approved by the federal cabinet. It also sought a matrix of policy actions proposed under the transformation plan, along with the implementation status of each policy action or initiative under previous, ongoing and proposed programmes. The FBR reported that the project was part of its 2024-28 Transformation Plan for accelerating the digitalisation of the revenue administration ecosystem, enhancing operational efficiency, improving taxpayer compliance, ensuring transparent customs clearance and elevating Pakistan’s global competitiveness. The Planning Commission had reservations that the project should not proceed without an impact analysis of already executed projects aimed at reforming and strengthening the FBR’s capacity, a gap analysis of existing facilities, a needs assessment and a feasibility study to determine the requirements of the proposed intervention. It also called for quantifiable results-based measurement indicators (RBMIs) and a plan to ensure the sustainability of the project’s activities after its completion. External experts from the National University of Computer and Emerging Sciences and the National University of Sciences and Technology also highlighted the lack of a gap analysis, a comprehensive data security framework, clarity on the basis of the AI model proposed for system design, and an analysis of the existing system and details of the AI model proposed to be deployed in the new systems. Among other things, the project also seeks to upgrade hardware and software, including existing 850TB servers, to 3PB as data storage requirements are expected to increase following the implementation of video-camera storage and processing systems on production lines in five major sectors, including sugar, cement, tobacco and textiles. The CDWP also recommended the “PakSat-2 Satellite System”, costing Rs37.2bn, to the ECNEC for approval. In June, the CDWP had supported the project in principle to deploy a sovereign, secure and ‘below-internet’ mobile communication network for at least 10,000 government users to address gaps that were causing critical information leakages, but had raised technical questions. The PakSat-2 project is aimed at replacing PakSat-IR, which is set to complete its 15-year operational life later this year. The Planning Commission had advised improving the technical capabilities of the Rs709 million ‘PAKAWAZ Secure Mobile Communication Ecosystem’, which aims to establish a secure mobile communication application capable of supporting video and audio calls, voice and video messages, file and photo sharing, centrally managed contact lists, and text and group messaging, besides application servers, mobile handsets, kill-switch controls and so on. The sources said the National Telecommunication Company (NTC), which had proposed the project, had improved the specifications for an unbreakable security system with maximum integrity to ensure confidentiality, integrity, availability and national data sovereignty. The project is designed to conform to national security requirements by providing an isolated “private 4G LTE Core Network” that is physically or logically air-gapped from the public internet, addressing shortcomings identified after the recent war with India and lessons learnt from other events across the Middle East. Besides referring these two mega projects, worth Rs94.3bn, to Ecnec, the CDWP itself approved five projects with a combined cost of Rs21.59bn. These included the Rs5.015bn “Promotion of Olive Cultivation on Commercial Scale in Pakistan Phase-II”. Phase I was implemented in Kallar Kahar, Islamabad and the wider Potohar belt and is now being expanded to Balochistan and the New Merged Districts. The meeting also approved Rs6bn for a revised project to establish the Dr Ashfaq Ahmad Khan Centre in Basic Sciences across eight national centres, seven of which — covering Artificial Intelligence, Cyber Security, Big Data and Cloud Computing, GIS and Satellite Technology, Automation and Robotics, Applied Mathematics, and Livestock and Genomics — had been completed. The meeting also approved Rs3.623bn for the “Development of Geospatial Complex (Geo-AI Development & Innovation Hub)” for the development of a space programme. Another Rs5.758bn was approved for “Revamping and Refurbishment of Different Facilities at Pakistan Sports Complex (PSC), Islamabad”. A water-sector project titled “Improvement of High Frequency (HF) Radios Network of Water Resources Management Directorate (WRMD), WAPDA”, worth Rs338.179 million, was also approved.

The day America lost its soul

WASHINGTON: Twenty-five years ago, on a bright September morning, America woke up to a different world. I was in Washington. I was asleep when John Hendel, our news editor at the American news agency where I worked, called. “There have been attacks of Biblical proportions in New York and Washington,” he said. “Go out and see what is happening. But come back and sleep. We have transferred you to the news desk. You’ll be doing night shifts.” The remaining tower of the World Trade Centre, Tower 2, collapses in a cloud of dust and debris about a half hour after the first tower fell, in New York City, the US on September 11, 2001. — Reuters/File My wife, Bushra, and I went out. We lived in downtown Washington, and the city suddenly looked unfamiliar. Armoured personnel carriers stood at crossroads and strategic points. Men in uniform were everywhere. The capital of the world’s most powerful country looked like a city preparing for war. We reached the White House. Bushra was wearing a shalwar-kameez. Near the White House, a man stopped his bicycle, pointed at her and shouted: “There goes a terrorist. Catch her, catch her.” A few people stopped, looked and walked on. I remember that moment vividly, perhaps because it contained, in miniature, something that would become much larger in the years that followed. The woman walking beside me had not changed. Her clothes had not changed. I had not changed. But suddenly those clothes could be interpreted differently. Something that had always meant home, culture and identity could, in somebody else’s eyes, become evidence of something sinister. Later, we drove as close to the Pentagon as we could. Smoke was still rising. There was a large hole in the building. We interviewed people who had seen the plane hit. The damaged area of the Pentagon, where a commercial airliner slammed into it September 11, 2001, is seen in the early morning at sunrise with the US Capitol Building in the background, on September 16, 2001. — Reuters Washington was frightened, but it did not descend into chaos. There was no mass killing, burning or looting. Government continued to function. People tried to understand what had happened and what might happen next. Yet something had happened to America that could not be seen in the smoke. America had changed. The physical destruction of September 11 was enormous: the World Trade Centre was destroyed, the Pentagon was struck, and nearly 3,000 people were killed. But the consequences went far beyond the buildings and the people who died that morning. The attacks changed the way Americans thought about safety, government, war, immigration, surveillance and, perhaps most importantly, one another. An American flag flies near the base of the destroyed World Trade Centre in New York, the US on September 11, 2001. — Reuters Before September 11, many Americans could believe that the great threats to their country existed somewhere else. Wars happened overseas. Terrorism was something that appeared on television from distant places. The oceans seemed to provide a measure of protection. Then four aeroplanes crossed those oceans and turned ordinary American objects into weapons. The country discovered its vulnerability. The response was understandable. America had been attacked, and Americans wanted protection. The government created new security institutions, tightened controls at airports and borders, expanded intelligence gathering and embarked on wars that would last for decades. The language of national security entered everyday life. But every society pays a price for living in permanent fear. Privacy became something that had to be balanced against security. Surveillance became a familiar part of public life. Immigration and national security became increasingly connected in the public imagination. People began to look more closely at those who seemed foreign, different or difficult to classify. For American Muslims, the change was particularly painful. The men who attacked America claimed to act in the name of Islam. American Muslims had nothing to do with their crimes, yet they found themselves living under a shadow cast by people they had never met and whose actions they condemned. Muslims were simultaneously victims of terrorism and, in some eyes, objects of suspicion because of terrorism. That was one of the great ironies of September 11. The attacks did not merely produce fear of terrorism. They produced a new social question: Who is an American, and how do we recognise one? For many Muslim Americans, the question became personal. A name could matter. A beard could matter. A headscarf could matter. A language could matter. A passport could matter. Even the alphabet in which a newspaper was printed could matter. I discovered this for myself when I visited New York soon after the attacks. I was on a train from Queens to Manhattan. It was packed, but nobody was looking at me. For a while I felt safe in the anonymity of the crowd. Then a burly white man boarded. His shirt carried Gen. George Patton’s famous philosophy of war: the aim was not to die for your country but to make the other fellow die for his. He sat opposite me, put his newspaper away and stared at me. I had no reason to believe he wanted to hurt me. But fear does not always require a reason. I tried to ignore him, but my fear —a nd something like guilt — rose in my throat. I called my friend Haider Rizvi in Brooklyn. Days earlier, Haider had been beaten outside a bar near his apartment. He knew something about this kind of hatred. Years earlier, Muslim zealots in Islamabad had burned his motorcycle because of his unconventional views. Years later, he moved to New York. “It is a city I respect,” he once told me. “It allowed me to be lost in its madness. It is so big and so crowded that you can live here without being discovered by friends or foes.” That was one of the great gifts of New York: anonymity. After September 11, even that anonymity seemed less secure. When I told Haider about the man staring at me on the train, he panicked. “Get off the train. Take a cab and come over to me,” he said. The train entered the tunnel and our conversation was cut off. I looked around for reassurance — a friendly smile, a familiar face, a little affection. I found none. When I got off, my phone rang. “What happened?” “We got disconnected.” “Keep it on. I’ll keep checking.” And he did. By then I felt better. I walked from Grand Central Station toward the United Nations, a walk I had always enjoyed, passing people of every colour and creed. Suddenly I understood why I liked that walk so much. It allowed me to forget myself. Becoming anonymous in a great city is liberating. You need not explain your religion, country, language or history. You are simply another person walking down the street. Coming into Manhattan from Queens on the A train, however, I had tried to hide behind an Urdu newspaper. Then I realised my mistake. Few people might know what Urdu was, but many could recognise its Arabic script. It could identify me as one of “them.” I folded the newspaper and picked up the New York Post. A girl wearing a hijab entered the train and sat beside me. “Salaam,” I said. She smiled but did not answer. Was she nervous? Or was I imagining fear everywhere? I don’t know. A large group of mostly white men entered. Their presence somehow increased my fear. I looked for the Muslim girl. She was gone. The train passed through stations where World Trade Centre commuters once emerged every morning. I felt dizzy. Something inside me seemed broken. I felt incomplete. Even the UN building looked different. Hundreds of soldiers guarded it. There were more on motorboats, patrolling the Hudson. Looking back after twenty-five years, I realise that this was not simply the fear of an individual immigrant. It was the beginning of a much larger psychological transformation. The country was trying to determine how much freedom it could sacrifice for safety. Muslims were trying to determine how much of their identity they could display without becoming targets. And America as a whole was trying to decide whether difference was something to be celebrated, tolerated or feared.

Terror forever

THE 9/11 attacks happened 25 years ago, ushering in a new epoch. The so-called global war on terror was launched by the Bush administration a few days later, and Washington has wreaked havoc across the globe ever since. The majority of the world’s states have followed the US lead by making the prosecution and hunting down of ‘terrorists’ their primary raison d’être. Ideological apparatuses, including the culture industry, have indoctrinated generations of young and old alike into believing that ‘terror’ is ubiquitous and that the state must be endlessly empowered to snuff it out, even at the cost of basic liberties. Pakistan was one of the major staging grounds of the ‘war on terror’, facilitating both the US invasion and occupation of Afghanistan whilst also launching innumerable military operations on its own soil. A plethora of ‘anti-terrorist’ laws have been promulgated since 2001, the scope of which is growing wider. While these instruments are deployed to clamp down on all forms of dissent and popular political mobilisation, the insurgencies and other forms of political violence that ostensibly should have been curbed by the state’s various ‘counterterrorist’ initiatives have only become more intense. Beneath the surface, a global military-industrial complex profits endlessly from the West’s totalitarian regime. Washington has thrown $8 trillion at various wars since 2001, a significant chunk of which has been pocketed by private defence contractors integrated with the deep state. In Pakistan, too, the ‘war on terror’ inaugurated a host of opportunities for state functionaries and private contractors to mint money. The policing of dissent is paralleled by Big Tech’s power. Today, we are at another crossroads. With the rise of Big Tech to the apex of global capitalism, the nexus of state and private capital has morphed into a behemoth without historical precedent. The discourse around ‘terrorism’ expanded some years ago in conjunction with the dramatic transformations of cultural, economic and political life provoked by digitalisation. Terms like ‘cyberterrorism/‘cybersecurity’ and ‘fifth-generation war’ reflect the evolution of a political idiom that both incorporates the logics of the war on terror and extends well beyond it. The policing of dissent by the state and aligned propagandists in the digital lifeworld is paralleled by the immense power of Big Tech companies to access, share and manipulate data. Earlier this week, a whistleblower resigned from Anthropic, warning that AI development is being allowed to spiral into an existential threat to humanity. Anthropic’s Claude tool has already shown its destructive capabilities at the outset of the war on Iran. Edward Snowden told us what was coming as early as 2013. Despite their claims of being pro-market libertarians, billionaires like Peter Thiel and Elon Musk are buttressing the state’s immense coercive and surveillance capacities. They view humanity as a plaything that is, at best, ‘saved’ and, at worst, sacrificed in the interests of landing on Mars or crafting more ‘intelligent’ species. Quite aside from hyperbolic claims that an AI-triggered extinction event is around the corner, the unbridled pursuit of power and profit by Big Tech alongside the contemporary state’s unlimited quest for social control must surely trouble anyone concerned with the age-old quest for human emancipation. Even non-human life is at stake. At a time when climate breakdown events are playing out with ever greater frequency and intensity, states everywhere are bending over backwards to build huge data centres to serve Big Tech’s interests by guaranteeing eno­rmous and unint­errupted supplies of electricity and water. The US st­­a­te and Silicon Val­ley billionaires are the primary protagonists of the contemporary militarised capitalist order. But we cannot be seduced by blind-faith arguments that there are ‘good’ states — not least of them our own — and ‘green’ capitalisms that do not conform to the overarching logic of the system. We need, instead, to learn the lessons from the ‘war on terror’ and stop being cheerleaders for an even more sinister and unhinged digitalised fusion of state and corporate power that will shape our present and future. A critical mass in Pakistan continues to speak truth to the militarised ruling class over its whittling away of the few civil liberties we still possess, including in the digital domain, whilst also resisting resource grabs and dispossession. The challenge, however, is outlined every time an episode of ‘terrorism’ is capitalised upon by the state to whip up a nationalist frenzy in which all good sense is abandoned. If the global totalitarian regime’s march is to be halted, we must coalesce around an anti-capitalist ideological politics rooted in working people and nature. And soon. The writer teaches at Quaid-i-Azam University, Islamabad. Published in Dawn, September 11th, 2026
