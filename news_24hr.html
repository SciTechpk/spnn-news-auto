India’s relevance?

INDIA is the most populous country in the world. With a nominal GDP of $4.15 trillion, it is the sixth-largest economy globally, with a big market share that makes countries want to do business with it. Its expansive territory extends down to the northern tip of the Indian Ocean. Despite this strategic weight, why is India losing proportionate relevance in today’s global geopolitics? Five reasons can be identified. Firstly, for decades, India was a partner of choice for the US in countering China’s rise. Imagining itself as the net security provider for South Asia, India joined the America-led Indo-Pacific Strategy, Quad, I2U2, and IMEC. Beijing sensed well in time that America was encircling China with the help of India, Japan and Australia. It could also see that America continued to stir trouble in the South China Sea and could well choke Chinese exports passing through the Pacific and Indian oceans. Pursuant to Russian President Vladimir Putin’s Greater Eurasia partnership initiative, China is increasingly using three overland northern routes and one sea route through the Arctic Ocean. Adapting quickly, America decided to work with China for “constructive strategic stability”. This was announced during President Donald Trump’s meeting with President Xi Jinping in May 2026. The US also reverted its Indo-Pacific Command back to Pacific Command. India’s salience began to change from a strategic US partner to a commercially relevant country. Secondly, nothing has altered the balance of power in South Asia more profoundly than the events of May 2025. Encouraged by its blatant aggression against Pakistan in 2016 and 2019, Narendra Modi’s government launched missile strikes against nine targets in Pakistan on May 7, wrongly assuming that Pakistan would not be able to respond. It learned to its surprise that Pakistan not only responded robustly on May 10 but also forced India to seek a ceasefire through the US leadership. When the US president helped secure a ceasefire, India did not have the grace to accept the role the US had played. As a result, it found itself in an uncomfortable situation in which the country it sought to please was unresponsive. Pakistan’s military credentials, on the other hand, were recognised by the world at large. Soon after the confrontation, Saudi Arabia entered into a Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement with Pakistan in September 2025. Earlier this month, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and Turkiye signed the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement, making India wonder how and why it lost its strategic space in West Asia. Thirdly, South Asia is the world’s least integrated region. A major factor is that India has unfriendly ties with most of its neighbours. It has also made Saarc dysfunctional lest other South Asian states team up to counterbalance it. Indian policymakers have failed to recognise that India’s global stature and relevance will never be realised unless it can take regional countries along. The May 2025 conflict has altered the balance of power in South Asia. Fourthly, for some time, India has tried to project itself as a champion of the Global South. The reality is that China is miles ahead in cultivating developing countries through massive investment in infrastructure, energy and other projects. Furthermore, India has sought to lean closer to the US, clearly signalling its priorities to the Global South. For instance, India opposed the creation of a common BRICS currency to challenge the US dollar, though it does not reject bilateral trade in national currencies. Finally, it was Pakistan, and not India, that was deemed a credible mediator to bring about a ceasefire to the 40-day war between the US and Iran. Pakistan hosted the US and Iran in Islamabad for direct talks, helped conclude the Islamabad MoU, and is continuing its efforts to achieve peace in West Asia. Pakistan’s diplomatic efforts have been appreciated by the entire world except India and Israel that have been courting each other. Three days before the US-Iran war started, the Indian prime minister was in Israel praising the latter as the fatherland without saying a word about the Israeli genocide in Gaza. India’s real priorities stood exposed to Iran and the Gulf countries. Importantly, Pakistan’s rising diplomatic profile has also thwarted India’s decade-long effort to isolate it. While these five reasons have made India strategically less relevant today, Pakistan’s diplomatic and military profile has continued to rise. For Pakistan to sustain its global relevance, an equal effort is needed to build up its economic profile. Given the close linkages between a country’s citizen-centric governance and economic output, the recent debate over how to devolve resources to the grassroots is timely. Durbar legacy in Balochistan

THE idea of a durbar still fascinates us and connects us to the subcontinent’s past when kings, rajas, maharajas and nawabs held court. The media, mythology, poetry, theatre, books, and cinema have helped shape the popular image of a ruler seated on his throne, with his ministers and courtiers standing before him. Matters of revenue, war, peace and justice were decided in the ruler’s court. Sometimes, the ruler decided on matters of life and death. However, our fascination may fade once we realise that the legacy of the durbar continues under different names. Pakistan is not the only country carrying this legacy. Many Western countries, including our former colonial ruler, Britain, still retain their monarchies though stripping them of their powers. During the colonial period, the British not only preserved but also strengthened the durbars to control the population and govern indirectly. At the end of British rule, the subcontinent contained approximately 565 princely states. However, historical estimates vary depending on how smaller states are classified. Thirteen were eventually incorporated into Pakistan after their rulers signed Instruments of Accession. Among the courts inherited by Pakistan, none carried greater political symbolism in Balochistan than the Sibi Durbar. It is associated with bitter memories from both the colonial and postcolonial periods. The Sibi Durbar became a major instrument of colonial administrator Robert Sandeman’s frontier policy in the late 19th century. It was an annual winter assembly at which British officials met the officially approved Baloch, Brahui, and Pakhtun chiefs. Tribal disputes were heard, successions and political relationships were considered, honours and allowances were distributed, and the hierarchy created by colonial rule was publicly displayed. Dismantling an institution isn’t the same as changing the political culture that sustained it. Many stories were subsequently associated with the durbar and the British treatment of Baloch and Pakhtun chiefs. One account concerns an earlier Mir Khair Bakhsh Marri, who reportedly refused an order that required the assembled sardars to pull the British political agent’s carriage. According to later accounts, he was punished by being denied privileges granted to more cooperative chiefs. The episode remains part of Baloch political memory. The formal arrangement of the durbar preserved the appearance of the older political order. The chiefs of Sarawan and Jhalawan traditionally sat to the right and left of the Khan of Kalat. Yet the most powerful person in the assembly was not necessarily the Khan. Effective authority rested with the British political agent. Pakistan retained the Sibi Durbar after independence. On Feb 14, 1948, the Quaid addressed what he described as the first “shahi durbar” of Balochistan held under the authority of Pakistan. Speaking to members of the shahi jirga, the sardars and other representatives, he promised political reform. He announced a governor-general’s advisory council as an interim arrangement until a representative constitutional system could be established. One of the last politically consequential Sibi Durbar assemblies took place on Feb 16, 1954. Several sardars submitted a petition to the office of the agent to the governor-general calling for the Balochistan States Union (BSU) to be merged with the former Chief Commissioner’s Province of Balochistan. The BSU emerged from an agreement among the rulers of Kalat, Kharan, Makran, and Lasbela in 1952 and formally came into existence on Oct 3 that year. The four rulers retained limited authority within a union headed initially by the Khan of Kalat. The arrangement, however, survived for only three years. In October 1955, the Union was dissolved and incorporated into the newly created province of West Pakistan under the One Unit scheme. Historians have questioned whether the petition presented at the 1954 durbar represented a spontaneous demand by the sardars or reflected a political process already being encouraged by the central government. Whatever its origins, the episode demonstrated that the durbar could still be used to provide traditional legitimacy for decisions being shaped elsewhere. The abolition of the BSU and the imposition of One Unit deepened political resentment in Balochistan. A few accounts also linked Nawab Nauroz Khan’s uprising with the decision, though his armed struggle began a few years later. The Victoria Memorial Hall, now commonly known as the Jirga Hall, was the principal setting for the Sibi Durbar. It has since been converted into the Sibi Jirga Hall Museum, although it continues to be used for official and cultural functions. Its collection includes photographs, documents, and objects related to the colonial period and the region’s history. Photographs of Jinnah’s 1948 visit preserve the moment when the new state addressed Balochistan through a political institution inherited from the Raj. It is understandable that a newly created country confronting immense administrative and security challenges, could not dismantle every princely and colonial structure overnight. Pakistan required several years, and in some cases decades, to integrate the former princely states. But dismantling an institution is not the same as changing the political culture that sustained it. The princely courts disappeared, the colonial officers departed, and the old administrative structures were formally replaced. Yet the concentration of authority, reliance on selected intermediaries, and preference for managed consultation over genuine representation continued to shape relations between the centre and Balochistan. There is an element of the colonial legacy in Balochistan’s governance model and in the administration of its resources and politics. The Baloch sardars, despite the abolition of the sardari system in 1976, follow the same order and remain subservient to the power elites; hardly anyone raises a voice, and the reason is obvious: they don’t want to lose power or diminish their relationship with the power elites. Though few satirically associate a new institutional mechanism, called ‘workshop’, with the tradition of the durbar, Balochistan is still suffering. Power elites address their grievances through colonial instruments and have not made an earnest attempt to initiate the nation-building process or remove intermediaries — such as sardars and other cronies — between the state and the masses. Perhaps the power elites know that such a shift would change the entire power dynamic in the province and the country. The writer is a security analyst. Published in Dawn, August 30th, 2026

The speakers rejected India’s position that the treaty would remain in abeyance until violence in the region ceased, arguing that water-sharing arrangements should remain separate from political and security disputes and that the treaty’s provisions should continue to govern management of the Indus river system. The observation came during a discussion on the future of the treaty hosted by the Pakistan Embassy, with participants warning against allowing political tensions to undermine an agreement that has survived wars and repeated crises between Pakistan and India. The dialogue, titled “The Indus Waters Treaty: South Asian Security at Crossroads”, brought together US policy experts, academics, legal and water specialists, representatives of international organisations and diplomats to examine the treaty’s future and growing challenges to regional water security. Former Pakistan ambassador to the United Nations Munir Akram said Pakistan should continue to abide by the treaty despite current tensions, arguing that India’s decision to place it in abeyance did not alter its legal status. “Pakistan shouldn’t depart from the letter and spirit of the treaty,” he said. Mr Akram also argued that Pakistan needed an active diplomatic strategy in response to India’s position and should continue participating in treaty mechanisms even if New Delhi refused to do so. He suggested exploring the creation of an impartial international mechanism, including a possible role for the United Nations, and said Pakistan should also consult China, which he viewed as being in a position to influence the outcome. The discussion repeatedly returned to the need to preserve the treaty’s legal and institutional framework. Participants noted that the Indus Waters Treaty had survived wars, crises and prolonged political tensions and had helped keep water cooperation insulated from disputes. Pakistan’s ambassador to the United States Rizwan Saeed Sheikh said Islamabad had consistently favoured dialogue, diplomacy and peaceful coexistence, pointing to its efforts to facilitate dialogue over other regional disputes. “If we can do it for others, we will do it for ourselves,” he said, referring to Pakistan’s efforts to help resolve the Iran-US dispute. He also urged India to remain engaged with Pakistan, saying: “You cannot change neighbours, so we want to engage in constructive dialogue.” In his concluding remarks, Mr Sheikh said the problem with the Indus Waters Treaty was not its substance but the manner in which it was being implemented. “There is no crisis of content in the Indus Waters Treaty … there is a crisis of intent in implementation,” he said. Adil Najam, professor of international relations and dean emeritus at Boston University, said the treaty defined the future of water cooperation in South Asia and stressed the importance of cooperation in managing shared resources. He said climate change and increasing water stress should encourage, rather than undermine, cooperation between countries sharing water resources. Participants also cautioned against dehumanising the other side, with one speaker noting that there were “many sensible people in India” and arguing that the two countries needed to look for practical solutions despite their political differences. The discussion examined whether the current dispute was primarily political or legal, with legal experts stressing the importance of international law and established dispute-resolution mechanisms. Speakers also warned about the consequences of treating water as an instrument of political or military pressure. A large share of Pakistan’s population and economy depend on the Indus river system, participants noted, raising concerns about the regional consequences if water were weaponised. The nuclear dimension of the dispute was also discussed, with participants cautioning against assumptions that nuclear deterrence alone would prevent escalation. Mr Najam noted that despite the lack of trust between India and Pakistan, the treaty had worked for decades and argued that the priority should be to prevent the existing framework from falling apart. Participants also stressed the importance of restoring technical engagement, transparency and data-sharing between Pakistan and India under the treaty’s provisions, particularly as climate change and growing water stress place additional pressure on the region’s shared resources. Senator Rehman said India could not unilaterally suspend, disregard or reinterpret a decades-old bilateral water management framework at will. She said any decision to withdraw from or suspend an international agreement could not be made through a unilateral declaration, but had to be undertaken in accordance with established procedures of international law. “The Indian government’s recent actions cannot, in any manner, undermine Pakistan’s legitimate rights and interests concerning its water resources,” she said. Info ministry rejects ‘misleading’ foreign media reports on ‘convicted prisoner’

The Ministry of Information and Broadcasting (MOIB) on Saturday rejected what it called “misleading stories” circulated by foreign media outlets about a “convicted prisoner” incarcerated in Pakistan. The statement came after CNN anchor Becky Anderson interviewed Zulfi Bukhari, a former aide to PTI founder Imran Khan, on television. Anderson later posted the ministry’s statement on her X account. According to the statement, Imran is “not a person held under executive detention” and that he has been incarcerated based on multiple convictions after judicial proceedings, noting that the PTI founder “remains entitled to pursue all remedies available to him under Pakistani law”. “His imprisonment is governed by the Pakistan Prison Rules, applicable judicial directions and security arrangements appropriate to a high-profile prisoner,” the statement read. The information ministry also rejected allegations that Imran was being subjected to “punitive solitary confinement, cruelty or deprivation”, stating that visits, phone calls, reading materials and home-cooked meals, among other amenities, were available to him. “His accommodation includes an exclusive seven-cell compound with sleeping, sanitation and exercise facilities,” the ministry said. It further dismissed allegations that the PTI founder was being denied meetings with his family and party members, saying visits were allowed and that he had been holding weekly meetings with his wife, Bushra Bibi — who is also incarcerated at Adiala — with the most recent taking place on Aug 4. “Since his imprisonment, records show approximately 198 interview sessions involving more than 900 visitor entries. These include his sisters, family members, lawyers, doctors, political representatives and other approved visitors,” the MOIB stated. “Prison records further document telephone, landline and WhatsApp communication with family members in Pakistan and abroad, including a recorded telephone conversation with one of his sons on March 21, 2026,” it added. “More recently, meetings were also recorded with his sisters on Aug 18, 19 and 25, 2026.” The ministry also categorically rejected allegations that Imran had been denied appropriate medical care or that authorities had failed to comply with a Supreme Court order directing that he be transferred to the privately run Shifa International Hospital for medical examination and treatment. Despite the SC order, in the early hours of last Friday, Imran was moved from Adiala jail but taken to the state-run Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences instead, where he was examined by a panel of doctors, with his sister Dr Uzma Khan present, according to Information Minister Attaullah Tarar. “Since his imprisonment, the convicted prisoner has undergone approximately 30 medical examinations by specialist teams and medical boards involving institutions including Pims, Shifa International, Shaukat Khanum and Al-Shifa Trust Eye Hospital, in addition to regular examinations by the prison doctor,” the MOIB stated. “Following the diagnosis of a retinal condition, the convicted prisoner received specialist treatment, including five intravitreal anti-VEGF injections, retinal imaging, medication and subsequent follow-up examinations,” it added, referring to Imran’s eye condition.
