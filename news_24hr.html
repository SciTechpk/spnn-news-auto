Bitcoin rises above $80,000 as soft dollar, debasement fears boost momentum

Bitcoin rose above $80,000 to hit a more than three-month high on Tuesday as a soft US dollar, in the wake of the moves by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent to calm the bond market, revived momentum in the crypto sector. US President Donald Trump last week called on Congress to pass a bill that would bring clearer definitions to the growing cryptocurrency sector. Since then, bitcoin, the world’s largest cryptocurrency, has risen 16 per cent. It was last at $80,323.24 in Asian hours, having earlier touched $81,237.94, its highest level since mid-May. Bitcoin is up 28pc so far in August, set for its biggest monthly gain since November 2024. Cryptocurrencies also got a big boost after the US Treasury last week unveiled plans to buy back more long-dated bonds to help cap the gains in the long-end yields, a move that has led to the US dollar bearing the brunt of investor angst. Tim Sun, senior researcher at HashKey Group, said Bessent’s messaging has reinforced the market’s view that, at least through the midterm elections, US policymakers may have a lower tolerance for a further rise in long-end yields. “That would create a relatively supportive macro backdrop for assets such as bitcoin and gold,” Sun said. Gold has been the other beneficiary of the dollar weakness, rising to a three-month high. The Treasury announcement is “exactly the type of thing bitcoin loves”, Geoff Kendrick, global head of digital assets research at Standard Chartered, said in a note last week, adding that bitcoin was built to allow investors a way to avoid this type of intervention. The action stoked increased chatter around the so-called debasement trade, where the moves to prevent long-end yields from reaching market-clearing levels via buybacks lead the pressure to shift from the bond market to the currency market. “This (Treasury announcement) prompted buyers to scramble into physical and digital assets as debasement trade fears re-emerged,” said Tony Sycamore, market analyst at IG. “A sustained break above here would open the door for a move towards $95,000 and $100,000.”

Why attempts to reform the UN Security Council keep failing ... and what would actually work

Every few years, the world revisits an old promise: reforming the UN Security Council so it reflects today’s realities rather than the victors of a war fought eight decades ago. Each round of talks ends the same way — more speeches, no structural change. The reason lies not in the details of who gets a seat, but in what that seat can do once occupied. The UN General Assembly’s decision, in its 62nd session, to begin intergovernmental negotiations (IGN) on equitable representation in the Security Council set this process in motion. It gained real traction in 2008, when the IGN was formally established to carry forward UNSC expansion and reform — an effort born of the decision-making paralysis inherent in the UN’s own institutional design. To understand the limits of that design, it helps to look back at the world that produced it. A council born of victors’ justice The UN system, established after 1945, reflected the realities of a Hobbesian world, where the victors’ justice was codified in the shape of a UN Charter that gave the five major victors of World War II the power to keep their interests protected and fence in their rivalries with a veto. The League of Nations’ failure to prevent that war offers a preview of the institutional rigidity now visible in the UN itself. The League’s absence of key powers such as Germany, Russia, and the United States, combined with its consensus-based decision-making and lack of enforcement mechanisms, left it unable to act on the Soviet Union’s attack on Manchuria, Italy’s invasion of Abyssinia, or Germany’s abrogation of the Versailles Treaty. The lessons of the League’s failure apply just as directly to the Security Council’s structure and decision-making today. The veto mechanism was designed less to prevent conflict than to stop the P5 from confronting one another directly, reducing the odds of a great-power war. That arrangement has, so far, held off direct confrontation between global powers, but has done little to stop regional and proxy wars, some of which those same powers have actively sustained or promoted. Though the present UN Charter defines the legality of war and the territorial rights of each member state through Article 2(4), there is no check or meaningful cost on the breach of the same article by veto-wielding powers of the P5. The result is an inability of the UN General Assembly and the UNSC to prevent violations of the territorial integrity of sovereign states and even acts of genocide. The Atlantic Charter, which gave rise to Nato, suffered a similar anomaly: for all of Article 5’s collective-security language, the alliance was better captured by its first Secretary General Lord Ismay’s remark that “Nato was created to keep the Russians out, the Americans in, and the Germans down”. The same flaw, in a harsher era The UN system’s principal instrument for preventing conflict, the Security Council, carries the same anomaly today. Whatever the 10 non-permanent members intend, the five permanent, veto-wielding states can block any effort at peace enforcement. This is a system built on power asymmetry, not on the democratic judgement needed to prevent conflict in an increasingly fragmented world. That democratic retreat is visible well beyond the Council: in unilateralism, tariff wars, de-globalisation, and and the emasculation of the institutions of economic cooperation such as the World Trade Organisation. As great-power competition rewrites the rules for weaker states, a Kantian world of cooperation is giving way to a Hobbesian one. In an environment where the weak must fend for themselves and the Charter fails to prevent crimes against humanity, annexations, and genocide, adding more layers of privilege to the Security Council makes little sense. Reform should instead make the Council more democratic and inclusive, and raise the cost of a veto used to shield genocide, crimes against humanity, or violations of territorial integrity. Sans above, any reforms would be merely cosmetic and incapable of resolving the peace and security issues among nations. Several groups have put forward competing ideas. The G4 (Brazil, Germany, India, Japan) proposes expanding both permanent and non-permanent membership: permanent seats for the G4 countries, two permanent seats for Africa, and more elected non-permanent seats. The African Group, representing all 54 African Union member states, is asking for two permanent seats with full prerogatives, veto included, plus five additional non-permanent seats. A France-Mexico initiative calls on the P5 to voluntarily refrain from using the veto in situations involving mass atrocities such as genocide, crimes against humanity and large-scale war crimes. The Uniting for Consensus (UfC) proposal, led by Pakistan and backed by Italy, Mexico, Argentina, Canada, and South Korea, rejects any expansion of the permanent membership category, with or without veto power. It instead argues for expanding the elected non-permanent category through longer, renewable terms. The only proposal that works A close look at these proposals shows that all but the UfC model sidestep the core issue: the veto itself, and the inability of the rest of the membership to challenge it even when it is used to pursue P5 geopolitical objectives at odds with international law, collective security, or Article 2(4). Supporting geographic representation, particularly for African nations, is worthwhile. But turning yesterday’s victims of exclusion into tomorrow’s veto-wielding hegemons would only deepen the present inequity. Similarly, individual countries’ pursuit of permanent seats has to be judged against the broader goals of equitable representation, justice, and a genuinely more effective Council. The UfC is the only proposal that expands the Council through more non-permanent seats with re-election, while freezing the current inequity by not adding to the permanent category. Moreover, it does so while still enhancing regional representation from Africa, Asia-Pacific, West Asia, and Latin America. This incremental, election-based approach can adapt to shifting geopolitical realities in a way that permanent structural change cannot. Finally, reform must be careful not to compound inequity by adding more aspirants to the permanent category. Countries like India, which have been serial violators of UNSC resolutions and have annexed disputed regions besides remaining outside UN-affiliated treaties such as the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT) and the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT), do not fit the profile of a permanent Security Council member.

Fixing agriculture

PRIME Minister Shehbaz Sharif’s renewed push to strengthen agricultural research, provide quality seeds to smallholders and medium-scale farmers, and improve crop estimate systems could help address some of the sector’s long-standing weaknesses in farm productivity. But good intentions alone are not enough. The real question is whether the government can turn his directives into a coherent policy and implement it effectively. That would require reforming federal and provincial agriculture policies, markets and institutions that determine what farmers produce, what they pay for inputs and what they earn. Above all, an agricultural policy must be responsive to the needs of small and medium-sized farmers rather than powerful lobbies. Take the example of the sugar sector. Policy has repeatedly been designed around the interests of millers, who have traditionally enjoyed political influence. Decisions on support prices, cane procurement, export, and other interventions aim to protect them. Cotton illustrates another failure of the agricultural system. Farmers often do not receive a fair share of the value generated by their crop, with middlemen and other parts of the supply chain capturing much of the margin. The result is reduced yields over the years. Improving farm productivity means little if farmers remain trapped in inefficient markets and weak bargaining arrangements. A serious agricultural reform programme must address these structural problems. Smallholders need easier and cheaper access to bank credit, fairly priced and reliable inputs, protection against adulterated pesticides and poor-quality fertiliser and certified, high-yielding and climate-resilient seeds. They also need better extension services that take modern farming techniques from research institutions to the field. Market reform is as important. Farmers should have greater direct access to efficient markets and buyers, instead of remaining dependent on middlemen. Better storage, grading, transport, digital market information and transparent price mechanisms can help reduce the gap between what consumers pay and what farmers receive. The proposed national system for estimating crop production is overdue. Reliable data is essential for planning imports, exports, storage and prices. But such a system will be useful only if its estimates are credible and produced through modern technology. The question, therefore, is not whether the PM’s intentions are honest. They are. The question is whether his government has what it takes to confront the entrenched interests that benefit from the system. Agricultural modernisation requires a comprehensive plan covering research, seeds, credit, inputs, extension, markets, water management and value chains, with the smallholder at its centre. It also requires political will to reform policies that currently favour powerful groups. Without these, no intervention will deliver the productive gains that farmers and the economy urgently need. The stable outlook balances a potentially faster improvement in Pakistan’s credit fundamentals against outstanding risks related to the vulnerabilities above, which, if materialised, could weaken access to foreign-currency financing and further reduce fiscal flexibility. He made the remarks while speaking outside the Supreme Court (SC) alongside PTI leader Sardar Latif Khosa after the two met with the court’s registrar. “Khan sahib does not make deals. He will not stay in the hospital a minute more than is necessary for his tests and treatment. If he had to make a deal, he would not have spent three years in jail,” Gohar said. “All we have to say is that there should be no politics on his health,” he said. During the press talk, Gohar said that he and Khosa had arrived at the SC to meet with Chief Justice of Pakistan Yahya Afridi in connection with the party’s contempt petition against the government’s alleged violation of its Aug 18 order for shifting Imran to Shifa International Hospital. “I asked to meet the chief justice. He was busy so it could not happen. Myself and Khosa sahib met the SC registrar and requested that a number be allotted to this case and it be fixed for hearing,” he said. He said that the registrar had assured that a case number would be allotted to the contempt plea. Gohar said that they were also assured of the early fixation of the case before the SC. “This [Aug 18 order] cannot be ignored. The whole world is looking at the SC and whether it is able to implement its order,” he said. “We only wanted PTI founder’s medical tests to be conducted at a private hospital, with both his sister Uzma and his personal physician Dr Faisal Sultan included in the medical board examining him,” he said. Meanwhile, Khosa said that a case number was being allotted to the was in Lahore. Last week, Imran’s sister Uzma Khan had filed contempt petition before the SC against officials for flouting its Aug 18 order. The petition, filed by Advocate Uzair Karamat Bhandari on behalf of Dr Uzma, also requested the apex court to depute an officer or appoint a local commission to ensure that Imran was immediately shifted to the private hospital in accordance with its earlier directions. Imran’s hospital transfer saga Last week, the SC had ordered the government to shift Imran to Shifa International Hospital within two days for examination and treatment by a multidisciplinary medical board. A three-member bench headed by Justice Shahid Waheed and comprising Justices Naeem Akhtar Afghan and Ishtiaq Ibr­ahim issued the interim contempt petition, and quoted the registrar as saying that one of the judges directions on petitions seeking Imran’s hospitalisation, access to personal physicians and family members and provision of his medical record to his family. The court had also ordered that Dr Uzma and Dr Sultan — Imran’s personal physician — be present during the medical check-up by a medical board. At the same time, Imran’s family members, PTI members and lawyers associated with him were barred from disclosing his health condition or medical reports to the media or public until the next hearing. The next day, the government had filed a review plea against the court’s order to shift Imran to the private hospital, arguing that the directive was discriminatory and issued in violation of prison rules and principles of natural justice. However, the SC had returned the plea on Thursday with the objection that the contents of the affidavit and facts, along with the review petition filed under Article 188 of the Constitution, had not been properly drawn up and that one of the paper books was not in order. The same night, elaborate arrangements were put in place in and around Shifa International Hospital, and all signs pointed to the PTI founder being brought there, in compliance with the SC order. But, it became clear on Friday morning that Imran had been taken to Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences (Pims) for a check-up and then subsequently transported back to prison. Information Minister Attaullah Tarar blamed “the security situation created by PTI workers on the way to and outside Al Shifa Hospital” for the diversion. In a post on X, he said, “In the light of the SC’s order, the prisoner was taken to the hospital under appropriate security arrangements during the night between August 20 and 21. “A team of qualified doctors, including an ophthalmologist, a cardiologist, and a physician, conducted a detailed examination of him and declared him medically fit,” he said. Later, the Pims administration said that two specialists from Shifa Inter­national Hospital participated in Imran’s eye assessment, while other examinations were carried out by the concerned Pims specialists. Imran has been taken to Pims multiple times after his eye ailment — right central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) — came to light in late January. “The clinical input arising from the ophthalmological assessment was taken into account alongside findings of the investigations conducted at Pims,” the statement said. Meanwhile, the government on Thursday filed a fresh review petition against the SC’s Aug 18 order. The Islamabad chief commissioner again filed the plea, saying that his office had a direct, substantial and legally protected interest in the matter and that the Aug 18 order adversely affected its constitutional authority.

Finance Minister Muhammad Aurangzeb, Adviser to the Prime Minister on Political Affairs Rana Sanaullah, Information Technology Minister Shaza Fatima Khawaja, Law Minister Azam Nazeer Tarar, Economic Affairs Minister Ahad Khan Cheema and Petroleum Minister Ali Pervaiz Malik also attended the meeting. American varsities urge international students to return to US before new immigration rules come into effect

WASHINGTON: Several major US universities have urged international students to return to the United States before September 15, when new federal immigration rules are scheduled to take effect. Under the new rules, international students can remain in the US as long as they maintain their academic status, with a fixed period of admission for holders of F (academic students), J (exchange visitors) and I (foreign media and journalists) category visas. Universities, including Harvard, Columbia, Ohio State University, Georgetown, George Washington (GW) and the University of Chicago, warned international students that the changes could affect the terms under which F-1 and J-1 students were admitted and allowed to remain in the country. Those planning international travel around the September 15 deadline were also advised to reconsider their plans. Universities said returning before that date could help students avoid being admitted under the new fixed-period system. Harvard’s International Office specifically advised F-1 and J-1 students and scholars to plan their travel so that they were physically present in the US by September 15. Columbia University set an even earlier target, asking international students to return by September 8 to allow sufficient time for the transition. Georgetown’s Office of Global Services (OGS) issued targeted warning letters to students, scholars and alumni. George Washington University’s International Services Office (ISO) advised students to check their records through the university’s portal on or shortly after September 15 to track their new, explicit programme-end dates. The University of Chicago’s Office of International Affairs held urgent informational webinars to explain the rule’s strict limitations on academic mobility and the shortened 30-day grace period. The new rule The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) rule, scheduled to take effect on September 15, replaced the long-standing “duration of status,” or D/S system with fixed periods of admission for many international students and exchange visitors. Under the existing system, eligible F-1 students are generally admitted for the “duration of status,” allowing them to remain in the US while they pursue their approved programme and maintain their immigration status. Under the new system, students entering or re-entering the US will generally receive an “Admit Until Date” on their Form I-94 — an official entry document issued by the US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) when a foreign national enters the US — and their authorised stay will normally be tied to the length of their programme, subject to a maximum initial admission period of four years. The change could have significant consequences for students enrolled in programmes that routinely take longer than four years, including some doctoral, medical and other professional programmes. Students who need additional time may have to apply for an extension of stay through US Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS). For students already in the US, the transition rules are less disruptive. Students who are physically present in the country on September 15 and are maintaining their F-1 or J-1 status will generally be covered by transition provisions and will not immediately have to obtain a new admission period simply because the rule takes effect. The situation changes, however, for students who leave the US and return after September 15. They could be admitted under the new fixed-date system and receive a specific end date for their authorised stay. Therefore, universities are urging students to complete international travel before the deadline. The Ohio State University warned students that the new system could affect the length of their authorised stay and their ability to complete their academic programmes without seeking an extension. It advised students to review their individual circumstances and consult the university’s international office before travelling. San José State University also highlighted the potential disruption for international students. Another important change concerns the grace period after completion of an F-1 programme. Under the new rules, students will generally have 30 days, rather than the current 60 days, to remain in the US after completing their programme. The rules may also affect students who change educational programmes or institutions, transfer schools or require additional time to complete their studies. Students planning to use post-completion Optional Practical Training (OPT) or STEM OPT should therefore pay particular attention to the new requirements and seek advice from their university before making travel or academic decisions. The new policy has been challenged in the federal court. A coalition of higher-education organisations and labour groups filed a lawsuit seeking to block the rule and asked the court for a preliminary injunction. But unless a court blocks or delays implementation, universities are advising students to plan on the assumption that the September 15 deadline will remain in force. If international students are outside the US and intend to return for the fall semester, universities are urging them to return before September 15. They should check their passport, visa, Form I-20 or DS-2019 and I-94 before travelling and contact their university’s international student office if they expect to need additional time to complete their programme.
