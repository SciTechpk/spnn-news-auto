Research shows that from the years 2000 to 2020, conflicts between humans and four large mammals — elephants, rhinos, tigers and common leopards — claimed 887 human lives and led to the killing of 1,139 animals. Of these, common leopards were the leading cause of human deaths at an average of 19 deaths per year, and the species most frequently killed. To try to bring the conflict under control, Nepal and other countries with human-large predator conflicts are exploring a range of solutions. Nepal has three ecological zones: lowland dominated by Bengal tigers, mountainous regions where snow leopards roam free, and the vast mid-hill ruled by common leopards as the top predator. While protected areas in the lowlands and mountains receive dedicated conservation funding for their charismatic predators, the mid-hills are largely managed as community forests. The celebrated community forest program is a key reason for leopards’ thriving in the mid-hills. In the early 1990s, amid worsening floods and landslides linked to forest degradation, King Birendra handed over management of large swaths of forest to local communities so that they would have stakes in conservation, and benefit from timber and fruit harvests. Today, almost 47 per cent of Nepal is covered by forest patches. About a third of that area belongs to community forests, most of which are run by people living in the mid-hills. “But what the communities didn’t realise was they also restored habitats so that the top carnivores could return,” said Kedar Baral, a wildlife biologist for Nepal’s Bagmati province. In 2020, Baral’s team conducted an analysis of leopard scat collected in the mid-hills that showed that 27pc of the animals’ diet consisted of domestic animals, while the wild ungulates that big cats typically prefer to hunt, like barking deer and wild boars, made up only 10pc. In search of sprouting leaves, barking deer venture from the dense community forests to farmland, and “as the deer enter the farmlands, that’s where the leopard follows,” says Baral, bringing them into contact with domestic livestock. Nepal’s hydropower and construction projects are also expanding into remote forested areas at higher elevations, disturbing habitat and bringing workers as well as a new hunting pressure against top predators, including common leopards, living there. That forces the leopards and their prey to leave their territory, says Madhu Chetri, a wildlife ecologist at the National Trust for Nature Conservation. Climate change might be influencing conflict patterns, too. While he was working as a district forest officer in Nepal’s western mid-hills, Baral and his team were repeatedly called to respond to common leopard attacks on local communities. Over time, he noticed a change in the pattern: Conflict incidents and human or livestock deaths were increasingly reported from higher-elevation settlements. Reporting in 2023, Baral and colleagues investigated whether climate change was driving this shift. Using data from 343 sites across Nepal based on signs of leopard scat, animal tracks and kills, he modeled habitat preferences of leopards, considering factors such as temperature, rainfall and elevation. The results suggest that leopards prefer mid-hill conditions at around 1,000 meters with moderate temperatures and high rainfall. Warming climates are likely expanding the range of suitable habitat uphill, which could allow the leopard population to grow and, as a result, have a higher risk of conflict with humans. Of course, it is a conservation success to be celebrated when numbers of threatened or endangered large carnivores increase — whether they be lynx, wolves, tigers or leopards — but it also presents new challenges, says Philip Nyhus, a conservation biologist at Colby College in Maine. Solutions to the problem have two parts, he adds: first, to keep wildlife separate from people and their livestock to prevent conflict to begin with, and second, to mitigate impacts when conflicts do occur. “A lot of attention has been given to the second piece,” he says. “What would be better is if we could prevent conflict from happening in the first place.” There are international examples to draw from. In the Argentinian Dry Chaco forest, for example, small households in woody areas have faced frequent attacks by pumas on sheep and goats. Sofia Nanni, a conservation biologist at the National Scientific and Technical Research Council of Argentina, worked with locals to come up with the idea of trying spiked leather collars on livestock, an approach that had proved successful in reducing leopard attacks on cows in countries like Iran. “We found a local artisan who made collars for dogs and we asked him if he could make collars,” Nanni says. Tracking collared goats and sheep for two years, her team found that the puma predation in uncollared animal groups was 10 times higher than that in collared groups. Several other strategies are successfully reducing leopard and other top predator conflicts globally. Still, changing attitudes and implementing effective interventions, as well as better compensation, can take years, while frustration among affected communities continues to grow.
