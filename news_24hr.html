Pakistan warns UN against weakening peacekeeping amid budget cuts

UNITED NATIONS: Pakistan has urged the United Nations not to allow financial constraints and cost-cutting measures to weaken its peacekeeping operations, warning that reductions in resources could undermine the organisation’s ability to maintain international peace and security. The warning comes as UN peacekeeping faces mounting financial and political pressures. The General Assembly has approved a $5.1 billion budget for peacekeeping operations for 2026-27, about 10 per cent lower than the previous year’s allocation. A funding crisis last year had already forced the organisation to reduce mission expenditure and repatriate a quarter of its uniformed personnel from peacekeeping operations. Against this backdrop, Pakistan’s Permanent Representative, Ambassador Syed Haider Shah, called for reforms that improve the effectiveness of peacekeeping without sacrificing the capabilities needed to carry out its mandates. “What is needed is the political will in the Security Council to use this important instrument when circumstances require,” he told the Fourth Committee of the 81st session of the UN General Assembly on Friday. Pakistan is one of the largest and longest-serving contributors to UN peacekeeping. More than 170,000 Pakistani military and police personnel have served in UN missions over the past six decades, with 183 losing their lives. Pakistan also hosts the United Nations Military Observer Group in India and Pakistan (UNMOGIP). Ambassador Shah’s intervention comes at a time when peacekeeping operations are confronting not only financial difficulties but also increasingly complicated political and security environments. Growing reliance on regional arrangements and ad hoc operations, he argued, demonstrated that the need for a credible UN presence on the ground remained undiminished. Welcoming the secretary-general’s review of peacekeeping operations, Pakistan outlined six priorities for reform: giving primacy to political solutions; ensuring focused and adequately resourced mandates; preserving the institutional strengths of peacekeeping; preparing for future military and police requirements; improving the safety and security of peacekeepers; and maintaining peacekeeping as a collective UN responsibility. The central concern was the widening gap between the responsibilities assigned to peacekeeping missions and the resources made available to them. Pakistan cautioned against allowing financial ceilings to determine operational requirements. Political objectives, Ambassador Shah argued, should first establish what a mission is expected to accomplish. Its mandate should then determine the personnel, equipment and financial resources required. Peacekeeping missions can help stabilise conflict situations, protect civilians, sustain ceasefires and preserve space for negotiations. But these functions depend on political processes and adequate operational capabilities working together. Pakistan also urged member states to preserve the distinctive advantages of UN peacekeeping, including its universal legitimacy, predictable financing, established command and logistics systems, and ability to draw personnel and specialised capabilities from across the UN membership. “Efficiency and cost reduction must therefore strengthen peacekeeping, not hollow out the capabilities that give UN peacekeeping its unique value,” the ambassador said. The protection of peacekeepers and accountability for crimes committed against them formed another major part of Pakistan’s intervention. Pakistan, alongside Denmark, played a leading role in advancing UN Security Council Resolution 2823, which was unanimously adopted with a record 153 co-sponsors. The resolution seeks to strengthen accountability for crimes against UN peacekeepers, an issue Pakistan has repeatedly raised at the UN. Welcoming the secretary-general’s first report on accountability for crimes against peacekeepers, Ambassador Shah also noted the appointment of Assistant Secretary-General Elizabeth Spehar as Senior Focal Point on the matter. He called for the resolution’s effective implementation through stronger investigations and prosecutions, better tracking of cases and greater support for national capacities to ensure that those responsible for attacks on peacekeepers were brought to justice. Pakistan also stressed the importance of the Special Committee on Peacekeeping Operations, known as C-34, describing it as the universal intergovernmental forum for collectively shaping peacekeeping policy. The consensus achieved within the committee, together with the broad support for Resolution 2823, demonstrated that member states continued to regard peacekeeping as an important instrument of international cooperation. Pakistan’s position was that reform should make peacekeeping more effective and adaptable to changing circumstances, rather than weaken the institution at a time when international conflicts and political divisions make its role increasingly important. “Peacekeeping remains a vital, effective and cost-efficient means for ensuring peace and security. We will need this instrument again. We should ensure that it is ready when we do,” Ambassador Shah concluded.

Exchange rate’s woeful tale

THE preoccupation of exchange rate fundamentalists in Pakistan with a single, partial indicator — Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) — is astonishing. As Nadeem ul Haque argued in an illuminating article, fixing or deliberately boosting exchange rates effectively ended with Bretton Woods’ collapse in the 1970s. Research has since confirmed that the exchange rate is a market price — the price of one currency in terms of another, determined by underlying economic conditions — and not a policy tool. Hence, the exchange rate is an endogenous variable reflecting domestic inflation, productivity, external balances, capital flows, reserve adequacy, fiscal credibility, interest-rate differentials, and market expectations. As Haque notes, “The rupee does not weaken because someone decides to weaken it. It weakens because underlying conditions make the existing price unsustainable.” REER has a legitimate but limited purpose. It measures changes in the nominal exchange rate adjusted for relative price movements against trading partners. But an index reading of 100, based on a selected base year, doesn’t establish an optimal exchange rate. Nor does a deviation from 100 automatically signal overvaluation or undervaluation. REER says little about balance-of-payments sustainability, foreign exchange availability from non-trade sources or exporters’ ability to compete through quality, technology, productivity, and delivery. There are questions to address before treating REER as an authoritative guide to exchange rate policy. First, does the base year 2010 represent an equilibrium exchange rate? Pakistan recorded a current account deficit of $3.95 billion, around two per cent of GDP, that year, compared with a near-balanced account in 2026. Second, have trade weights and trading partner composition remained representative over 16 years, especially after the China FTA? Third, have movements in individual partners’ currencies been adequately reflected in an index referencing the US dollar? Fourth, the choice of price deflator (CPI, PPI, unit labour costs or GDP deflator) can produce very different results. These points need serious thought before prescriptions are based on a single index. REER doesn’t capture many of the external account components. Workers’ remittances have grown faster than merchandise exports. They’re a major source of foreign exchange, financing imports of raw material, equipment and fuel, enabling domestic industry to use productive capacity that would otherwise be underemployed. They help build reserves, boost external stability, and reduce reliance on borrowing. FDI, portfolio investment, and external debt disbursements provide more foreign exchange liquidity. Assessments limited to REER overlook these determinants of external sustainability. Questions must be addressed before treating REER as an authoritative guide to exchange rate policy. Three empirical tests help establish whether the exchange rate is artificially managed or broadly market-determined. The first is the differential between official interbank and open-market rates. It peaked at Rs10-15 per dollar in FY2023. As reserves accumulated and market conditions normalised, the gap narrowed to its customary Rs0.50-2 range, indicating a more unified foreign exchange market. The second test is whether administrative restrictions on imports and profit and dividend repatriation are being used to suppress foreign exchange demand. Such curbs were imposed in FY2023 when SBP reserves fell to $3bn or so. Imports declined to $53bn and profit and dividend repatriation to just $331 million against normal levels of about $70bn and $2bn. Last fiscal, imports recovered to $71bn. Profit and dividend remittances reached $2.3bn. Arrears were cleared and current obligations met. This restoration hardly supports the view that the exchange rate is being sustained through administrative demand suppression. The third, perhaps most compelling, test is whether SBP has depleted its reserves to defend a parity. In FY2023, reserves fell to about $3bn recovering to around $21bn despite annual external debt-servicing dues of about $25bn. This accumulation occurred while the rate was around Rs280 per dollar and REER above 100. It’s difficult to reconcile this outcome with the claim that an artificial exchange rate is being maintained when SBP is mopping up surplus dollar liquidity after interbank clearance. Historical comparisons reinforce this view. Bet­ween 2015 and 2018, the rupee was maintained aro­­und Rs104 per dollar, but SBP reserves dec­li­ned from $18bn to $9bn. In contrast, in 2000-2007 when reserves increased from barely $1bn to $13bn, the exchange rate remained stable around Rs60 and exports more than doubled from $9bn to $19bn. Still, critics said the rate was artificially defended, overlooking the net foreign exchange inflows being sterilised to prevent excessive appreciation and protect exporters’ competitiveness. Market expectations are another important aspect. In Pakistan, they are often driven by a bandwagon effect instead of rational assessments of fundamentals. When reserves fall below comfortable levels, depreciation expectations can become self-fulfilling. Exporters withhold dollar proceeds within the permitted 90-day surrender period, anticipating a better rate; importers advance foreign exchange requirements to avoid future depreciation. Supply contracts and demand rises putting pressure on the rupee. The anticipated depreciation then materialises. Would a 10pc depreciation advocated by REER fundamentalists improve competitiveness? Foreign buyers may demand price discounts of at least 5pc from exporters, treating additional rupee earnings as a windfall. About 40pc of exports use imported inputs including fuel, LNG, and textile raw materials. Their costs rise, leaving exporters with a net gain of perhaps only 2-3pc. The impact goes beyond exporters. Costlier imports fuel inflation, hurting poorer households whose main asset is their labour and whose wages, particularly in an informal economy with extensive underemployment, do not adjust proportionately. The central bank may raise interest rates, increasing domestic borrowing costs. Imported machinery becomes costlier, forcing investors to put off or abandon projects, hurting both investment and growth. As the rupee cost of servicing external debt increases, partly offset by higher import-duty collections, government may have to borrow more, crowding out private-sector credit. Foreign investors earn lower dollar returns, discouraging fresh investment. The lesson is that exchange-rate stability can’t be engineered through administrative intervention or depreciation alone. It must rest on credible fiscal management, a broader tax base, sound trade and tariff policies, remunerative energy pricing, higher productivity and prudent monetary management. Together, these policies reduce the need for disruptive exchange-rate adjustments. Pakistan must not treat REER as a magic number and depreciation as a remedy. The aim should not be an arbitrary index reading but sustainable external competitiveness, stronger reserves, higher exports and investment, and protection of purchasing power. The exchange rate is a reflection of economic fundamentals, not a substitute for them. Correcting symptoms while neglecting underlying weaknesses will only perpetuate the rupee’s woeful tale. The debt burden

PAKISTAN’S external debt has nearly doubled in a decade — from $68.7bn in 2015 to $135.1bn in 2025 — according to a new World Bank report. Public and publicly guaranteed debt accounts for $107.1bn of the total, private debt for $17.6bn, and short-term borrowing for $10.45bn. China is the largest single creditor with $22.4bn outstanding, followed by the World Bank and Asian Development Bank. The rise itself is not surprising but the pace is striking. Much of this borrowing went towards plugging external financing gaps, propping up reserves, and repaying older loans to keep the economy running. The debt spike describes an economy that invests, produces and earns too little to service what it owes. Debt has become the main constraint on our fiscal and economic prospects. Servicing debt eats up meagre financial resources, which should be expanding productive capacity for exports, and makes faster growth harder to reach. That leads to more borrowing. The government borrows to cover consumption, budget deficits, and external payments — not to build anything that pays for itself. Development spending is usually the first thing to be cut, and growth has crawled along at 2.7pc in recent years. There is nothing wrong with borrowing. Developing economies need foreign capital for infrastructure, energy, technology, and industry. The trouble starts when loans do not raise domestic productivity or lift exports. Successive governments have failed to tell apart borrowing that builds capacity from borrowing that only buys time. Pakistan’s recurring balance-of-payments crisis underscores this fact. The country’s exports are weak, the production base is narrow, investment is low, and fiscal deficits refuse to go away. So, the economy keeps relying on foreign and domestic loans. Remittances, rollovers by friendly countries, and one IMF programme after another may have staved off immediate default, but none of them has addressed the underlying problem. It does not matter much if the country can raise another loan or defer another repayment. What matters is whether the country can generate enough growth, tax revenue, and foreign exchange to meet its obligations without sacrificing its development. The situation calls for a credible, short- to long-term strategy to invest in projects that raise productivity, exports, and foreign exchange earnings. The strategy must be accompanied by wider reforms aimed at expansion of the tax base, more productive public spending, and lower costs of doing business. Exporters need energy that is reliable and competitively priced, policies they can predict, and access to modern technology. Without these, even fresh foreign investment and cheaper credit will yield little value, growth will remain anaemic, children stunted, and communities without basic services such as clean drinking water. LHC rejects plea for directions to legislation on marital rape

LAHORE: The Lahore High Court (LHC) has dismissed a public interest petition seeking directions to the federal and provincial governments to legislate on marital rape, holding that a constitutional court cannot compel the legislature to enact a particular law or create a new offence. Justice Abher Gul Khan clarified that the ruling neither endorses violence or abuse within marriage, nor prevents the Parliament or any provincial assembly from taking up the matter. Areej Sohail Bhutta, a lawyer, filed the petition, stating that she had encountered women clients who complained of non-consensual sexual conduct within their marriages. She argued that the law lacked a distinct statutory framework for sexual violence within marriage. She sought directions for legislative measures, including amendments to sections 375 and 376 of the Pakistan Penal Code. A court can’t force the legislature to enact a law or create an offence, observes the judge Justice Khan observed that the Constitution divides powers among the legislature, the executive and the judiciary, and that judicial review does not authorise courts to assume legislative functions. She said Article 199 empowers the high court to issue directions for the enforcement of fundamental rights. However, she maintained, there is a distinction between directing an authority to perform an existing constitutional or statutory obligation and compelling the legislature to enact a particular law or create a new offence. The judge noted that articles 9, 14 and 25 protect the security, dignity and equality of a person, while articles 25(3) and 34 allow special protections for women and promote their participation in national life. She said Article 35, which requires the state to protect marriage and the family, does not provide a licence for violence against a spouse. Justice Khan observed that foreign laws can provide comparative guidance, but cannot override Pakistan’s Constitution or replace the judgment of its legislature. She said any proposed legal framework also has to be examined in the light of the Constitution, existing laws and the injunctions of Islam. The judge noted that under Article 142(b), the Parliament and provincial assemblies are competent to legislate on criminal law, procedure and evidence. “The question whether an additional offence, a separate statutory provision or a further protective mechanism is required, is essentially a matter of legislative policy,” the judge observed. Citing Supreme Court judgments, including Tika Iqbal Muhammad Khan versus Pervez Musharraf (PLJ 2008 SC 500), the judge observed, “A court cannot issue a mandamus compelling the legislature to enact a particular law.” The judge noted that the petitioner had neither challenged a specific statutory provision as unconstitutional nor identified an authority’s failure to perform an existing legal duty. She said the relief sought would require the court to direct legislation according to a model proposed by the petitioner, amounting to interference in the legislative process. The judge stressed that the decision does not diminish a woman’s dignity or bodily integrity because of marriage, or endorse coercion and abuse within marriage.
