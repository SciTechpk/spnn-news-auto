Rule of the people?

THE resounding defeat of the Republican Party in the US midterm election this week has triggered celebratory chatter around the world. Many see it as the beginning of the end for Donald Trump; the vibe was very similar when Trump was defeated by Joe Biden in the 2020 US presidential election. Yet four years later, Trumpism proved to be alive and very well. Trump cannot stand in the 2028 presidential election. For that reason alone, his political career is reaching its end. But that does not mean that Trumpism — both the ideology and the social base to which it speaks — will disappear alongside the man himself. Here I offer some suggestions what this latest election signals about the future of democracy in the US, and, by extension, around the world. We should harbour no illusions about the power of big money in American politics, or the Democratic Party’s historical commitment to US empire — the Biden administration’s diabolical warmongering after Oct 7, 2023, in Gaza, after all, set the stage for Trump’s 2024 win. Given this backdrop, the victory of candidates like Abdul el-Sayed who oppose imperialist wars abroad and are fronting progressive economic policies at home — just like Zohran Mamdani did during his successful campaign to become New York mayor — confirms that societal attitudes are changing. A growing number of young Americans now view socialism favourably and want unconditional support for Israel to be discontinued. It is another matter entirely whether an increasingly influential left-wing of the Democratic Party can win the inner-party struggle and then the presidency. The contemporary state is whittling away democratic freedoms. While progressive ideology has gained a foothold in American society, the Trumpist right maintains its powerful, and mobilised MAGA base, sustained in part by Christian evangelicals. Before his win in 2024, Trump vowed to stop fighting wars abroad, and bring back industrial jobs and prosperity. His base has been antagonised by the disastrous and prolonged war on Iran, and the inflationary spiral it has triggered. Vice-President J.D. Vance has already acknowledged this, and even added that Washington’s ‘special relationship’ with Israel must change. Trumpism will not die out because of a midterm defeat. Long-term economic stagnation will give it social mooring, while its major ideological enemies — immigration, the left, and outsourcing — remain easy sells. A useful analogy is that of Brazil, where Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva recently staved off the most recent articulation of Bolsanarismo — the parallel ideology to Trumpism — to win his fourth term as president. Brazil’s left has far greater social roots than the emergent Democratic left in the US, hence its ability to mobilise the last-minute votes necessary to re-elect Lula. But the far right under the leadership of the Bolsanaro family also has a mobilised social base, which will rebound from the election loss. Lula is 81, and his Workers Party will have to deepen the social struggle to keep Bolsanarismo at bay in the years to come. Zooming out further, the right wing is ascendant across much of the world, while the contemporary state is whittling away democratic freedoms and paving the way for capital to carry on with business as usual. Electoral victories for left-of-centre candidates in the US, Brazil or wherever else they may come to pass will not produce structural change in isolation. Does going to the ballot allow ordinary people an opportunity to exercise political choice? Yes. But this is not the same as working-class majorities being organised for progressive social transformation. The latter is re­­quired both to ensure more significant electo­r­­al success and then to turn such victories into su­­bstantive cha­­­nge. Of course, we in Pakistan do not even get to enjoy the unfettered right to vote. The military-bureaucratic oligarchy that has ruled over us from soon after 1947 did not even allow a general election until 1970, and then chose military action rather than handing over power to the winning party afterwards. More recently, the electoral mandate in the 2024 general election was stolen blatantly, culminating in the imposition of emergency in KP. There is something to be said for at least the temporary waves of hope that are generated by electoral victories, even if one should at the same time be conscious of the highly delimited nature of democracy under the rule of contemporary capital. Progressives in Pakistan have to fight an epic battle against tyranny to even retain the pretence that democracy means rule of the people. And then we have to come up with a shared project for how to run a country ravaged by violence, repression and a deep crisis of identity. The writer teaches at Quaid-i-Azam University, Islamabad. A bleak economic future

ROBERT Lucas, a towering figure of 20th-century macroeconomics and a Nobel laureate, opined on economic growth and the standard of living that “the consequences for human welfare involved in questions like these are simply staggering: once one starts to think about them, it is hard to think about anything else” (On the Mechanics of Economic Development, 1988). The context of his observation was the significant differences in standards of living across the globe. I contemplated the same — specifically, whether our economic future might offer us some respite from our perennial failures. Sadly, it does not look good. What follows is a brief narration of various considerations that led me to this conclusion. The incentives and structure of the economy resemble anything but a viable plan for sustained improvement in living standards and chances of shared prosperity. Tellingly, Pakistan now has the unique distinction of being the only country in the world where three different arrangements compete for a limited set of resources. The first is the formal sector, which mainly comprises an extremely unproductive and extractive public sector. The second is the ‘deep state’, growing within the bosom of the public sector (formally and informally) and meeting its growing economic interests through forced ingress. The third is the ‘informal’ sector, estimated at about 35 to 40 per cent of the formal economy, that tries its best to stay away from formality. The implications of continuing this arrangement now and in the future are not well understood. There is little, if any, chance, for example, of an entrepreneur arising who could upend traditional industries and usher in economies of scale or creative disruption. It follows, then, that we will remain stuck with low-productivity, rentier industries like sugar that rely on legislative rents (taxpayer-financed subsidies) and tariff protection to shield them from competition. The incentives and structure of the economy resemble anything but a viable plan for sustained improvement in living standards. An even more damning predicament is the continuation of economic management as merely an accounting exercise to balance the books. From having some of the worst development indicators to the absurdity of taking foreign loans for purposes such as pension reforms, the adverse spillovers of this mediocrity are well known. Yet one is astonished to see such pedestrian management celebrated officially. Witness the bewildering displays of vainglorious puffery under the banner of ‘nationalism’. At the same time, trade with three of our neighbours is almost non-existent, a situation akin to a contraction of the available resource envelope and growth possibilities. This attitude helps explain why we, the world’s fifth most populous country, account for less than 1pc of the massive $35 trillion global trade in goods and services. What we have done to our human capital is another indicator of our dim prospects. Since at least the time of Adam Smith’s seminal work (1776), it has been understood that it is the most valuable input in the process of economic growth, something that Paul Romer’s endogenous growth theory (1990) put an authoritative stamp upon. Developing this input has always remained low on our priorities. An even more grievous spectre now haunts our economy: the flight of quality human capital. It is officially celebrated as an achievement in the context of remittances. Still, no policymaker is willing to realise that the full value of quality human capital is captured outside Pakistan. Think of the innumerable examples of people who have made a name for themselves abroad. What if they had remained here and invested their time, resources, and effort to create wealth at home? Or are we just happy to receive a pittance of that effort in the form of remittances that merely sustain consumption? The ‘Lucas critique’ of the 1970s, which transformed macroeconomics, implies that people respond to policy regimes by revising their expectations. The steady flight of skilled people from Pakistan, a trend since the 1970s, has accelerated sharply in recent decades, suggesting that citizens’ expectations of the country’s future have turned deeply pessimistic. This is reflected in what young people think. Having taught at several universities, I can attest that the majority of students view their education as a stepping stone to settling abroad. For those who cannot make it to foreign shores and are active in the workforce, a difficult predicament under a poorly designed incentive structure awaits. Try justifying, logically, how in a country with one of the lowest labour force participation rates, the salaried class gets bilked for more than Rs2tr of its income in the last five years, while record inflation has decimated purchasing power for the majority. The obvious lesson (which then spills over into expectations) is that the main beneficiary of workers’ effort is the government’s coffers rather than the person. Better then to try one’s luck elsewhere. This hollowing out, which will continue, leaves the public sector and its PSDPs as the major avenue of growth. This system spawns corruption, multi-trillion liabilities, and waste (the burden of which ultimately falls upon the taxpayer). Lahore is the most pertinent example among Pakistani cities, with the second-largest economy and one of the highest per capita incomes. Yet it regularly tops the world’s most polluted city rankings rather than quality-of-life rankings, thanks mainly to brick-and-mortar-based PSDPs. Any material gains are more than offset by deteriorating air quality and rising health costs. I can continue in this manner — for instance, by pointing out that the legislature is held hostage by the same people who have been there for at least three decades, with no meaningful economic reform emerging during their tenure. It is further compromised by their progeny, who inhabit both Houses. But I am sure the reader gets the gist of my argument: the chances that we will witness a significant turnaround in our economic fortunes are remote. We will likely remain saddled with a state whose main aim, as Marx described it, is ‘nothing else’ other than extracting taxes. At least that’s how I see it. Bob Lucas, I posit, would also have nodded in approval. The writer is an economist. His current research focuses on the long-term analysis of various issues plaguing Pakistan’s economy, as well as economic reforms and the history of economic thought. Country’s external debt nearly doubles in a decade

• World Bank’s global debt report shows figure climbed from $68.7bn in 2015 to $135.1bn in 2025• China remains Pakistan’s largest single creditor• Developing economies’ external debt rises to record high of $9.4tr ISLAMABAD: Pakistan’s total external debt almost doubled to $135.1 billion over the decade ending in 2025, according to the World Bank’s Inter­national Debt Report 2026. The report noted that Pakistan’s external debt increased from $68.673bn in 2015 to $135.107bn in 2025. This included public and publicly guaranteed debt of $107.076bn, private non-guaranteed debt of $17.58bn and short-term debt of $10.45bn. Of the public and publicly guaranteed debt, $56.83bn was owed to multilateral creditors, $42.43bn to bilateral creditors and $7.8bn to private creditors. China was Pakistan’s largest individual creditor, with $22.4bn outstanding, followed by the World Bank with $19.2bn and the Asian Development Bank (ADB) with $16.83bn. The report put the average maturity of loans from official creditors at 16 years, with a four-year grace period and an average interest rate of 3.6 per cent. On the other hand, private credit carried an average interest rate of 8.6pc, with a three-year repayment period and a one-year grace period. The Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan and Pakistan (Menap) region recorded improvements in debt-service and interest ratios. However, its external debt-to-gross national income (GNI) ratio rose from 26.7pc to 29.4pc in 2025, the largest single-year increase of any region, indicating continued debt accumulation. In 2025, China extended the maturities on $2bn in loans to Pakistan, while the International Monetary Fund (IMF) provided $2.3bn in new disbursements under the Extended Fund Facility and Resilience and Sustainability Facility arrangements to support the country’s long-term economic stability. Global debt at record high Overall, the borrowing by low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) surged in 2025, marking the sharpest increase in external debt in four years and a forceful return of private creditors to developing country markets. External debt climbed 5.5pc to a record $9.4 trillion as bond issues rebounded, the US dollar weakened and investors again showed greater willingness to lend. But the reopening of capital markets came at a steep price, especially for the poorest countries, which faced some of their highest borrowing costs in decades. Debt service payments also rose for a fifth consecutive year. Even so, stronger economic growth helped stabilise some debt ratios and net long-term debt transfers turned positive after years of outflows. The increase in external debt was even faster for countries eligible for International Development Association (IDA) financing, rising 8.1pc to $1.3tr, and for low-income countries, climbing 8.9pc to $262.4bn, the biggest increase since 2020. A weakening dollar amplified the rise in debt. The dramatic depreciation of the US dollar during the year accounted for 28.2pc of the increase in LMICs’ external public and publicly guaranteed debt. For several of the largest borrowers from concessional windows, currency valuation gains in 2025 not only offset principal payments made during the year but also, in some cases, partially reversed the stock reductions achieved through repayments in 2024, leaving reported balances higher at end-2025 despite continued debt servicing. Private lending, which is more susceptible to fluctuations in financial market conditions, pricing conditions, and perceived creditworthiness of borrowers, saw a strong rebound in 2025. LMIC debt stock owed to private creditors increased 5.2pc in 2025 to $5tr, a significant acceleration from the 0.6pc increase recorded in 2024. This increase was due to a combined increase in debt owed to bondholders and debt owed to commercial banks and other private creditors, which increased by 5.7pc (to $2.4tr) and 4.7pc (to $2.7tr), respectively. LMIC economies, measured by gross national income, grew by 5.1pc. This strong economic growth encouraged lenders to return to these countries, and many LMIC governments were willing to borrow despite relatively high interest rates. The countries that can least afford it are paying the most to borrow. Increased borrowing from private creditors hardened lending terms disproportionately for IDA-eligible countries, which faced an average interest rate of 8.2pc on new commitments during the year, the highest since the late 1980s. The difference in interest rates that this group of borrowers pays when borrowing from official creditors and rates on debt from private creditors widened significantly over the past decade, from 3.4 percentage points in 2016 to 5.5 percentage points in 2025. International Bank for Reconstruction and Development borrowers, by contrast, saw improvements in the terms on new commitments, with an improved 5.5pc average interest rate, down from 5.8pc the previous year. The report noted that the debt service burdens remain a pressure point for LMICs. Total debt service payments (interest plus principal) rose for the fifth year in a row, increasing 1.9pc to $1.4tr in 2025. Payments to private creditors drove the increase, with payments to bondholders rising 13.7pc to $363.1bn. Between 2022 and 2025, LMICs paid about $436.1bn more in debt service than they received in new financing.
