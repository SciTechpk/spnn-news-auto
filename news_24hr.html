The case for smaller administrative units in the age of AI

Let us be honest about something Pakistan’s governance discourse rarely says plainly: political parties do not want local governments to work. This is not a failure of will or capacity. It is a deliberate choice, repeated by every party in every province across every cycle of government, regardless of ideology, coalition or electoral mandate. Local bodies are constituted late, funded poorly, stripped of administrative authority and dissolved early. The pattern is too consistent, too cross-partisan and too durable to be accidental. It is policy by other means. A rival power centre The reason is straightforward. A functioning local government is a rival power centre. It has an electoral base, a budget, a staff and a mandate to deliver services that citizens can see and touch. It can build a political constituency independent of the provincial party machine. For parties whose power rests on controlling the distribution of roads, postings, contracts and patronage from the provincial capital, that is an existential threat not to the state but to them. So they keep the tier alive enough to satisfy constitutional requirements and dead enough to pose no challenge. The local body hence becomes a holding cell for lower-tier party workers, not a governing institution. Waiting for parties to change this calculus is not a strategy. No party in power has ever voluntarily strengthened a tier of government that could constrain it. The few periods of active local government in Pakistan’s history: the 1960s Basic Democracies, the 2001 devolution under Musharraf and the post-18th Amendment councils were all driven by either military governments with no electoral stakes or by court orders that were subsequently ignored by political parties. Democratic parties have never, left to themselves, built strong local governments. Expecting them to do so now is to misread the incentive structure entirely. Meanwhile, the citizen’s need for governance does not wait. Sewage backs up. Schools sit without teachers. Basic registrations, certificates, and permits require travel to a city three hours away. Climate shocks, floods, heat waves and droughts demand local response capacity that no provincial capital can provide at speed. The gap between what people need and what the state delivers grows wider each year and the existing institutional architecture with four massive provinces, a thin federal tier and a local layer that exists mostly on paper cannot close it. The fix If parties will not allow local governments to function, the logic points toward a different structural fix; smaller administrative units. Not smaller local bodies within existing provinces, but the provinces themselves reconfigured or supplemented by administrative divisions drawn on rational, service-delivery grounds. Units small enough that a single administration can actually sense and respond to local conditions. Units where the district health officer knows the district and where the education planner is not managing the scale of a mid-sized European country from a single office building. The critical question is the basis on which these units are drawn. Ethnicity and language are the obvious political demands, but they are the wrong answer. Ethnic administrative boundaries encode group competition into the map permanently. Every resource allocation becomes a proxy for ethnic power. Every appointment is a signal about whose territory this is. Countries that draw administrative lines on ethnic logic spend their governance energy managing the boundary itself rather than delivering services within it. Pakistan, with its layered ethnic, linguistic, sectarian and tribal identities — many of which overlap, shift and are internally contested — cannot afford administrative units that calcify those identities into state structures. The alternative is ruthlessly administrative logic: population thresholds, geographic coherence, economic connectivity, river basin and infrastructure alignment and service delivery catchment areas. A unit that can support a tertiary hospital, a polytechnic institute, a land records office and a local court without borrowing capacity from a neighbouring unit. Boundaries drawn by asking what geography enables efficient governance, not by asking which group lives here. This is not ethnicity-blind; a sensible administration accounts for linguistic diversity in staffing and communication but it refuses to make ethnicity the organising principle of the state’s internal architecture. The tools are available This is where the twenty-first century offers Pakistan something it did not have in earlier rounds of administrative reform: the tools to actually run small units efficiently. Artificial intelligence, digital service delivery and real-time data platforms are collapsing the administrative cost of small-scale governance. A local administration no longer needs to physically house every function. AI-powered triage systems can route citizen complaints, allocate maintenance crews and flag anomalies in service delivery faster and more accurately than a provincial bureaucracy managing 10 million people from a single dashboard. Land records, business registrations, birth and death certificates and public grievance systems can all run on shared digital infrastructure, with the local unit as a point of contact rather than the processor. In an AI-assisted governance model, the case for large administrative units historically justified by economies of scale in bureaucratic capacity largely disappears. What you need at scale is a shared technology layer, shared standards and interoperable data systems. What you need locally is a small, elected or appointed administration with genuine authority, a budget tied to local revenue and population and accountability to the people who live there. The AI layer handles the complexity that used to require size. The local layer handles the judgment, the presence and the trust. Several countries are already moving in this direction. Estonia’s entire public administration operates at a scale: 1.3 million people that is comparable to a mid-sized Pakistani district and its digital governance stack is the backbone of service delivery rather than a supplement to bureaucracy. Rwanda reorganised its districts in 2006 on service-delivery logic, not ethnic logic after the catastrophic lesson of what ethnic administrative boundaries produce. South Korea’s smart city initiatives embed real-time sensing and AI-driven resource allocation at the municipal level. The direction is consistent: governance is becoming more local, more data-driven and less dependent on administrative mass. Pakistan’s current map is a colonial inheritance optimised for revenue extraction and security control, not for citizen service. The four-province structure, including Gilgit Baltistan and Azad Jammu and Kashmir, whatever its historical justification, was never designed around the question of what unit of government could best respond to a family that needs a functioning school, a clean water connection and a place to file a legal complaint. Answering that question now with the tools available in 2026 points toward smaller units, administrative boundaries and a digital backbone that gives those units the capacity that size used to provide. The parties will resist. They always do. But the argument for smaller, rationally drawn administrative units does not require party consent; it requires a constitutional conversation about what the state is for. Pakistan’s citizens have been patient with an architecture that was never built for them. The AI era doesn’t just make better governance possible. It makes the old excuses for not delivering it obsolete.

Rethinking governance in Pakistan

EVERY few years, Pakistan revisits the debate on creating new provinces. The interior minister’s recent proposal to establish smaller administrative units has again raised an important question. Yet the real issue is not how many provinces Pa­­kistan should have. It is whether government is close enough to serve its citizens effectively. Adm­inistrative boundaries matter only because they influence how quickly people receive education, healthcare, justice, infrastructure and disaster relief. If governance remains distant and unresponsive, merely redrawing maps won’t improve lives. With over 240 million people spread across nearly 796,000 square kilometres, Pakistan is one of the world’s largest federations. Its administrative challenges vary enormously — from the mountains of Gilgit-Baltistan to the deserts of Thar and the vast expanse of Balochistan. A governance model suited to Lahore or Karachi cannot automatically meet the needs of Kech, Upper Kohistan or Astore. The real challenge is one of state capacity rather than geography alone. Pakistan’s history reflects repeated efforts to balance national unity with effective administration. At independence in 1947, West Pakistan comprised Punjab, Sindh, the North-West Frontier Province, the Chief Commissioner’s Province of Balochistan, princely states — including Kalat, Bahawalpur and Swat — and the Tribal Areas. The 1955 One Unit scheme attempted to centralise administration; instead, it deepened regional grievances. Its abolition in 1970 and the federal framework established by the 1973 Constitution reaffirmed the importance of provincial autonomy. More recently, the merger of Fata into KP demonstrated that Pakistan’s administrative structure can evolve when necessary. Yet demands for South Punjab, Hazara and Bahawalpur continue because many citizens still feel distant from provincial capitals. The case for smaller administrative units is fundamentally administrative rather than political. Punjab’s population exceeds that of many countries, while Balochistan covers almost 44 per cent of Pakistan’s territory but contains only a small share of its population. Administering such contrasting regions through similar institutional arrangements limits efficiency. Have power, resources and decision-making been devolved enough to serve citizens effectively? Governments perform best when decision-makers are close to the communities they serve. Greater administrative proximity improves supervision of schools, hospitals, roads and development projects while strengthening transparency and public accountability. The devastating floods of 2022 illustrated the value of capable local institutions. Where local administrative capacity was stronger, relief reached affected communities more quickly. At the same time, creating new provinces is neither simple nor inexpensive. Constitutional ame­n­­dments require broad political consensus, while new provinces need legislatures, secretariats, ju­­d­­icial institutions and sustainable financial res­o­u­­rces. Without careful planning, administrative re­­organisation risks creating additional bureaucracy without significantly improving governance. International experience offers useful lessons. India has repeatedly created new states — including Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Uttarakhand and Telangana — to improve governance in historically neglected regions. Although disputes over assets and water accompanied these reforms, smaller states enabled governments to focus more closely on local priorities. Bangladesh adopted a different approach. Rather than creating provinces, it strengthened districts, upazilas and union parishads. Improvements in rural healthcare, primary education and disaster management owe much to effective local administration. Indonesia embarked on one of the world’s largest decentralisation programmes after reforms in 1998, transferring substantial authority and financial resources to districts and municipalities. Nigeria, by contrast, demonstrates the risks of expanding administrative units without fiscal sustainability; while political representation improved, the country developed an expensive state structure heavily dependent on federal revenues. France provides yet another model, devolving significant powers to departments and municipalities without repeatedly altering regional boundaries. Despite their different political systems, these countries illustrate one common principle: successful administrative reform depends less on the number of administrative units than on whether authority, financial resources and accountability actually reach local institutions. Ironically, Pakistan already possesses the constitutional foundation for this approach. Article 140A requires provinces to establish elected local governments and devolve political, administrative and financial responsibility to them. Yet successive governments have implemented this provision unevenly. Pakistan’s own experience supports stronger LG. The 2001 Local Government Plan transferred significant responsibilities for education, healthcare and local development to elected district governments. Although politically controversial, it demonstrated the benefits of bringing decision-making closer to citizens. After the 18th Amendment, provinces gained substantial autonomy from Islamabad, but meaningful decentralisation often stopped at the provincial level. Many districts and municipalities remained dependent on provincial governments for authority and funding. Thus, the debate over new provinces and LG should be seen as complementary rather than competing. Where there is broad public consensus, administrative necessity and economic viability, new provinces should remain a legitimate constitutional option. But such reforms must be guided by transparent criteria and accompanied by agreed arrangements for finance, water, civil services and transitional administration. At the same time, meaningful LG reform should begin immediately. Provinces should guarantee predictable financial transfers to elected LGs, devolve responsibility for primary education, basic healthcare, water supply, sanitation and local infrastructure, conduct regular local elections, and protect local institutions from arbitrary dissolution. Devolution without financial autonomy cannot succeed. For nearly eight decades, Pakistan has alternated between centralisation and decentralisation, often expecting structural changes alone to resolve governance failures. Experience at home and abroad suggests otherwise. Institutions matter more than boundaries. The purpose of administrative reform is not to redraw maps but to improve lives. When a child in Rajanpur, Tharpar­kar, Upper Kohistan, Kech, Astore or Neelum Valley enjoys the same access to education and healthcare as one in Lahore, Karachi, Peshawar, Quetta or Islamabad; when a mother in Dera Bugti can obtain timely medical care; and when citizens across Punjab, Sindh, KP, Balochistan, GB and Azad Kashmir experience responsive public institutions, Pakistan will know that administrative reform has achieved its purpose. A federation’s true strength is measured not by the number of provinces on its map, but by the confidence of its citizens that their government is never too distant to serve them.
