Lahore DC gives formal go-ahead for 3-day Basant festivities in March 2027, outlines conditions and restrictions

LAHORE: The Lahore deputy commissioner (DC) on Friday gave the formal go-ahead for three-day Basant festivities in the district from March 12-14 next year, outlining guidelines, conditions and restrictions for the celebrations. Basant returned to Lahore earlier this year after an 18-year hiatus, with the passing of the Punjab Regulation of Kite Flying Bill, 2025 by the provincial assembly on December 24, 2025. The legislation authorised deputy commissioners to grant conditional permission for kite-flying — which otherwise remains banned in Punjab — on specified days and at designated locations. In August, Punjab Chief Minister Maryam Nawaz announced that the festival would next be celebrated from March 12-14. On Friday, the decision was formalised in notification by the Lahore DC. The notification, a copy of which is available with Dawn, said that kite-flying would be allowed in Lahore during Basant 2027 except at designated prohibited zones and subject to certain conditions. “Kite-flying through the use of permissible kite-flying material, as specified in Schedule-I of the Punjab Regulation of Kite Flying Rules, 2025 and Punjab Kite Flying Regulations, 2025 shall be allowed from March 12-14, 2027,” it added. The notification said the manufacturing of permissible kite-flying material would be allowed from Sept 5, 2026 to March 14, 2027, subject to valid digital registration. Trade, including the purchase, transportation and storage of the material, will be permitted from Feb 1, 2027 to March 14, 2027 while sales to the general public will be allowed from Feb 10, 2027 to March 14, 2027, exclusively through registered sellers. The notification further said that no manufacturer, trader, or seller should manufacture, trade, store or sell permissible kite-flying material unless registered with the deputy commissioner through the government’s eBiz application. “Patang (kite) shall not exceed four-and-a-half githi (span of a hand) and 35 inches in width and 30 inches in length,” it said. “Gudda (a large kite) shall not exceed one-and-a-half tawa and 40 inches in width and 34 inches in length,” the notification added. The kite string, known as dor, shall only be wound in the shape of pinna, it must be made of cotton with no more than nine taars (threads) and no fewer than 28 counts and shall be coated with maanjha having a minimum sheesha size of 350 mesh. “Preparation, coating, treatment or processing of dor or maanjha shall be strictly restricted to maya (laundry starch), 350-mesh glass powder and only rice flour, and no other material, chemical, adhesive, hardener, abrasive, metallic substance or strengthening agent, including rema tip top, amri powder or any other material having similar properties or effect, whether used individually or in combination with the permitted material shall be added, mixed or applied at any stage,” the notification said. The DC also prohibited the manufacture, trading, transportation, storage, sale, possession and use of charkhi, sharp maanjha, metallic wire, nylon (plastic) dandi, and any other thread coated with sharp maanjha or any other injurious material. Registered manufacturers, traders and sellers will be required to “conspicuously” display their valid registration certificates at approved premises for kite-flying. “Complete stock, sales and movement records shall be maintained and produced on demand by registered manufacturers, traders and sellers to any authorised officer of the deputy commissioner.” The notification further said that all registered kite-flying associations should comply with the functions assigned under the Punjab Regulation of Kite Flying Rules, 2025 and remain in close coordination with the deputy commissioner and his authorised officers for smooth and safe conduct of kite-flying. Moreover, motorcyclists in Lahore would be required to comply with all the safety measures, including the installation of safety rods, as specified in Chapter-III of the Punjab Kite Flying Regulations 2025 and guidelines issued by the Punjab government secretary and the Home Department under the kite flying law. “All departments, attached departments, agencies and government organisations shall assist the district administration and police for strict compliance of [the] terms and conditions to ensure public safety,” the notification said. According to the notification, kite-flying will not be permitted in the following areas of Lahore during Basant: Aircraft approach path Nadirabad Gulshan Ali Colony Nishat Colony Bhatta Chowk DHA Lahore (blocks R, S, P and Q) Aircraft take-off path Al-Faisal Town Jorraypul Canal Bank Road (adjacent to tajoura) Tajpura Basant had been banned in 2007 because of an increasing number of deaths and serious injuries caused by sharp strings, particularly to motorcyclists and pillion riders, as well as by celebratory gunfire. Rising flour prices fuel weekly inflation

ISLAMABAD: Short-term inflation rose to 11.85 per cent year-on-year in the week ending October 22, driven largely by wheat flour prices, according to official data released by the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics (PBS) on Friday. The index, which comprises 51 items whose prices are collected from 50 markets in 17 cities, is calculated weekly to track changes in the prices of essential commodities and services. Short-term inflation has remained in double digits for several consecutive weeks, largely on the back of persistent increases in petroleum prices. The data showed that of the 51 essential items monitored during the week, the prices of 19 commodities (37.72pc) increased, while those of 12 items (23.53pc) declined. Meanwhile, the prices of 20 items (39.22pc) remained unchanged from the previous week. The Sensitive Price Index (SPI), which tracks short-term inflation, slightly increased by 0.65pc week-on-week, reflecting price fluctuations in both food and non-food items. Unwilling to ease the petroleum levy, the government has continued to raise petrol and diesel prices on a daily basis. The surge in energy costs is not only fuelling inflationary pressures but also indirectly boosting sales tax collections through higher prices of goods and services. This is cost-push inflation, in which rising energy costs are followed by increases in food prices. As a result, households are under pressure from higher consumer prices. The items whose prices increased the most over the previous week included liquefied petroleum gas (22.44pc), chicken (12.20pc), onions (4.19pc), shirting (1.86pc), plain bread (1.66pc), wheat flour (1pc), garlic (0.74pc), firewood (0.71pc), georgette and one kilogramme of vegetable ghee (0.59pc each), beef (0.31pc) and pulse mash (0.30pc). The Gulf’s emerging web of security partnerships

The signing of the Badr Memorandum of Understanding by Pakistan, Türkiye, Qatar and Indonesia in Doha on Wednesday is the latest manifestation of Middle Eastern states accelerated efforts for diversifying their defense partnerships amid growing doubts about the reliability of existing security arrangements of the region led by the United States. But, rather than laying the foundations of a new regional security architecture, the proliferation of such agreements is producing a more fractured landscape in which countries are drawing in more partners, pursuing separate strategic interests and accepting varying levels of military commitments. There could not be a bigger example of how uneven is the process than the agreements being signed up by regional countries themselves. Badr, for instance, focuses on defense manufacturing, technology transfer, research and development, and training, allowing its participants to pool industrial capabilities without committing themselves to military assistance in the event of an attack. In contrast the Makkah Joint Defense Agreement, concluded in August by Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkiye, goes considerably further by providing that an attack on one participant will be treated as an attack on all as it builds on a bilateral Strategic Mutual Defense Agreement between Pakistan and KSA signed in Sept 2025. These are two major agreements in addition to several others being signed or solidified by the countries in the region, but the contrast between Badr and Makkah agreements is telling because these agreements are often taken as evidence of an emerging regional security architecture, although the arrangements differ considerably in their purpose and obligations. Makkah agreement formalises security guarantees between participating countries, while Badr aims to develop industrial capacity. The momentum behind these regional security initiatives has grown since this year’s US - Israel war with Iran exposed the risks of relying heavily on external protection. For Gulf states in particular, the Iran conflict raised questions about whether existing security partnerships could adequately protect their interests during a major regional confrontation. Importantly, the response of the affected countries has not been to abandon US, whose military capabilities are still considered indispensable, but to seek additional options through new partnerships, domestic defense production and selective commitments. Besides the impression of the inadequacy of US security umbrella, this search for greater strategic autonomy also needs to be looked at in the context of the Gulf Cooperation Council’s own Joint Defense Agreement, signed in 2000, which too provides that aggression against one member constitutes aggression against all, yet the bloc has struggled to translate this commitment into an integrated military capability because of differences in equipment, command systems and national priorities. The hurdles to full operationalisation of Peninsula Shield Force has further exposed the gap between a collective defense principle and the ability to organise a coordinated response. The newer arrangements that the countries in the region are signing up partly reflect this GCC experience. Therefore, while these countries want enhanced security cooperation, they are also reluctant to surrender the freedom to make independent decisions. As a result, a network of partnerships is emerging that may complement one another in some areas without actually adding up to a common defense system. Pakistan’s expanding role illustrates both the opportunities and the limitations of this approach. Its relationship with Saudi Arabia has acquired a more formal security dimension through the 2025 bilateral agreement and the subsequent Makkah framework, while cooperation with Turkiye offers scope for closer military coordination and defense industrial collaboration. Egypt, another key player in the Arab world, has stayed outside the Makkah framework and has offered defense cooperation to Saudi Arabia, reflecting its interest in protecting its own regional priorities without becoming committed to a wider military confrontation. Islamabad’s other Gulf relationships, however, remain more limited in scope. Kuwait has strengthened defense cooperation with Pakistan through an upgraded agreement - the Protocol Agreement 2026 - covering training, capacity building and border management, while Bahrain has developed close links with the Pakistani military through the presence of Pakistani personnel in its National Guard and Coast Guard, alongside cooperation in training and logistics. These relationships have provided a basis for continued military engagement but do not carry the mutual defense obligations associated with the Pakistan - KSA partnership. Ties with UAE have, meanwhile, followed a more complicated course marred by political and financial strains, Islamabad’s closer alignment with Riyadh and Abu Dhabi’s expanding defense relationship with India. Nevertheless, training, personnel exchanges, counterterrorism activities and joint exercises between the two countries continue. Qatar has signed a defense industrial cooperation agreement involving Pakistan among others while Oman has maintained selective military ties with Pakistan and Turkiye, simultaneously preserving its wider security relationships and avoiding binding mutual defense commitments, consistent with its preference for neutrality and diplomatic engagement, particularly in relation to Iran and the Strait of Hormuz. This makes it difficult to describe Pakistan’s growing defense relationships as a unified regional bloc. Moreover, commitments under these agreements vary according to the interests and calculations of individual partners. Islamabad, in this situation, is left to balance closer security ties against its available military resources and the risk of becoming involved in conflicts whose objectives may not coincide with its own. At the same time the Gulf states are also pursuing different courses. Saudi Arabia is strengthening its relationships with Pakistan and Turkiye while continuing to rely on Western military capabilities and Egypt and exploring defense industrial cooperation with China. The UAE, which has not joined either the Makkah or Badr frameworks, has deepened security cooperation with Israel, including in air defense and counter drone capabilities, while maintaining close relationships with US, France and India. Qatar is seeking to expand its defense industrial partnerships through Badr but at the same time retains US military presence at Al Udeid. Bahrain remains closely aligned with Saudi Arabia and the GCC defense framework, while Kuwait continues to prioritise bilateral military cooperation with Pakistan alongside its substantial security relationship with Washington. Oman’s preference for neutrality and mediation further distinguishes its approach from those seeking more formal security commitments. The differences are not only confined to the choice of partners, but the states also have divergent assessments of the threat posed by Iran, the role of Israel in regional security and the degree to which they should commit themselves to one another’s defense. Their partnerships are therefore being shaped as much by the need to preserve freedom of action as by the desire to develop collective capabilities. The new partnerships do not either automatically translate into military independence for regional states, which continue to operate substantial inventories of Western origin aircraft, air defense systems and other advanced platforms that require specialised maintenance, software support, spare parts and replacement munitions. New partners may help diversify procurement and develop local production, but they cannot necessarily replace established suppliers on the required scale. Moreover, addition of newer systems can also complicate logistics and interoperability unless countries invest in common procedures, training and secure communications.Defense industrial cooperation presents another challenge and agreements such as Badr will acquire practical significance only if they lead to funded production programs, meaningful technology transfer and capabilities that participating countries can maintain and sustain. Similarly, the credibility of mutual defense arrangements will depend on whether their members can coordinate intelligence, plan jointly and establish procedures for responding to a crisis. The number of agreements signed, therefore, is less important than the military capabilities and obligations they actually produce. For Washington, this proliferation of new defense agreements may apparently look like a challenge, but this should definitely not be seen as it being replaced. US undoubtedly remains deeply embedded in the region through military bases, intelligence cooperation, arms sales and longstanding defense relationships. The emerging shift may, however, give the regional countries greater bargaining power with established suppliers, particularly as they press for domestic production, technology partnerships and greater control over maintenance and supply chains. To put it simply the emerging arrangements are an attempt to manage the uncertainty created by recent geo-political developments especially the receding US footprint rather than replace the existing system outright. The central contradiction in the Gulf countries response to the emerging situation, however, is that while these states are seeking greater security through a proliferation of partnerships, they remain unwilling, or unable, to reconcile their differences over the threats they face and the obligations they are prepared to assume.
