Accepting an absurd claim made by the group’s leader confirms an individual’s loyalty to the group, whereas rejecting the claim means aligning with political opponents. Research led by the American professor of psychology and neural science Jay Van Bavel suggests that political loyalty in this regard acts as a “cognitive filter.” It overrides basic critical thinking and yields psychological rewards that reinforce one’s sense of belonging to a group, even when a leader’s claim contradicts reality. Historically, mainstream media often acted as gatekeepers. A ridiculous statement was processed through editorial standards, highlighted for its absurdity and derided on public platforms. Today, they are a norm. The rise of algorithmic platforms has gradually dismantled the role of gatekeepers. Various studies show that repeated exposure to a statement increases its perceived truthfulness, regardless of how implausible it sounds. Social media algorithms prioritise outrageous content because it drives engagement, causing absurd claims to be repeated millions of times in digital echo chambers. Studies by psychologists Gordon Pennycook and David Rand on misinformation show that repetition lowers a reader’s analytical guard, transitioning statements from sounding ridiculous to familiar and, eventually, to plausible and true. In their Rand Corporation paper on propaganda techniques, researchers Christopher Paul and Miriam Matthews coined the term “firehose of falsehood” to describe this “strategic disorientation.” The goal of flooding public space with continuous and blatantly absurd claims is not necessarily to convince everyone that a lie is true. Instead, expertise is reframed as an establishment conspiracy. Instead, they spend more energy in contemplating which side they belong to if they accept or reject the claim. The dispute, therefore, centred on the question whether the agency had the authority to restrain a citizen from travelling on a valid passport. According to the judgement, his name was placed on the PCL on Dec 24, 2024, on the recommendation of NCB-INTERPOL, forwarded by the FIA’s Assistant Director (Immigration Wing). He was neither a proclaimed offender nor the subject of an extradition request. The court observed that these grounds were not contained in the Passports Act or the Passport Rules. Rule 21 dealt with refusal of a passport and did not authorise restraint of a citizen who already possessed a valid passport, the judgement stated. “The refusal of a passport and the prohibition of travel are two separate exercises of power,” Justice Minhas observed, declaring that the function of the relevant division and directorate general to “prepare and maintain” the PCL was administrative and did not itself confer jurisdiction to decide whose name could be placed on the list.
