We were ready with our tax filings, was the FBR?

“Wherever law ends, tyranny begins”—John Locke A few years after my resignation from the Income Tax Service, I happened to visit the offices in Lahore as a representative of a corporate client. A female officer made a remark that left me somewhat bewildered. According to her, laws were binding on taxpayers, not on those administering them. Having spent nineteen years within the department, I had thought I had understood the relationship between law and administration reasonably well. Her statement appeared astonishing then. However, as I watch the annual ritual surrounding income tax returns unfold today, I sometimes wonder whether she was merely articulating an institutional philosophy that the rest of us had failed to recognise. For individuals and associations of persons (AOPs), the statutory date is on or before September 30 following the end of the tax year. In the case of companies, section 118 of the Income Tax Ordinance, 2001 prescribes December 31 where the tax year ends between January 1 and June 30, and September 30 in other cases. The words “on or before” are important. A normal tax year ends on June 30, which corresponds to the financial year (July to June) adopted by the state. Nothing in the statutory formulation suggests that a taxpayer who is ready to comply should have to wait until September merely because the administration has not yet made its prescribed return operational. It stipulates the last date by which the obligation must be discharged but there is one rather substantial difficulty. While the taxpayer may be ready, the FBR may not. Income tax returns in Pakistan are now overwhelmingly filed electronically through the online portal named IRIS (Inland Revenue Information System). Taxpayers cannot invent their own return form. They cannot prepare an Excel sheet containing what they consider adequate information and declare that they have complied with section 114. They must use the prescribed electronic return made available by the FBR. The state, therefore, controls the only doorway through which compliance is possible. Compliance cannot be a one-way street. Once the state makes itself the exclusive gatekeeper of compliance, its own preparedness ceases to be an internal administrative matter. It becomes part of the taxpayer’s ability to obey the law. This becomes particularly important because every Finance Bill, once passed an Act of Parliament, introduces amendments affecting rates, exemptions, deductions, credits, withholding provisions, declarations and sometimes the very manner in which income is to be reported. Naturally, the return form has to accommodate those changes. Nobody disputes that software requires modification in the light of these amendments. What is difficult to understand is why this annual phenomenon continues to be treated as an unforeseen emergency. Pakistan does not suddenly discover on June 30 or before, that a Money Bill, presented with the annual federal budget, on approval by the National Assembly, has become a Finance Act, effective from the first day of July. The budgetary exercise begins months earlier. Proposed amendments are available through the Finance Bill. The FBR itself participates intimately in the legislative exercise. Its policy officers know what changes are being contemplated and its information-technology teams should be working simultaneously on alternative versions of the return. Tax Year 2026 provides a particularly revealing example. The FBR issued draft electronic returns for individuals, SMEs, AOPs and companies on May 6, 2026. Yet the final electronic returns for Tax Year 2026 were notified only at the beginning of September — SRO 1495(I)/2026 dated September 2. In other words, although the taxpayer’s statutory deadline remained September 30, the final return itself arrived when nearly two-thirds of the presumed compliance period had already vanished. Consider what this means. The statutory deadline remained September 30, but taxpayers and practitioners were effectively given certainty about the final prescribed electronic return only when September had already begun. Millions of returns then had to be prepared, checked, reconciled with wealth statements and submitted through a single electronic system within a dramatically compressed period. Inevitably, tax bars and trade organisations sought an extension. The original September 30, 2026, deadline was first extended to October 15, 2026 and, on October 15, the FBR extended it again to October 31, 2026, expressly referring to requests received from trade bodies and tax bar associations. And here lies the irony. The FBR calls this an extension. Taxpayers may reasonably regard part of it as restitution. The extension is presented as a concession to taxpayers but is it really so? If an authority takes a substantial portion of the statutory filing period to make the final compliance mechanism available, the additional time subsequently granted cannot honestly be regarded entirely as administrative generosity. Part of it merely restores time which taxpayers never effectively possessed. We have somehow reversed the relationship between citizen and state. The taxpayer is admonished to comply punctually. Failure attracts consequences. Late filing affects status and can invite statutory penalties and other disadvantages. Notices, payments, appeals and responses carry dates. The taxpayer lives by the calendar. Yet the institution that determines whether electronic compliance is even possible appears to face no comparable clock. When it delays, taxpayers must petition that very institution for additional time. Administrative default is thus converted into administrative discretion. This is not merely an issue of convenience. It raises a basic question of administrative fairness. A government cannot reasonably demand punctual compliance with an obligation while withholding, until late in the prescribed period, the instrument through which that obligation must be performed. Imagine a court requiring an appeal to be filed by September 30 but refusing to provide the prescribed appeal form until September 2. Would we seriously congratulate the court for subsequently extending the deadline? Digitalisation was supposed to facilitate compliance. It should not convert administrative preparedness into a condition precedent to the taxpayer’s ability to obey the law. Nor is the answer complicated. The return for the following tax year should be substantially designed alongside the Finance Bill. Drafts can be programmed and tested before June. Once Parliament enacts the Finance Act, final changes can immediately be incorporated. The electronic form should ordinarily be operational from July 1, or as close to that date as technically unavoidable. More importantly, the law itself should guarantee a minimum effective compliance period — say ninety days — beginning only when the final prescribed electronic return is fully operational. Every day of administrative delay should automatically move the statutory deadline by an equivalent period. If the prescribed electronic return is not available by July 1, the statutory filing date should automatically move by the corresponding period. Better still, legislation could provide that no taxpayer shall have fewer than, say, ninety days from the date on which the final electronic return becomes fully operational. Then nobody would need to plead for extensions. There would be no annual suspense. No last-minute circulars. No ritual exchanges between FBR and tax bars. In addition, no pretense that restoration of lost compliance time amounts to benevolence. The FBR rightly expects taxpayers to organise their affairs, maintain records, understand amendments and respect statutory deadlines. Taxpayers are equally entitled to expect their tax administration to anticipate legislation, prepare its systems and provide the prescribed mechanism in good time. The rule of law does not mean that citizens obey while administrators improvise. It requires the State itself to organise its conduct around the law it enforces. Perhaps that officer’s remark from many years ago deserves to be remembered — but as a warning, not a principle. Laws are not made merely for those upon whom the state imposes obligations. They bind the institutions entrusted with administering them as well. Until that elementary proposition enters our tax culture, the ritual will continue: The FBR will provide the instrument late, taxpayers will plead for more time, and the restoration of lost days will be announced as facilitation. That is not taxpayer facilitation. It is compliance by endurance.

The answer lies in how they measure El Nino’s strength, with meteorological agencies using different methods to assess the climate phenomenon that make direct comparisons difficult. “By any measure, we are looking at a very strong El Nino,” climate scientist Emily Becker of the University of Miami told AFP, adding that only once it is over will scientists be able to assess how big it is and “where it fits in the context of history.” Natural climate driver The Pacific Ocean’s El Nino Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is the planet’s main natural climate driver. Normally, trade winds blow east to west along the equator, pushing warm water from South America toward Asia, while cold water rises from the depths to replace it. El Ninos, which occur every two to seven years on average and last nine to 12 months, disrupt this pattern. The trade winds weaken and warm water is pushed back east toward the Americas. That shift in where ocean heat is released into the atmosphere ripples across global weather, making some regions cooler and wetter and others hotter and drier. But absolute temperatures are just one way of measuring El Nino’s strength. Agencies also look at anomalies — how far temperatures rise above a historic baseline — to assess the event. The Japan Meteorological Agency last week declared this El Nino the strongest on record based on how far sea surface temperatures were above normal in the Nino3 region of the eastern Pacific. By its count, the event beat 1997 in observations going back to 1949. The US watches a neighboring box, Nino3.4, in the east-central Pacific. It declares an El Nino when temperatures there run at least 0.5C above normal and the atmosphere responds, weakening the trade winds and shifting tropical rainfall eastward. The US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) and other agencies including the UN’s World Meteorological Organisation long used the Oceanic Nino Index (ONI), which compares temperatures to a 30-year average that is updated every five years, so that the baseline keeps pace with a warming climate. RONI also relies on a 30-year average but goes on to measure how warm or cool the east-central Pacific is compared to the rest of the tropical ocean, because it is that difference that drives the weather impacts we care about. “We were finding a mismatch between the temperature of the tropical Pacific and the impacts,” said Becker about the old index.
