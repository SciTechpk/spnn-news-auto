Anger mounts in US over vast network of car license plate cameras

In only a few years, the surveillance start-up Flock has installed more than 100,000 license plate reader cameras across the United States. But a grassroots movement upset over privacy concerns is fighting back — even going so far as systematically vandalising the equipment. The first time he decided to trash one of the cameras, a self-styled vigilante wearing all black and known as Nomark says he was “super nervous”. Now, after disabling about 30 of the devices near his home in the Minneapolis area, he told AFP: “I’m not really as scared anymore.” Flock cameras are installed by police departments, homeowners’ associations and businesses, ostensibly to catch criminals in the act. Data is uploaded to Flock’s cloud and can be shared with subscribers. But opponents are outraged at what they say is a dangerous tool that can be used to infringe on American privacy and civil liberties. At just 20 years old, Nomark offers his 500,000 Instagram followers a how-to guide for rendering the cameras useless, raising awareness about the devices. His detractors point out — correctly — that his actions are illegal, but like-minded individuals have followed suit nationwide over the past few weeks. Instead of being dismissed as a radical fringe outfit, the anti-Flock movement is taking hold. Its proponents have been active online for months, creating crowdsourced maps of cameras, sharing ways to block their lenses, and hailing the “heroes” who are arrested for destroying them. The Facebook group DeFlock America has more than 600,000 members, and is gaining thousands more by the day. Massive database Flock cameras differ from traditional surveillance cameras in that they do not record video footage. Instead, they take still images of vehicles, capturing the make, model, colour, license plate data and other distinguishing features such as bumper stickers. And unlike classic surveillance cameras, which are not networked, this data is stored for up to a month and shareable, sometimes across state lines. Opponents say this gives police an overwhelming look into the private lives of law-abiding Americans. Jon Bridges, a police official in Virginia’s state capital Richmond, says that for “practically any crime that has a connection with a vehicle or a vehicle involved, we will at least query the database to see if there’s anything there”. His department has used Flock since 2023. Bridges gave an example of how useful the technology can be: during the investigation of a domestic-related double homicide in May, police quickly identified a suspect who fled the scene in his pick-up truck. Detectives retrieved his license plate data and entered an alert in Flock’s system. When he appeared on another camera, officers in a neighbouring county arrested him. Potential abuses Detractors fear that this allows officers to track vehicles without warrants, opening the door to a host of possible abuses of power. According to an investigation done by The Washington Post, at least 50 police officers have been accused of misusing the Flock system, most of them to spy on the comings and goings of their romantic partners or exes. In other cases reported in US media, cops used Flock to track women who had abortions — illegal in several US states. Some illegally sent data from the system to immigration agents. Flock CEO Garrett Langley has accused some opponents of being “terrorists,” stoking the fire. “There is really an incredible grassroots movement against Flock,” said Chad Marlow, senior policy counsel for the American Civil Liberties Union, calling its growth “stunning”. The movement has support on both the political left and right, notably from libertarians. Former Fox News host and conservative heavyweight Tucker Carlson has called the vandals “decent, patriotic Americans” who are simply “taking matters into their own hands.” Across the country, local groups are pushing their lawmakers to step back from Flock. Steven Keener, a professor of criminology at Christopher Newport University in Virginia, said residents can meet with city council members or mayors to convince them to cancel their contracts with the surveillance company. So far, such grassroots efforts are proving successful. From Los Angeles to small towns in Virginia, more than 70 municipalities have ended their association with Flock in recent months, according to an ACLU tally. For Keener, the movement stands out in a country riven by political divisions. “Because decisions are at the local level, people realise that they can actually make a difference,” he told AFP.

Wrong solution to the right problem

PAKISTAN’S governance crisis is fundamentally institutional rather than geographical. While the creation of new provinces may address certain concerns relating to regional identity, representation, and administrative reach, it cannot by itself remedy systemic deficiencies in governance. Without meaningful institutional reform, decentralisation, and fiscal planning, new provinces risk reproducing existing problems at a greater administrative and financial cost. Modern federal democracies increasingly function through a three-tier system of governance, in which constitutional authority is distributed among the federal government, provincial governments and elected local governments. The rationale is both democratic and practical; decisions should be taken at the lowest level capable of addressing them effectively. National defence and foreign affairs belong to the federation, provincial policy belongs to the provinces while matters such as municipal services, sanitation, local infrastructure and community development are most effectively managed by local governments. This principle of democratic decentralisation ensures that government remains closer, more responsive and more accountable to the people. Pakistan’s Constitution already embraces this model. Article 140A requires provinces to establish local governments and devolve political, administrative and financial responsibility to elected local representatives. In other words, the Constitution already provides a mechanism for bringing government closer to the people. The challenge, therefore, is not the absence of a constitutional framework but the failure to implement it meaningfully. Unfortunately, Pakistan’s experience has often been the opposite. Local governments have frequently been weakened, dissolved or deprived of meaningful financial autonomy, while development funds and administrative authority remain concentrated at the provincial level. This is, in part, a consequence of the political incentive structure within provincial governance. Meaningful devolution inevitably reduces the concentration of power, resources and political visibility enjoyed by the provincial leadership, creating little incentive to transfer genuine authority to local institutions. The consequence is a constitutional distortion. Members of the provincial assemblies, instead of focusing on legislation, policy formulation, executive oversight and public finance, increasingly involve themselves in roads, schools, local development schemes and other matters of municipal administration. Legislators are elected to make laws and hold the executive accountable, not to perform functions that properly belong to elected local governments. When legislative institutions assume executive responsibilities, both governance and lawmaking suffer. The question is whether good governance will be achieved by creating more provinces or by strengthening the institutions that already exist. Before creating additional provinces, Pakistan must therefore ask why the existing constitutional model of local government has not been fully implemented. If governance is the primary objective, strengthening local governments may prove to be a more effective and less costly reform than creating entirely new provincial structures. Another aspect that merits consideration is the financial implications of creating new provinces. A province is not merely a new chief minister and provincial assembly. It requires a complete governmental framework, including a high court, provincial secretariat, public service institutions, tax authorities, police administration, regulatory bodies, specialised departments and thousands of public servants. These institutions create permanent financial obligations in terms of salaries, pensions, infrastructure and operational costs. The question is not whether Pakistan can create new provinces. It can. The question is whether the anticipated governance benefits justify the substantial administrative and financial costs. Another important consideration is economic sustainability. Many proposed provinces, particularly less industrialised regions, possess significant agricultural potential but limited industrial and service-sector capacity. Without a diversified economic base, newly created provinces may remain heavily dependent on federal transfers for their survival. Therefore, any restructuring of provincial boundaries must be preceded by serious fiscal analysis. This is not an argument against creating new provinces. It is an argument that constitutional restructuring must be accompanied by institutional and financial planning. The identity dimension of this debate must also not be forgotten. For many communities, demands for new provinces are not only about administration but also about recognition, representation and political inclusion. Historical regions such as Bahawalpur and Hazara have long sought acknowledgment of their distinct identities. Such aspirations cannot simply be dismissed. However, political representation must translate into actual improvement in the lives of citizens. Creating new provinces may provide opportunities for regional leadership and address feelings of exclusion, but changing boundaries alone cannot resolve deeper issues of governance, economic opportunity, institutional capacity and public service delivery. Pakistan’s governance challenges require a comprehensive approach. Strong local governments, professional civil services, transparent administration, fiscal responsibility and constitutional continuity are the foundations of effective governance. If, after meaningful reforms and careful fiscal assessment, parliament concludes that additional provinces are necessary, such decisions should be taken on constitutional and administrative grounds rather than political expediency. Pakistan undoubtedly needs better governance. The question is whether that objective will be achieved by creating more provinces or by strengthening the institutions that already exist. The problems undermining governance are not merely products of geography. Before redrawing the map, we should first reform the machinery that governs it. 33 held, booked under ATA for resisting anti-encroachment drive

KARACHI: Police on Saturday claimed to have arrested 33 suspects after violence erupted during an anti-encroachment drive at Shafiq Mor. The police said that the angry mob attacked the police and district administration, causing injuries to them, blocked main roads, resorted to firing and attacked vehicles with stones. According to a police statement, on Friday night, the district administration carried out an anti-encroachment drive on Shah Waliullah Road at Haji Agha Hotel in the limits of Samanabad. During the drive, the owner of the hotel, namely Mohammed Hashim, and his employees put up resistance and attacked both police and district administration officials, resulting in injuries to SHO Hyderi Market Adeel Ahmed, constable Asif and one administration official. The police took immediate action and arrested hotel owner Hashim along with three other suspects, who were shifted to the police station for further legal action. Later, one suspect, namely Jaffer Shah, along with four-five armed guards travelling in a double cabin, arrived at the spot. He also called other people, resulting in around 100 persons gathering there. The police statement said that the mob blocked Shah Waliullah Road and F.B. Industrial Area Road illegally, attacked the police with stones and resorted to firing at the police. Additional force and anti-riot police were called, who managed to disperse the mob and get both roads cleared. During the action, 30 more suspects, including Jaffer Shah, were arrested. The police have registered two FIRs against the held suspects on the complaint of the Mukhtiarakar of Samanabad and on behalf of the State through a police officer under terrorism, rioting and other charges.
