Left embraces socialist label in US as stigma fades

NEW YORK: “Socialism,” once considered a scarlet letter in the United States, is growing increasingly buzzy and mainstream as a slate of politicians embracing the label has gained significant ground. In the wake of November’s midterms, members of the Democratic Socialists of America — a leftist organisation that’s made inroads within the Democratic Party — now lead cities across the country including its two largest, New York and Los Angeles. DSA-endorsed politicians boosted their congressional seats from two to eight and won dozens of local elections, victories partly due to the Dem­ocrats’ wider battering of President Donald Trump’s Republicans. The so-called “bluenami” of Democratic wins “would not have been possible without a socialist surge,” DSA communications chair Cara Tobe said. “People are starting to realise and learn that socialism is not a big scary word,” she said, noting that the Democrats’ focus on affordability was lifted straight from the DSA playbook. But some political scholars aren’t convinced “socialism” earned votes. “A rising tide lifts all boats. This was a huge win for the Democratic Party across the board,” said Stephen Ansolabehere, professor of government at Harvard University. Hans Noel of Georgetown University attributed the success of Democrats — and DSA-backed candidates — to dissatisfaction with Trump. “It’s important to remember that while DSA-aligned candidates did very well this year, that’s mostly in the primaries and in very Democratic areas,” the politics professor said. Yet polls show a rising acceptance of socialism in the United States, a quintessentially capitalist country where politicians have long conflated the term with communism to vilify it. The DSA, founded in 1982 with a focus on labour organising, was for years a relatively tiny left-wing pressure group. In 2015, about 6,000 supporters paid dues, but after Donald Trump’s first presidential election new membership surged. Today, the group has more than 130,000 members and has proven an electoral force, with legions of volunteers helping elect candidates nationwide to state and local office. And New York’s young and charismatic Mayor Zohran Mamdani has proven a poster child for furthering progressive politics under a socialist banner in cities, with a focus on core DSA issues including affordable housing, immigrant protections and healthcare access. It’s a model politicians elsewhere in the nation are picking up on. Most notably, the so-called “sewer socialists” ran Milwaukee for more than half a century through 1960, dramatically scaling up public works projects including a modern sewer system, public housing and social infrastructure. Middle power aspirations

AS the global order shifts away from unipolarity toward fragmented multipolarity, middle powers have gained unprecedented strategic weight. In a fragmented multipolar world, middle powers serve as indispensable diplomatic bridges and economic shock absorbers that keep the international system functioning despite great-power paralysis. Middle powers lack hegemonic ambitions, and retain the credibility to mediate active conflicts, build agile issue-specific coalitions, and maintain open trade and supply-chain corridors across rival blocs. Ultimately, these middle powers are preventing total global bifurcation of the international system between Washington and Beijing by stabilising their immediate regions and upholding international rules. However, scholars and policymakers rarely agree on a single checklist for middle power status, largely because different states exert influence through different mechanisms. Some, such as South Korea or Australia, earn the label through thriving, high-tech economies and global trade integration. Others, like Norway or Qatar, leverage niche diplomacy and active mediation to punch above their geographic weight. Still others, such as Türkiye and Pakistan, rely primarily on formidable military capabilities and regional security projection. Scholars generally evaluate middle power status across three core pillars including military capability, diplomatic outreach, and comprehensive national strength. National strength is largely defined by sustainable economic vitality and technological adaptation. Over the past two years, Pakistan has demonstrated a resurgence in the first two arenas, vaulting itself back into global strategic relevance. Yet, because its third and most foundational pillar remains weak, Pakistan’s recognition as an emerging middle power rests on a fragile foundation that cannot endure without a decisive domestic economic pivot. A credible middle power must possess the military capacity to defend its sovereignty and project stabilising influence across its neighbourhood. Between 2025 and 2026, Pakistan highlighted its hard-power credentials through both high-intensity deterrence and institutionalised defence partnerships. History shows that hard power and ingenious statecraft cannot substitute for domestic economic strength. During the brief but severe four-day conflict with India in May 2025, South Asia witnessed its first multi-domain confrontation integrating aerial clashes, drone swarms, and precision missile exchanges. Pakistan’s military response, culminating in Operation Bunyanum Marsoos, demonstrated modern warfare capabilities and aerial superiority. Pakistan’s swift military response compelled a rapid White House-facilitated ceasefire on May 10. By denying a larger neighbour unilateral escalation dominance, Islamabad reinforced its conventional and strategic deterrence. Since May 2025, Pakistan translated its battlefield credibility into a web of security pacts across the Middle East and the Muslim world. In September 2025, Islamabad and Riyadh signed the Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement, and in August 2026, Pakistan joined Saudi Arabia and Türkiye to sign the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement. These pacts not only created a formal collective deterrence network but also enhanced Islamabad’s strategic utility in the Gulf. The Badr agreement with Türkiye, Qatar, and Indonesia, has further strengthened Pakistan’s position as a net security provider of Arab Gulf states. Beyond military muscle, middle powers distinguish themselves through niche diplomacy. Following the outbreak of direct US-Israeli military hostilities against Iran in early 2026, traditional Gulf mediators found their channels strained. Adopting a posture of active neutrality, Islamabad stepped in. In March 2026, the US accepted Pakistan’s role as a primary conduit to Tehran, which led to the historic Islamabad peace talks in April 2026 and the subsequent June MoU that temporarily reopened the Strait of Hormuz. Even as the truce faced repeated strain through late 2026, the civil and military leadership of Pakistan continued shuttling proposals between Washington, Tehran, and European capitals. The decision to join Trump’s Gaza peace plan as a founding member also proved Islamabad could exercise diplomatic power beyond its immediate borders. However, despite these military and diplomatic milestones, history demonstrates that hard power and ingenious statecraft cannot substitute for domestic economic strength. In this third pillar, Pakistan exhibits severe structural deficits that undermine its middle power aspirations. Macroeconomic indicators showed surface stabilisation in FY2025-26 with inflation remaining between 7-9 per cent and GDP growth edging up to 3.7pc. However, the underlying growth model remains weak. For instance, investment hovers near 15pc of GDP, far below the 20-plus per cent threshold required to sustain the 6pc growth rates needed to absorb a booming youth population. Deindustrialisation and high energy costs continue to stifle export competitiveness. Foreign investors are largely concerned with the security situation in Balochistan and KP. Unrest in AJK has further marred prospects of foreign direct investment. The privatisation process is critically slow, while political consensus is absent over reforms for new provinces. With a low tax-to-GDP ratio and heavy external debt servicing, state solvency still relies heavily on remittances, multilateral bailouts, bilateral rollovers (such as a $3 billion Saudi cash deposit in April 2026) rather than productive capital formation. Hence, Pakistan today represents a textbook case of an asymmetric emerging middle power. Its disciplined military and seasoned diplomatic corps have successfully leveraged regional crises to earn Islamabad a seat at high-stakes global tables. However, geopolitical leverage built on security crises and external rent is inherently transactional. When regional wars subside or great-power priorities shift, Islamabad — lacking economic autonomy and technological innovation — will risk losing its strategic relevance. Therefore, to cement its status as a genuine middle power, Pakistan must execute an urgent domestic pivot. This requires dismantling elite economic capture, broadening the tax base, overhauling education to build a tech-literate workforce, and enhancing an export-driven industrial base. Until Islamabad matches its battlefield deterrence and diplomatic agility with sustainable economic sovereignty, its middle power status will remain an aspiration suspended over a precarious fault line. Judges must speak

A JUDGMENT is a public document, written in the name of the Constitution and delivered in open court. The people may read it, praise it and criticise it, and the academy, bar, press and civil society should do so freely. Criticism is not the enemy of judicial authority; it is its source. A judiciary whose judgments are never read critically will never grow. One voice is largely missing from that conversation: the judge who wrote the judgment. In Pakistan, judges mostly retire into silence. That must change. Retired judges must come out among the people, above all into the law schools, and explain their reasoning, or say, with hindsight: “I think I got that one wrong.” Such candour teaches the profession and builds public trust and confidence in the institution. A sitting judge who knows retirement means facing the public will brush nothing under the carpet. A judgment open to criticism falls into one of three kinds. The first plainly violates the Constitution and the law. The second was legally permissible when given, but its consequences proved damaging. The third is the judgment in which the judge was complicit, bending the law to reach an outcome decided elsewhere. To assess one, read it whole and in context: the moment, the circumstances, the law as it then stood. Then ask whether it was permissible, plainly wrong, or the judge simply listening to power. Let me test three of my own. In Pakistan, judges mostly retire into silence. That must change. In the 2023 case on Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa elections, Justice Jamal Khan Mandokhail and I wanted the high courts to decide first: Lahore had already ordered elections, with appeals pending, and Peshawar was seized of the petitions. Law and practice both counselled that restraint. We read the outcome as four to three, because two colleagues who had already voted to dismiss could not, in our view, be dropped from the count; Justice Athar Minallah, one of them, recorded as much. In hindsight, the mistake, I believe, was the chief justice’s hurried leap in; hearing the matter in appeal, the court could have spoken with one voice. Had we set the count aside and presented a united court, whether that would have produced elections is anyone’s guess. Later, the chief justice’s own bench rejected the four-three objection and fixed May 14 for Punjab polls, yet could not enforce it. The date passed, contempt was never pursued, and the court fell silent. The larger lesson is that a court must stand united, and the chief justice’s strength is the real spine that holds it together. The Supreme Court as it stands today shows how much rests on that spine. In the Justice Qazi Faez Isa case, the court unanimously quashed the reference, the majority for malice in law; I held it malicious in fact as well. The majority directed the FBR to examine the family’s properties and report to the council; three of us held that no direction was needed, the FBR being free to act under the tax law. On review, that view prevailed. No one is above the law, and no one below its protection: a judge’s spouse answers under the tax law, a judge under the Code of Conduct, only in accordance with law. I am often asked whether we saved the wrong man. We saved due process and the independence of the judiciary, and in doing so we saved a man. What people thought of him later was not our concern then. A judgment is not a character certificate. In the Practice and Procedure case, I believed, and still believe, that the court had to become collegial rather than be run by one man. Opening the door was not itself the mistake; so long as the court stood united, no one could have walked through it. What we failed to see was that the court was weak within, and weakness inside invites those outside. And it did: a 2024 ordinance gave the chief justice’s nominee a seat on the bench-forming committee. Had earlier chief justices brought in the collegial model through the full court, that door need never have opened from outside. This is a lesson, not an accusation. Each choice was made in good faith, within the law. In hindsight, wiser choices may have been available, but choosing in the moment is the judicial function, and there the judge must be trusted. Unless a judge was complicit, there is nothing to apologise for, only lessons to share. What must worry us most is complicity. It is not an error of judgment but a surrender of it, and it has hollowed out our courts. A judgment bent to power may be overruled, but the betrayal stays on the record. Error is part of judging. Capitulation is not. And complicity has a way of showing its face, in the promotions, benefits and advantages that later reward it. Today, that trail must be made visible. A word to the critics. Those quick to call out judicial error must be as open-hearted in acknowledging courage. Dissent, refusal of oath and resignation are the building blocks of an independent judiciary. Ask what a judge gained by resigning when a simple yes would have secured everything. Nothing, except the honour of the institution. Criticism that records every failure and never that courage leaves the next generation no one to look up to. So let the conversation begin, and let it be honest. Let judges speak: give the context of their decisions, own their errors without fear and explain their choices without apology. And let complicity be named for what it is. Judicial independence has never been absolute. Sadly, there has always been a chink in the armour, a door left ajar, and someone willing to push it open. No single judgment or judge will close that door. But an honest and open conversation, carried from one generation to the next, can. That will be the real victory: not a court that never errs, but a court with no door through which power can enter. Muddled hierarchy

THE Federal Constitutional Court marked its first anniversary on Friday with a commemorative stamp. Speaking on the occasion, its chief justice remarked on how fleeting the year had been. Litigants, lawyers and the judges of the Supreme Court might have described it differently. A year after the 27th Amendment rewrote the structure of the country’s judiciary, one of the most basic questions raised by the new order remains unanswered: which court, the FCC or the SC, has the final word? This was, perhaps, bound to happen. Critics had foreseen problems in the short and hurried route the government took from the 26th to the 27th Amendment. In October 2024, the 26th Amendment carved constitutional benches out of the SC, with their members chosen by a reconstituted Judicial Commission in which judges no longer held a majority. Critics warned then that the independence of the judiciary was at stake. Barely a year later, and in evident haste, the 27th Amendment turned those benches into an entirely separate court. Two of the SC’s senior-most judges resigned in protest the day it became law. Thousands of cases were soon transferred to the FCC, and the SC was stripped of its constitutional jurisdiction. What has followed has seemed less a division of labour than a contest for primacy. Last December, the FCC held that its rulings bind all courts, including the SC, but that the latter’s do not bind the FCC. In May, Chief Justice Yahya Afridi pushed back, ruling that the two are coordinate courts, neither an appellate forum over the other. Last month, the FCC rebuked an SC bench for revisiting a matter the FCC considered settled, raising concerns over judicial discipline. The sparring shows no sign of stopping. The text of the amendment arguably favours the FCC, while the SC relies on the overall constitutional scheme to assert its position. That both positions can be argued is precisely the problem. Two hasty amendments have created an ambiguity that leaves the two courts defining their relationship through pointed observations in their judgments. The costs are borne by litigants, whose cases risk being shuttled between forums. The FCC, meanwhile, has worked with seven judges against a sanctioned strength of 13. And its order barring the Peshawar High Court from hearing petitions on emergency and governor’s rule in KP has pushed citizens away from a court closer to home. Since parliament created this ambiguity, it must now resolve it. The jurisdiction of the two courts and a mechanism for settling disputes between them should be spelt out in law, not left to judges to fight over. According to Muhammad Amin Rajput, the schedule was necessary as the country’s gas production had fallen about 50 per cent over the last decade, while imported LNG supplies, too, were hard to come by since April due to the Iran war. “By focusing on supply during cooking hours, the company can protect domestic consumers,” the managing director said in an exclusive interview with Dawn. He said SSGC’s gas availability had fallen to around 605 million cubic feet per day due to depletion in gas fields, compared with 695mmcfd last year. “Of this, around 300mmcfd is being given to domestic consumers, while the rest is supplied to industry, power and fertiliser sectors,” Mr Rajput added. The utility has decided to supply gas during the breakfast, lunch and dinner windows to ensure that every household got the fuel when it mattered most. “The SSGC has been monitoring availability continuously since last week,” Mr Rajput said. Supply to households restricted to three meal times He said domestic gas supply in the city was adequate. Residents in many areas, however, complained the flame was too low for cooking purposes. “A localised issue should not be generalised across the entire network that serves roughly 50,000 kilometres of pipelines, 16,000 of them in Karachi alone. The SSGC has 3.2 million customers across Sindh and Balochistan, including two million in Karachi,” Mr Rajput observed. Use of compressors, pumps The SSGC MD blamed the low pressure on the illegal use of gas compressors and suction pumps. These devices can drain away the supply to an entire block or building, he cautioned. “Besides being illegal, substandard devices pose safety risks and have caused property damage, and sometimes even loss of life.” Mr Rajput urged consumers to report such use, saying that removing the devices would help restore pressure during supply hours, even though they may inflate bills by distorting meter readings.
