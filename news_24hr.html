Pakistan's mango exports fall 36pc amid US-Iran war: exporters' association

Pakistan’s mango exports fell 36 per cent this year as the US-Iran war disrupted shipping routes and drove up freight costs, industry figures showed on Wednesday. According to All Pakistan Fruit and Vegetables Exporters Association, the country exported 78,000 tonnes of mangoes this year, compared with 123,000 tons last year. Exports to the Middle East, a major market for Pakistani mangoes, dropped from 36,000 tonnes last year to 14,500 tonnes this year, the association said. Waheed Ahmed, head of the association, told Anadolu that the ongoing conflict had reduced the country’s mango export earnings from approximately $100 million to around $60m. He cited the months-long near-closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a key shipping route, and rising freight rates as major factors behind the decline, particularly in exports to Middle Eastern countries. “The freight rate that was $1,000-1,400 before the US-Iran war, now stands at $8,000. Secondly, the closure of the Strait of Hormuz also increased the shipping period, which affected the exports of perishable items like mangoes,” he said. Ahmed said the United Arab Emirates and Oman were the main Middle Eastern destinations for Pakistani mangoes, particularly the internationally acclaimed Chaunsa variety, followed by Saudi Arabia. Kuwait and Qatar also import significant quantities of Pakistani mangoes, according to the association. Mango exports to neighboring Afghanistan were also disrupted by frequent border closures amid strained relations between the two countries, Ahmed said. Pakistan produced 1.9m metric tonnes of mangoes annually until 2018, ranking sixth globally behind India, China, Thailand, Indonesia and Mexico. However, production has declined over the past four years, with the association attributing the drop to global warming. Climate change has reduced Pakistan’s total mango production by 35pc, according to the association.

ICJ report sees 'judicial capture' in Pakistan after 26th, 27th Constitutional Amendments

A report released by the International Commission of Jurists (ICJ) on Wednesday said that the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments led to a “systemic judicial capture” in Pakistan. The 26th Amendment, passed in October 2024, carved constitutional benches out of the Supreme Court, with their members chosen by a reconstituted Judicial Commission of Pakistan (JCP) in which judges no longer held a majority. Barely a year later, the 27th Amendment turned those benches into an entirely separate court, the Federal Constitutional Court (FCC). It also made significant changes to the judicial system, the military’s chain of command and granted immunities to government officials. Two of the SC’s senior-most judges resigned in protest the day it became law. On Wednesday, the ICJ released a report — titled ‘Judicial Capture: Independence of the Judiciary in Pakistan after the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments’. It said that after analysing the institutional framework governing higher courts following the two amendments, the judiciary in Pakistan no longer enjoyed institutional guarantees of independence required under international law and standards. “The 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments were a decisive break from the model of judicial independence that Pakistan had built after the restoration of the judiciary in 2009,” the report noted. However, it observed that the “erosion of judicial independence” was done through constitutional amendments, rather than the overt removal of judges. The report said that while the government argued that amendments were made to “check judicial overreach, reduce the backlog of cases, end the perceived politicisation of the SC’s original jurisdiction and give Parliament a role in judicial appointments comparable to that of legislatures in other democracies”, the claims did not justify “changing the judicial structure in a manner that is glaringly inconsistent with Pakistan’s obligations under international law”. It also acknowledged that the Supreme Court had “encroached into roles of Parliament and other institutions”. However, it also noted that with the 26th and 27th amendments, the Parliament failed to ensure these “deficiencies” were fixed in line with judicial independence. “Instead, the 26th and 27th Amendments have led to systemic judicial capture by other branches of the state and the dismantling of the rule of law in the country,” the ICJ stated. It was found that the ruling coalition had “exploited” the public’s grievances over the functioning of courts in a “self-serving manner” to control the judiciary. “As a result, even the highest courts of the land in Pakistan no longer serve as a check on the executive or the Parliament and their function as avenues for redress of victims of human rights violations is seriously impaired,” the report stated. Call for comprehensive review In its recommendations, the report appealed to the government to begin a comprehensive review of the amendments and their implementation in “light of international law and standards”, particularly under the framework of Pakistan’s obligations under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and by holding “transparent and broad” public consultation. It suggested that the government engage in international cooperation, including seeking technical assistance from the UN special rapporteur on the independence of judges and lawyers and inviting them for a country visit. In its recommendations to the JCP, the ICJ called on it to revise the criteria for judicial appointments in accordance with international law, standards and best practices. It further suggested: “Ensure the published criteria are the sole basis for nomination of judges to the FCC, the SC, the high courts and constitutional benches of the high courts.” It also called on the commission to establish a transparent procedure for appointments and publish reasons for each appointment that “demonstrate the published criteria are being implemented in practice”. It also called on the JCP to refrain from transferring a high court judge without their consent. In its recommendations to the Supreme Judicial Council, the ICJ said: “Define ‘inefficiency’ in a manner that is consistent with international standards to only mean serious incompetence that renders judges unfit to discharge their duties.” For the Special Parliamentary Committee, which has the power to nominate the chief justice of Pakistan, the report called for it to adopt “clear and transparent criteria and procedures” for the appointment of the SC and FCC top judges and to publish the reasons for each selection. Lastly, the report urged the FCC, the SC and the high courts to “interpret and apply the provisions of the 26th and 27th Amendments, to the greatest extent possible, in a manner consistent with the independence of the judiciary as guaranteed by Article 14 of the ICCPR”.

Cricket Australia to open 'Sandpapergate' forum for players to share their versions but rules out new probe

Cricket Australia (CA) on Wednesday ruled out reopening an investigation into the 2018 ball-tampering scandal but will launch an independent forum for players to share their version of events as a “circuit breaker”. Festering wounds over what happened in the third Test at Cape Town eight years ago reopened last week when former Australian cricketer Usman Khawaja released his autobiography. In the book, he alleged that the whole team knew about the plot to rough up the ball with sandpaper to make it swing, an allegation strenuously denied by current captain Pat Cummins. A Cricket Australia probe at the time concluded only skipper Steve Smith, his deputy David Warner and Cameron Bancroft were in the know, with all three sent home in disgrace and banned. Warner, who has backed Khawaja’s new version of events, over the weekend accused CA of conducting “a shoddy investigation”, claiming not all players were interviewed. CA Chief Executive Todd Greenberg said the past week had been an “incredibly difficult and uncomfortable time for the game and for everyone who was involved”. But, speaking in Hobart ahead of Australia’s one-day clash with England on Wednesday, Greenberg was adamant that “the investigation has been completed” and ruled out making it public, citing confidentiality. Interim chairman David Boon said instead that a forum, likely led by a former judge, would be established. But there would be no new sanctions coming out of it. “After careful discussion, we agreed to establish a forum that will allow players and staff involved in the events of South Africa in 2018 to share their experiences and their perspectives,” he said. “We believe it is time for a circuit breaker. This is not about litigating the past. It is about giving the opportunity to those involved to have their voices heard. “Australian cricket is at its strongest when we’re united, and we owe it to everyone who loves the game to make every reasonable effort to move forward together with understanding, respect and perspective.” ‘Game needs to move on’ Cricket Australia would not commit to making the contents of the forum public, which it hopes can be wrapped up within a month. “We will look to be as transparent as we possibly can, whilst at the same time protecting the confidentiality of the people who come forward,” Greenberg said. He denied the forum was to appease Warner, who has been vocal in support of Khawaja. “This is not about appeasing any individual. This is about the game, it’s about the sport,” Greenberg said. “The game needs to move on somehow from this. These events were eight years ago. They caused enormous pain and hurt for lots of people and are doing it again now. “And at some point we have to move forward. And we can’t move forward without putting some of the steps in place.” Warner said he agreed that there should be no new sanctions for anyone involved, but would prefer to have an open inquiry. “I would’ve liked an open inquiry and the people that were there at the time actually can come forward and speak,” he said while commentating on the Australia-England match. “It’s not about penalties. It’s about transparency and accountability — which Todd’s acknowledged, on CA’s behalf, about the culture at the time with the players and CA.” Khawaja, also on commentary for the match, said he welcomed a “safe space to chat”, but voiced concern that it would be behind closed doors. “The biggest question I have is [that] the board makes the decisions of what comes out at the end,” he said. “How is that an actual way of trying to present what people have said? I can go in there and say something but then the board can decide they’re not going to release that.”

Tech outage wave reaches US government, Trump says all is 'fine'

Technology outages that shut down email for many companies and grounded thousands of flights over the past few days extended to the US National Security Agency (NSA) on Tuesday, as President Donald Trump’s administration downplayed concerns about a broader problem. The NSA said its network experienced a “temporary degradation” during routine maintenance, but that “mission-critical capabilities” remained operational. The series of disruptions to critical systems, which also touched the banking system on Monday, prompted even some Trump allies to demand more information. “If this is all a coincidence, it’s like the greatest coincidence in the history of the world,” US Senator Josh Hawley, a Missouri Republican, told reporters. Trump earlier played down the incidents, which raised questions of whether the government and private sector had moved too quickly in cutting tech staff. “It’s like when you’re big and powerful, and you have all the money. Things like that happen. It’s fine,” Trump told reporters in Manila, where he was attending meetings with the Association of Southeast Asian Nations. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who doubles as national security adviser, acknowledged the incidents were “unsettling” but said they were completely unrelated. “We are not under systemic attack,” he told reporters in Manila. “We have zero evidence or any reason to believe that any of these isolated or individual instances have anything to do with nefarious actors, with cyberhackers, with criminals, with another nation state, with a malign actor of any kind,” Rubio added. Daily downtime Technology-related disruptions have hit every weekday since Thursday, when a Microsoft outage disrupted many of its services, including email. The next day, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) called for a nationwide ground stop that lasted 4-1/2 hours, the longest halt to US air travel since the attacks of September 11, 2001. The spate of outages included a United Airlines technology failure on Monday that delayed about 150 flights on the same day that a JP Morgan Chase software problem disrupted some banking services. On Tuesday, Apple said it was working to restore its systems after an internal update caused an outage that affected customer services including iCloud and Apple Pay. The issue was not caused by a cyberattack, Apple said. Insurance provider Marsh also said on Tuesday its clients reported 105 cyber incidents in the last two weeks of October more than double the 48 recorded in the final two weeks of May. Experts worry that AI tools are feeding a surge of cyber activity, with cybersecurity companies regularly turning up examples of hackers using AI to launch scores of intrusions at once. “The recent string of major disruptions across industries highlights how deeply interconnected our digital infrastructure has become, and how a single technical failure or software update can cascade with outsized effects across critical systems,” said Cynthia Kaiser, a former senior FBI cyber official and current senior vice president of cybersecurity firm Halcyon’s Ransomware Research Centre. The string of tech outages is likely due to issues emerging from system upgrades, US Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton said in an interview with Fox Business on Tuesday. “We do not see the indicia of a coordinated cyberattack … And we do not see the indicia of state-sponsored activity here. And we can be fairly confident in that,” Clayton said. He attributed the NSA outage to patches deployed for a PHP vulnerability discovered using AI last month a bug that could let hackers remotely take control of servers. “Everybody recognises it’s time to upgrade their systems, and as they upgrade them, they’re not coming back as expected in all cases, and in some cases, systems have to hand off to other systems, and those handoffs are not coming back as expected,” Clayton told the ‘Claman Countdown’ programme on Fox Business. Outages follow layoffs, warnings At many organisations both private and government the outages follow layoffs and, in some cases, explicit warnings about potential disruptions. At the FAA, which was hit by Elon Musk-driven job cuts after Trump took office last year, Congress warned the agency’s sped-up modernisation effort was poorly organised and could trigger service outages. United Airlines said last year that AI advances had helped it cut management headcount by four per cent and that it planned to cut by an additional four per cent this year. At Microsoft, the email outage came a month after the company said it was offering buyouts to a quarter of its global workforce, a move that follows previous headcount reductions. While there is no evidence job losses at any of these places led to the disruptions, one tech labour organiser said cuts tended to make safety margins thinner. “Part of the resilience of a system comes from people who are experienced with a system,” said Liz Fong-Jones, who has worked for 20 years on software resilience issues. Democratic US Senator Jack Reed of Rhode Island joined in calls for more information from the White House. “Just the last few days, America has seen tech glitches all across the board,” Reed told reporters. “The president has to get on top of it, explain to the American people what’s going on, and do it quickly, and that is a mission that he is not doing at this moment.”
