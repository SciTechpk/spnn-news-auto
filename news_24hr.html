FAO flags gaps in Pakistan’s nutritious food supply

• UN agency says over 60pc of citizens unable to afford healthy diet• Calls for overhaul of agrifood system ISLAMABAD: The Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) of the United Nations has emphasised the urgent need to transform Pakistan’s agrifood system to improve access to healthy diets, a critical step towards addressing all forms of malnutrition and their related costs to society. The UN agency, in its latest assessment on “Food Supply for Healthy Diets in Pakistan”, observed that a major challenge in the country’s current food supply is the persistent mismatch between the quantities of food available and those required for the population to consume a healthy diet aligned with the national food-based dietary guidelines (FBDGs). “Enabling access to healthy diets for all is essential to achieving Pakistan’s national development priorities. Yet over 60 per cent of Pakistanis cannot afford a healthy diet, contributing to the high rates of malnutrition across the country. At the same time, diet-related non-communicable diseases (NCD) are rising, while various forms of undernutrition persist. Unhealthy diets are a common cause of all forms of malnutrition and contribute directly to morbidity and mortality from NCD,” the assessment shows. The FAO listed priority policy actions to strengthen demand for healthy diets through consumer-focused policies; updating Pakistan’s FBDGs to reflect nutrition, health, and environmental priorities and use it for nutrition education and behavior change; introducing evidence-based fiscal measures, including taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages and other foods high in sugar, salt, and unhealthy fats; introducing front-of-pack nutrition labelling to help consumers make informed food choices; protecting vulnerable populations; restricting the marketing of unhealthy foods, particularly those targeting children, expanding nutritious school meal programmes; and strengthening social protection programmes to improve access to nutritious foods. The assessment recommended reorienting the agrifood system to deliver healthy diets, aligning agricultural policies and investment priorities to increase support for the production of vegetables, fruits, pulses, roots and tubers across the country, while supporting milk production in some regions; and providing greater incentives for food processors to meet food safety and quality standards and reformulate foods to reduce salt, sugar and unhealthy fats. It called for promoting healthier retail food environments, through investments in storage, cold chains, and market infrastructure to reduce losses. These actions, combined with regular monitoring, can contri­bute to increasing access to and consumption of healthy diets in Paki­stan, both today and in the future. The FAO assessment says large gaps were observed for several nutritious food groups, particularly vegetables, roots and tubers, fruits, and legumes and pulses. However, important misalignment exists between Pakistan’s dietary guidelines and food supply, with supply gaps for several nutritious food groups, particularly vegetables, roots and tubers, fruits, legumes and pulses, while the supply for staples and sugar exceeds what is needed for healthy diets. “Enabling equitable access to diverse, nutritious, safe, and affordable foods that support healthy diets is a policy imperative for Pakistan,” the assessment said. The Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA), which administers the proceedings over the IWT, has determined that the treaty remains in force despite India’s decision to place it in abeyance. India has rejected the arbitration process, while Pakistan has cited the court’s order as supporting its position that the agreement remains effective. Rashida Abbas, founder of the Hydro-Justice Student Research & Policy Fellowship and a PhD candidate at the University of Geneva, addressed the South Asian dimension of the discussion, focusing particularly on the Indus system. Abbas said rivers should not be viewed solely as matters between states because their consequences are experienced directly by people and communities. “Rivers are not experienced only by states,” Abbas said. “They are experienced by people.” She linked water governance with human rights, noting that decisions concerning rivers can affect drinking water, agriculture, livelihoods, communities and health. Abbas argued that the future management of South Asia’s shared waters should be based on law, dialogue, science, functioning institutions and justice. She also stressed that trans-boundary agreements should not become optional when relations between states deteriorate. The discussion addressed concerns about using shared water resources as instruments of political pressure. Abbas highlighted the interests of downstream communities and the need for legal frameworks to protect their water security and human rights. Other speakers placed the Indus dispute within a broader discussion of trans-boundary water challenges. Majid Hawi Alwan addressed geopolitical pressures surrounding shared water systems in arid regions, including the Gulf and wider Middle East. His contribution focused on regional approaches in which water security is treated as a shared necessity rather than an instrument of unilateral leverage. Seraina Eldada brought a mediation and diplomacy perspective, examining the institutional design of transboundary water arrangements and the role of third-party mediation and permanent mechanisms in resolving disputes. Harmayn Mujahid, a legal scholar and fellowship coordination officer, linked water insecurity with human rights concerns. The discussion considered how changing river patterns and climate-related stress can affect vulnerable communities and why questions of equity need to be considered alongside state sovereignty in international water law. Tahir Mahmood addressed the relationship between resource scarcity and security, including risks associated with water-related tensions in regions already affected by political and territorial disputes. He also discussed prevention and resilience in the context of what he described as “hydro-terrorism.” The contributors noted that shared river systems face increasing pressure from climate change, population growth, environmental degradation and rising demand for freshwater.
