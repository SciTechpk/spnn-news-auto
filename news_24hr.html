Why Pakistan needs administrative restructuring

Pakistan’s administrative map is carrying a population load it was never designed for. We are now a country of more than 240 million people. Punjab alone is home to over 127 million people, Sindh more than 55 million and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa over 40 million. These numbers matter beyond being a headcount from a census. They help decide how far the government has to reach, how many people local institutions must serve, how development funds are shared and how fast the state can act. Yet the spaces where political power, financial decisions and government action are concentrated remain very few. At the national level, Pakistan has only 266 directly elected National Assembly seats for a population of 241.5 million, averaging roughly one directly elected representative for every 908,000 people. Overtly concentrated Government is heavily concentrated in Lahore, Karachi, Peshawar and Quetta. Ministries, senior bureaucracies, large hospitals, universities, courts and development authorities gather around provincial capitals. Over time, economic activity follows them, as do jobs, professional services, infrastructure and investment. Such concentration has consequences for regions farther away. A citizen in southern Punjab experiences provincial government differently from someone in Lahore. The same holds for Hazara in relation to Peshawar, or for distant districts in Sindh and Balochistan. Geography eventually becomes administrative distance. Files move through longer chains, senior decision-makers remain elsewhere and development priorities compete for attention with those of much larger metropolitan centres. This is exactly why the debate over new administrative units deserves to be linked directly with prosperity. In economics, government exerts a gravitational pull. A centre of administration creates surrounding departments, courts, universities, hospitals, infrastructure, housing, services and jobs, and this draws businesses towards it. It also fosters professional communities; roads are built or improved, land prices rise and new markets form. All these dynamics can be seen across Pakistan around existing provincial capitals. There is, therefore, no real reason to assume that the same institutional pull would not help build additional regional growth centres. South Punjab is an obvious example of this administrative logic. The establishment of the South Punjab Secretariat in 2020 acknowledged the problem caused by placing all authority in Lahore. One of its stated purposes was to allow people in the area to have their issues resolved closer to where they live. This recognition was important because it accepted that proximity to government has both economic and social value. Similarly, Hazara has demanded a separate province for many years. In August 2026, proponents again linked the demand with demographic growth, governance and administrative feasibility. Previously, the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Assembly had adopted resolutions in favour of a separate province of Hazara. Bahawalpur has a similar history of demand for a separate province, and that demand persists. A rally calling for the restoration of the province of Bahawalpur was held in August 2026. New centres of gravity Although each of these regions has its own history and political environment, what unites them is the desire for better access to the state’s institutions, greater representation and a greater share of development. Giving these areas their own “economic centre of gravity” would involve creating a new provincial capital with all its attendant institutions: universities would expand and hospitals would gain specialised departments, among other things, providing professional employment opportunities. As the private sector responds to public investment and the movement of people, a region that relies heavily on a distant provincial capital for the income generated by administrative activity will, over time, begin to develop its own administrative and commercial ecosystems. This is when the issue of prosperity becomes most relevant. Balanced development is also difficult to achieve where opportunity is spatially concentrated. Young people should not have to migrate to Lahore, Karachi or other large cities for opportunities such as quality education, decent jobs and access to specialist services. Establishing other centres of administration could help spread some of these opportunities. There is also a simple administrative benefit. Governments can gain a more thorough overview of the people within smaller jurisdictions, attend to needs specific to the district and monitor the progress of schools, hospitals, roads and other such projects. Regional budgets can better reflect such differences in conditions. Lessons from overseas Regional experience offers similar examples. India created Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand and Uttarakhand in 2000 to give greater administrative and development focus to distinct regions, while Bangladesh later created Mymensingh as a separate administrative division. Another example is Indonesia, which in 2022 created new provinces in Papua, stating that the goals were to shorten administrative reach, improve public services, make development more focused and reduce regional disparities. President Joko Widodo called the division an attempt to make development more equitable across a territory too large to be managed in this way. The comparison is relevant to Pakistan because of its own combination of population growth, geographical distance and differential development. Local government is especially important. Prosperity spreads more effectively when authority reaches municipalities and districts rather than remaining concentrated at the provincial tier. Provincial restructuring can create the larger framework, while stronger local institutions carry that change into towns and communities. The process also has to reflect public aspiration. Credible surveys, consultations and economic feasibility work can help establish where demand is strongest and what form people expect restructuring to take. The Constitution already provides a demanding route: Article 239 requires a constitutional amendment to receive two-thirds support in both Houses of Parliament, while an amendment altering a province’s limits also requires two-thirds approval of the affected provincial assembly. That framework gives administrative restructuring the scale of national reform it deserves. Pakistan’s population will continue to grow. Its secondary cities will expand, regional economies will become more important and pressure on existing provincial centres will increase. Governance structures eventually have to respond to that reality. A more distributed administrative system offers a route towards a more distributed economy as well. More centres of government can mean more centres of education, healthcare, employment, investment and political representation. For a country seeking broader prosperity, that is a possibility worth examining seriously.

The forgotten NSP

WHEN the PTI government introduced Pakis­tan’s first-ever National Security Policy (NSP) in 2022, the framework placed its primary emphasis on geo-economics and the pursuit of economic security. However, as this five-year policy nears expiration, it seems that the geo-economic vision and citizen-centric approach envisioned under the NSP have failed to achieve their objectives. This lack of success stems from three major reasons. The foremost reason is the curse of political discontinuity. When prime minister Imran Khan unveiled Pakistan’s first-ever NSP in January 2022, it was hailed as a revolutionary pivot towards a citizen-centric geo-economic future. Yet, just months later, the predictable wheel of Pakistani political instability turned, and with the ouster of the PTI government, the strategic document was promptly relegated to bureaucratic limbo. It seems that in Pakistan’s deeply polarised political landscape, structural continuity is a luxury. Successive administrations traditionally suffer from a profound ‘ownership gap’, and view the policy blueprints of their predecessors with suspicion. This does not mean that the incoming coalition government ripped up the NSP. Rather, the subsequent administrations of the PDM alliance and then the PML-N-led coalition found itself completely consumed by the base instincts of political survival and immediate fiscal fires. These priorities diluted institutional focus and shifted the national priority from long-term economic integration to immediate to short-term emergency bailouts. Ready for the future?

A RECENT McKinsey report outlines three forces reshaping organisations globally — technology and artificial intelligence (AI), economic and geopolitical disruption, and a changing workforce. These forces may appear less immediate to Pakistani business leaders, who already contend with volatile costs, uncertain demand, disrupted supply chains and regulatory uncertainty, amongst other existential issues. With these new challenges, naturally, the question arises: how prepared are our organisations for change? It is a crucial question, but I believe a more fundamental one lies beneath; are Pakistani organisations ready to learn? This shift in focus stems from my belief that organisations should prioritise building capability over simply adopting the latest technology or management ideas. Capability encompasses an organisation’s accumulated ability to achieve its purpose; making sound decisions, developing people, learning from experience and adapting to changing circumstances. Results are undoubtedly important — profit, growth, market share, impact and productivity — but they alone do not define good management. The more important question is whether an organisation is becoming more capable of fulfilling its purpose. Are they making better decisions? Are their people developing? Does experience influence behaviour? Can they respond to environmental changes? And would they continue to perform even if their most senior leader were no longer present? With new challenges, a fundamental question arises: are Pakistani organisations prepared to learn? These questions hold particular relevance for Pakistan. We boast many highly capable founders, family-business leaders and senior executives whose market knowledge, relationships and judge­­­ment have often propelled successful enterprises throu­­gh challenging times. However, personal capability alone does not equate to institutional capability. I have witnessed organisations where every significant decision ultimately falls to the owner(s) or the most experienced person. This applies to difficult customers, major investments, hiring decisions and operational problems — everyone waits for the person who is assumed to know the answer. While this can work well in the short term, with a capable individual at the centre, it leads to a growing dependence on that single person. Managers become adept at escalating problems rather than solving them, and experience becomes concentrated in a few heads rather than embedded in systems, processes and shared judgement. The organisation might grow larger or become more profitable, but it has not necessarily become stronger. True leadership is not measured by whether everything functions when the leader is present; it is whether the organisation has become more capable in their absence. This is one reason Pakistani organisations often struggle with succession. We tend to think of succession as simply finding the next CEO or family member, but it is a much broader question. Has the organisation developed people capable of making decisions? Has it created systems that do not rely on personal intervention? Has it made knowledge accessible beyond those who acquired it? Has it given capable younger managers real exposure to challenging work before they are expected to lead? If not, the organisation may have found a successor, but it has not built succession. The same issue arises with AI. Many leaders are asking which tasks AI can automate, which is a reasonable starting point. However, the more important question is what new capabilities the organisation will need when routine work changes. Technology matters. AI will change how work is done, which skills matter and what customers expect. But technology does not transform an organisation. People using technology differently might. A company can buy the latest systems, appoint a chief digital officer and run pilots in AI. It can still make the same slow decisions, hide the same bad news, reward the same behaviour and depend on the same few senior people. In that case, technology may simply make the organisation more efficient at doing what is becoming less relevant. These are management issues, not technology ones. They also explain why so many transformation programmes disappoint. An organisation announces a new strategy, redraws reporting lines, creates a digital team, brings in consultants and launches a new performance management system. Each of these might be sensible but none automatically changes how the organisation behaves. If people were afraid to speak honestly before the transformation they will remain afraid afterwards. If bad news is softened before reaching senior leadership a new dashboard will simply pre­­sent a cleaner version of the same reality. If managers are rewarded only for their personal results they will have little incentive to develop others. An organisation learns only when experience changes its behaviour. This requires leaders to protect the flow of difficult information. No institution can learn from a reality it is afraid to see. Leaders must distinguish respect for experience from obedience to hierarchy. Older leaders bring perspective while younger people often understand emerging technologies, customers and expectations more instinctively. Healthy organisations create a conversation between the two. The old answer to every new question is the clearest sign of institutional ageing. Pakistan’s organisations do not need to imitate every global management trend or become technology companies. They need to become learning institutions — organisations that can use technology without being defined by it, develop people without abandoning accountability, preserve purpose without protecting every old habit and change before failure makes change unavoidable. The question is not whether Pakistani organisations are ready for change and disruption. It is whether they are ready to learn. ISLAMABAD: The Islamabad High Court (IHC) has ruled that any activity resulting in infringement of citizens’ fundamental rights amounts to a violation of the Constitution, warning that public office holders responsible for such violations would also be deemed to have breached their oath of office. The ruling came in a detailed 37-page judgment on a petition concerning the proposed nationwide PTI protest towards Islamabad on September 27, 2026. PTI had announced the march to demand release of party founder Imran Khan and mobilise people for the supremacy of the Constitution. A citizen, Waqas Ahmed, had challenged the planned move on the grounds that the protest could disrupt routine life, traffic and business activities in the federal capital. A three-member bench headed by Chief Justice Sardar Muhammad Sarfraz Dogar and comprising Justice Muhammad Asif and Justice Muhammad Azam Khan issued the judgment. The bench had announced its short order on September 14 after hearing Attorney General for Pakistan Mansoor Awan, Additional Attorney General Amir Rehman, the advocate generals of the four provinces and Islamabad, and counsel for the petitioner, Barrister Akhter Cheena. The court described the case as requiring an “unusual degree of constitutional balance” between the right of political parties and citizens to assemble peacefully and express dissent, and the rights of other citizens to life, liberty, dignity, free movement, trade, business, property, and access to hospitals, educational institutions, courts and workplaces. “The question is not whether a political party is entitled to protest. It plainly is, subject always to the Constitution and law,” the judgment observed. The court held that the right to peaceful assembly could not be exercised in a manner that infringed the fundamental rights of other citizens. A key objection raised by the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa advocate general concerned the territorial jurisdiction of the IHC over provincial authorities functioning outside Islamabad. Rejecting the objection, the court held that Article 199(1)(c) empowered a high court to issue directions where a governmental power or function was being exercised “in, or in relation to” territory within its jurisdiction. The court clarified, however, that the provision did not confer general supervisory jurisdiction over another province. A high court could issue directions to an authority outside its territorial seat where the governmental function had a direct and proximate legal relation with territory within its jurisdiction and intervention was necessary to protect fundamental rights there. The bench also observed that where executive action or inaction was likely to infringe the fundamental rights of citizens within Islamabad, the court was under a “bounden duty” to assume jurisdiction under Article 199(1)(c). The court also rejected the objection that the petition was not maintainable because KP Chief Minister Sohail Afridi had not been impleaded as a respondent. It noted that the Advocate General KP was representing both the provincial government and the chief minister. The court further rejected the argument that the petitioner had an adequate alternate remedy under the Peaceful Assembly and Public Order Act, 2024. It noted that no application had, admittedly, been filed under Section 3 of the Act and observed that its provisions had not been adhered to by the KP chief minister and others who had announced their intention to march towards Islamabad. The judgment extensively referred to the Supreme Court’s decision in the Faizabad sit-in case, observing that the constitutional right to assemble and protest was protected but remained subject to restrictions where its exercise infringed the fundamental rights of others. The IHC also referred to the Supreme Court judgment concerning the PTI march of May 25, 2022, noting that assurances regarding a peaceful rally and non-obstruction of the Srinagar Highway had not been honoured. The judgment referred to damage to public and private property, fires in the Blue Area greenbelt, injuries to police personnel and the subsequent invocation of Article 245 to call in the armed forces in aid of civil power. The court also cited its November 21, 2024 order directing that no protest, rally or sit-in be allowed in violation of the Peaceful Assembly and Public Order Act. It observed that the order had also been flouted. During the proceedings, the court allowed the Islamabad deputy commissioner/district magistrate to place electronic material on record. Video clips of the 2022 and 2024 protests were played before the bench, which described the conduct shown in the footage as “highly disturbing and reflective of violence and aggression”. The court noted that PTI was in power in KP during both episodes and observed that provincial government resources had been used in pursuit of political objectives. Regarding the proposed September 27 march, the court held that the petitioner’s apprehension was not merely speculative. It noted that the date and destination of the proposed gathering were known and that statements attributed to the KP chief minister indicated that the date was firm and that he intended to lead the march. While disposing of the petition, the court directed that no political party, political leader, provincial government or public office holder could lawfully occupy roads, highways, interchanges, toll plazas, buildings or similar public places within, or leading to or from, Islamabad in a manner that obstructed free movement or hindered trade, business, professions or access to medical and educational facilities.
