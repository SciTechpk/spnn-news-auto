What is fuelling school protests in France?

Protests by teenagers outside their schools have shaken France in recent days, with students blockading entrances to their institutions and security forces on occasion responding with tear gas. The government has said that while the grievances may be legitimate, violence is not and has accused the hard-left of whipping up the movement. Critics have meanwhile accused some police of heavy-handed force against protesters, who are legally still children. More than 5,000 people have been arrested since Monday, according to the interior ministry, while about 735 educational establishments were affected on Friday, the education minister said. While the weekend may see a lull, a major new day of protest is planned for Tuesday. AFP looks at the origins of the movement. Multiple grievances The protests affect lycees, the schools where French teens have their last years of school, aged from 15-18, the equivalent of high school in the United States and sixth form in the United Kingdom. The students’ grievances are numerous, but chief among them are teacher absences, conditions in classrooms, overloaded school timetables and a school day that can be as long as 8:00am to 6:00pm, which some say is excessive. “High-school students — particularly those from low-income neighbourhoods — feel they are receiving an inferior education,” said French sociologist Valerie Becquet. A student puts trash into a fire as protesting students carry out blockading action near place de la Nation, in Paris, on Oct 2, 2026. — AFP Origin of the movement The movement began two weeks ago on Sept 17 at the Saint-Exupéry high school in the Paris suburb of Créteil, in support of teachers striking over a lack of staff and resources. It gained momentum, gradually spreading across the country over the last week. Ironically, as tensions raged elsewhere in France, the situation at the Saint-Exupery high school returned to normal after educational authorities agreed to send additional staff, including a deputy principal and a nurse. “Our grades were slipping. If we had not rallied now it would have augured very badly for the future,” said pupil Abderrahmane, who expressed “enormous” pride at taking part in the inception of the movement. Mayor of Metz Francois Grosdidier (L) stands in front of burned vehicles belonging to staff members during a visit, at the Francois Rabelais secondary school, after the fire started last evening during students’ blockading action protest in Metz, northeastern France on Oct 3, 2026. — AFP Role of social media The protests are being further fuelled by the spread of dramatic images and videos on online platforms, notably TikTok. Students themselves hold up their phones to record scenes such as burning trash cans or police deploying tear gas. Numerous channels, seeking to boost their reach, spread this footage and edit together the most dramatic moments. “With social media, they can offer a narrative different from the one presented in traditional media — a narrative that is not manufactured,” said sociologist Becquet. Social inequality France offers all students access to the lycee but in reality there is a vast spectrum, ranging from vocational schools to elite institutions. Despite numerous diversity initiatives, the latter remain dominated by students from white, affluent families with university-educated parents. “There are glaring inequalities among different students and schools, and this is a real problem,” said Becquet. Political exploitation The protests have become a major issue of political tension just over half a year ahead of presidential elections, with the government accusing the hard-left France Unbowed (LFI) of being behind the movement. Senior LFI official Manuel Bompard has rejected the government’s accusations, saying it was “descending into conspiracy theories”. Veteran LFI presidential candidate Jean-Luc Melenchon — who is currently polling at over 40 per cent among 18-25-year-old voters — has strongly supported the protests, while calling for non-violent methods. Police response Protesters have blockaded schools, set fire to bins and other objects and hurled projectiles at police. But police have also moved to disperse some of the protesters, notably firing tear gas canisters, using rubber bullets and sometimes beating them with batons. After students clashed with police on Wednesday in Tours in central France, one young man wounded lost the use of his right eye, the city’s prosecutor said. Numerous videos have gone viral showing alleged brutal conduct by security forces — such as a police officer placing his arm around the neck of a girl during her arrest. But police have also been targets, notably an officer who was chased and beaten and kicked by a group of youths from a school in the eastern city of Belfort. Yemen presidency announces military drive to retake all territory seized by Houthis

Yemen’s leader announced on Sunday the start of a new military operation to retake all territory held by the Houthis, even as the group made fresh gains on the ground, effectively encircling a government-held city. The announcement sets an ambitious goal for the embattled government, which recently lost swathes of the country’s Red Sea coastline and areas around the Bab al-Mandab strait — a chokepoint for international shipping — after a lightning offensive by Houthis. Yemen’s government has struggled to roll back the surge, even with the military support of Saudi Arabia, where the Houthis said they had attacked oil infrastructure in the capital yet again on Sunday. “I announce today the start of military operations to retake the remaining territories of the republic and extend the authority of the state and its institutions across the entire national territory,” Rashad al-Alimi, the head of Yemen’s presidential council, said in a statement. Alimi called on all the armed forces “to begin carrying out their assigned missions, in accordance with the approved plans, until the country is liberated from the grip of the Houthi terrorist militia”. The statement came just hours after the Houthis cut a vital supply route linking the besieged city of Taiz with the government’s interim capital Aden, according to four security sources. The Houthi advance followed days of heavy fighting in and around Taiz, where dozens of combatants were reported killed on Friday. The battle for Taiz is taking place on several fronts, including highlands overlooking coastal areas recently seized by the Houthis. Taiz is Yemen’s third-largest city after Sanaa and Aden. “We were informed that the Houthis had taken control of the al-Safiya area, where they cut the main road and took control of the police station,” a police source in the area told AFP. Three sources with the Yemeni military also confirmed Houthi control over the area and the supply route, with traffic along the road halted and the group now surrounding Taiz city. The military sources added that fighting in the area was ongoing. Sources from both sides told AFP on Sunday that fighting in and around the western province of Taiz has killed more than 70 people over the past day. Sources from the internationally recognised government reported that 34 government troops were killed — 20 on the edge of Taiz province in neighbouring Lahj — while Houthi military sources said 39 of their fighters were killed in fighting across Taiz and in border areas with Lahj. Taiz city itself remains under the control of government forces but has long been besieged from the north, east and west by Houthi fighters and remains one of the main fronts in Yemen’s long-running civil war. “They’ve achieved the military objective of isolating Taiz from Aden, even if they haven’t fully taken control of it,” Farea Al-Muslimi, research fellow at the Chatham House think-tank, told AFP. But Taiz residents vowed to resist any Houthi advance. “We will defeat and crush anyone who enters the city of Taiz. It has also drawn in Saudi Arabia — leader of an anti-Houthi military coalition since 2015 — with the group regularly claiming attacks on the kingdom’s territory in recent weeks and declaring a blockade of its ships in the Red Sea. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree on Sunday said the group launched ballistic missiles and drones at Saudi energy giant Aramco’s sites in Riyadh and in the oil-rich Khurais area in the east. “The strikes were precise, causing large fires at the targeted sites,” Saree said on X. Experts believe the recent operations have most likely been conducted by Saudi warplanes as well. They have also struck multiple Saudi targets including oil infrastructure and the capital Riyadh while also announcing a naval blockade on Saudi shipping in the Red Sea. WITNESSES testified that a senior banker sketched private parts on the glass of his desk, asked a woman colleague to name them, and threatened to tear off her clothes if she wore a particular outfit again. Another officer made indecent remarks about her attire before colleagues. The ombudsperson found the charges proved; the Islamabad High Court agreed. On Sept 16, the Federal Constitutional Court (FCC) set that judgement aside without substantively engaging with this evidence or the high court’s careful appraisal of it. Its central reason was a definition. Harassment under the 2010 workplace harassment law, it declared, “is only restricted to conduct which is sexual in nature”. Even by that narrow test, the conduct proved was plainly sexual. Before the high court, the officers never contended it fell short of harassment. Why does the FCC confine harassment to sex? The Act’s title and preamble speak of ‘harassment’, not ‘sexual harassment’, and anchor it in the right to dignity. Nor does ‘sexual’ mean only the act of sex; it also means relating to the sexes, or to gender. The Supreme Court said so on review in Nadia Naz, through Justice Ayesha Malik; “sexually demeaning attitudes” means demeaning a person because of their sex. Yale’s Vicki Schultz has shown that much workplace harassment has nothing to do with desire; it belittles women and signals they do not belong. And the 2022 amendment, ignored altogether, covers gender discrimination “which may or may not be sexual in nature”. Courts elsewhere agree. The US supreme court held in Oncale that harassment need not be motivated by desire; Canada and India root it in equality and dignity. India’s law lists “sexually coloured remarks”, exactly what was proved, and the 2021 Nadia Naz judgement the FCC relies on quoted that very provision. ILO Convention 190 treats gender-based harassment, including sexual harassment, as one wrong. This is where a constitutional court should begin. Article 14 declares the dignity of man inviolable, and the Act’s own preamble rests on it. A constitutional court’s first duty is to read statutes through fundamental rights and expand their frontiers, not shrink them. A woman made to name drawings of private parts before her colleagues has had her dignity violated by any measure. Constitutional courts are judged not by the powers they claim but by the rights they protect. Our Supreme Court had already arrived there. In Uzma Naveed (2022) and Muhammad Din (2025), both of which I authored, it held that harassment is not confined to the act of sex and that dignity is absolute and non-negotiable. The FCC dismisses Muhammad Din in one sentence, “with due respect”, ignores Uzma Naveed, and leans instead on the 2021 Nadia Naz judgement the Supreme Court set aside on review. In Haji Adam, decided by the same author the same day, substantial passages reappear word for word; it even names the bank case’s witnesses as the Pemra complainant’s. That raises a serious question whether each record received independent judicial consideration. Precedent is not a courtesy; it is law. Judgements of the Supreme Court delivered before the 27th Amendment bound every court under Article 189. The amendment operates prospectively: it makes the FCC’s decisions binding from now on, but it does not erase nearly eight decades of constitutional jurisprudence. Having inherited the Supreme Court’s constitutional jurisdiction, the FCC inherited the law declared under it. If it doubts that law, the doctrine of precedent requires reasoned reconsideration by a larger bench, not a three-member bench’s single sentence. Even the FCC’s own test, set this March, forbids precedent being “ignored and disregarded in silence”. The deeper problem is the court itself. It was created by the 27th Amendment, rushed through a parliament whose own mandate remains clouded by the disputed elections of February 2024, and carved out of a functioning Supreme Court for reasons never convincingly explained. Britain, the US, India, Canada and Australia entrust constitutional questions to a single apex court, and for good reason: splitting the apex invites conflict and political engineering. South Africa moved the other way in 2013, making its constitutional court the apex court in all matters. Its first bench was chosen by the president on the prime minister’s advice, bypassing the Judicial Commission, with no published criteria or open assessment of merit. Such a court must earn confidence through its work. A judgement rendered without hearing the attorney general or appointing an amicus (even the high court had one), and thin on scholarship and constitutional understanding, does not earn it. Nor does the price: salaries above the Supreme Court’s until that court was raised to match, generous staff allowances, and three more years of tenure. Not a single woman sits on the FCC, a conspicuous absence in a harassment case. I cast no aspersion on any judge who accepted appointment; that is for each conscience. Yet the absence speaks. The women of our judiciary stand apart from this court and deserve the country’s admiration. Such judgements belong to the bar too, for it is very much part of this new judicial era. Bar associations are guarantor institutions; in 2007 they marched until judges were restored. When the architecture of the 26th and 27th Amendments was assembled, that resistance never came. Too many at the bar’s helm measure ambition by proximity to power rather than fidelity to democracy. That is a deficit of character, not of numbers. When the guardians fall silent, unconstitutionalism rises. Constitutional courts are judged not by the powers they claim but by the rights they protect. If this is the direction of travel, dark years lie ahead for rights. Faiz urged us to speak while our lips are still free. It now falls to the lawyers who remember 2007, the academy and those who refuse to be silenced to keep the lamps lit. Analysis: Why lower inflation still feels expensive

A SLOWDOWN in inflation may offer some statistical relief to the government, but prices of everyday essentials remain substantially higher than they were two years ago. The apparent contradiction lies in the difference between what inflation measures and what consumers experience when they enter the market. Headline inflation measures how quickly the overall price basket is changing. It does not show how far the general price level has already risen. The official measure is based on prices collected from different markets and cities and combined into urban, rural and national indices. Therefore, a national average may not correspond to the retail price paid by an individual consumer in a particular market. Lower inflation does not mean prices have returned to previous levels. It simply means that they are rising more slowly on average. Consumers continue to bear the cumulative impact of price increases recorded in previous years. This helps explain why easing inflation has yet to translate into a comparable reduction in the cost of living. A comparison of Pakistan Bureau of Statistics prices between October 2024 and October 2026 illustrates the extent of that accumulated burden. Several essential food and energy items remained substantially more expensive than they were two years earlier, even though headline inflation later moderated. Among the sharpest increases were those in onions, electricity, diesel and petrol. Slower inflation masks cumulative rise in essential prices over past two years These individual price movements do not, of course, represent overall inflation, which is calculated from a weighted basket of goods and services. But they help explain the disconnect between official inflation readings and what consumers encounter in retail markets. Wheat flour provides one of the clearest examples. The national average price of a 20kg flour bag increased from Rs1,835 in October 2024 to Rs2,697 in October 2026 — an increase of 47 per cent. Beef with bone became 26.7pc more expensive, while the price of a 2.5kg tin of vegetable ghee rose by 18.5pc. Retail prices in individual markets can be even higher than these national averages. The increases were steeper in transport fuels. Petrol rose from Rs248.18 per litre in October 2024 to Rs389.51 in October 2026, an increase of 56.9pc. During the 1970s and 1980s, a dramatic political shift permanently altered the geopolitical topography of the Middle East and the broader Muslim world. Islamism gained momentum as secular nationalism declined. It was a collapse accelerated by the defeat of Egypt, Syria and Jordan in the 1967 Arab-Israeli War. At the time, Western powers viewed Islamism, a right-wing ideology opposed to socialism and secular nationalism, as a useful counterweight to Soviet-backed nationalist regimes in Egypt, Syria, Iraq and, later, Libya. One of the most consequential applications of this strategy unfolded in the Israeli-occupied West Bank and Gaza. Anti-Israel Palestinian resistance was largely dominated by secular and left-leaning groups operating primarily within the Israeli-occupied territories of Palestine and neighbouring nationalist Arab states. The French scholar Jean-Pierre Filiu wrote that, in order to neutralise Palestinian resistance groups, Israel quietly nurtured the growth of local Islamist movements, specifically the Palestinian branch of the Muslim Brotherhood. This was a deliberate strategy, designed to fragment and undermine the primary face of Palestinian nationalism: Yasser Arafat’s left-leaning and secular Palestine Liberation Organisation (PLO). For Israeli strategists, the PLO’s anti-colonial rhetoric posed a serious political threat. To curb the PLO’s influence, Israeli military intelligence began to cultivate a counterweight in the shape of Sheikh Ahmed Yassin. A charismatic Palestinian cleric, Yassin had begun building a social and religious network under the Muslim Brotherhood umbrella in Gaza, culminating in the founding of al-Mujama al-Islami in 1973. The PLO viewed Yassin’s followers as “quietist fundamentalists” preoccupied with social welfare and moral purification. The PLO did not exhibit concern even when Israeli authorities permitted al-Mujama to operate unhindered. In 1979, the Israeli military administration formally recognised the al-Mujama charity, granting it permission to establish mosques, schools and medical clinics across Gaza.
