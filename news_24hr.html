'Conscience does not permit': LHC's Justice Kayani resigns, cites executive's growing control over judiciary

LAHORE: Lahore High Court’s (LHC) Justice Mohsin Akhtar Kayani resigned on Wednesday, citing personal reasons, including health concerns following a recent surgery, and “reasons of conscience”. In his resignation letter to the president of Pakistan, a copy of which is available with Dawn, Justice Kayani said it had been a privilege for him to serve at the Islamabad High Court (IHC) and LHC. “These years of judicial service remain among the most meaningful and fulfilling of my life. During my tenure, I have disposed of more than 23,000 cases of various categories, endeavouring always to uphold the Constitution, safeguard fundamental rights, and administer justice without fear or favour,” he continued. However, he added, “in my considered view, the changes introduced through the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments have compromised the independence and institutional integrity of the high courts”. “I have witnessed what I regard as the executive’s growing control over judicial institutions, extending to the appointment and transfer of judges. Such interference has, in my assessment, seriously eroded public confidence in judicial independence and the fairness of the judicial process.” Justice Kayani stated that of particular concern was the plight of the ordinary litigant, “who is the principal stakeholder in the justice system, yet whose suffering continues to deepen”. The needs of those seeking justice had too often been neglected, while other actors had become the “beneficiaries of a system intended to serve them”, he said. “In these circumstances, my conscience does not permit me to continue on the bench. This is a painful decision, but it reflects my conviction that judicial office must remain inseparable from independence, fidelity to the Constitution, and the protection of human dignity. “I, therefore, consider that the time has come for me to lay down my robes and return to legal practice, through which I intend to continue serving the people, protecting their rights, and promoting legal awareness,” he added. The judge ended his letter expressing gratitude to “my teachers, fellow judges and family members for their guidance and steadfast support, including during the deliberations that led to this decision”. His resignation has come years before the date of his retirement — Feb 10, 2032. He hails from Islamabad and was serving at the IHC before his transfer to the LHC in April. He was assigned to the LHC’s principal seat in Lahore. Despite being a senior judge of the LHC, he was not assigned to any division bench. Justice Kayani previously served as the senior puisne judge of the IHC and was expecting to be elevated to the post of the high court’s chief justice. However, the transfer of incumbent IHC Chief Justice Sardar Muhammad Sarfraz Dogar from the LHC, along with two other judges from the Sindh and Balochistan high courts, prompted him to challenge the inter-high court transfers. The Supreme Court’s constitutional bench upheld the transfers to the IHC, including that of Justice Dogar. The decision, however, triggered a broader legal battle. Justice Kayani and other senior IHC judges approached the Supreme Court to challenge the administrative powers exercised by Chief Justice Dogar. They alleged that the office had been turned into a “monocracy” and that judges could be excluded from the roster at will. Following his own transfer to the LHC, Justice Kayani lost his seniority and was placed 12th on the high court’s seniority list. His resignation came days after he returned to the twin cities to preside over a single bench at the Rawalpindi registry following a reshuffle at the high court. The new duty roster assigned him only to a single bench at the Rawalpindi registry despite his previous position as the IHC’s senior puisne judge. Education and career According to information available on the LHC’s website, Justice Kayani completed his early education at FG Boys High School in Islamabad and pursued higher education at FG College for Men. He graduated in 1989. The judge earned his LLB from the University of Punjab in 1994, followed by an MA degree in Political Science in 1996, an LLM from Karachi University in 2003 and a MSc in Pakistan Studies in 2005. He also has postgraduate diploma in human rights from Peshawar University in collaboration with the Norwegian Institute of Human Rights, Oslo University. Justice Kayani was enrolled as an advocate of the district courts in 1995 and ascended to the high court in 1997 and the Supreme Court in 2009. He was appointed as an IHC judge on Dec 23, 2015 and transferred to the LHC on April 29 this year. The judge also served as the joint secretary of the Islamabad District Bar Association from 1998 to 1999, general secretary of the Islamabad District Bar Association from 2002 to 2003 and the president of the Islamabad High Court Bar Association from 2013 to 2014. “He has presided over significant cases, particularly those related to enforced disappearances. Constitutional questions

PAKISTAN has had three constitutions. Two given to it by its people in 1956 and 1973. One promulgated by a field marshal in 1962. The 1973 Constitution was adopted by the people through their representatives in the constituent assembly. Article 1 states that Pakistan shall be a federal republic. Neither the term “federal” nor “republic” is expressly defined. The Objectives Resolution provides guidance. The power and authority of the state in our republic is to be exercised through the chosen representatives of the people. A republic is distinct from rule by one man. In a republic, rulers are not immune from accountability. They can be removed and replaced if they fail to perform. The various territories that are part of Pakistan or that accede to Pakistan form a federation. These territories are autonomous and their boundaries and powers and authority are prescribed. The Objectives Resolution guarantees that the integrity of the territories shall be safeguarded. A federal state is brought into being by the territories that comprise it through a written instrument. This instrument defines the authority of the federation and these territories. The federation presupposes and is premised on the existence of these territories. Dismantling the territories would undermine the foundational integrity of the federation and the compact reflected in the 1973 Constitution. A constitution can be amended. The limits of a province or territory can be changed with its consent. Such an amendment may alter limits or provincial boundaries. It does not contemplate partition or subdivision of existing provinces or existing territories. Actions that compromise provincial integrity inevitably lead to questions regarding the benefits of being part of the federation. These questions have been resolved by the various territories and reflected in the 1973 Constitution. It would not be wise to reopen them. Emergency powers cannot be used to deprive any citizen of the right to a fair trial. Provincial integrity is not incompatible with devolution of power and effective local government. The Principles of Policy of the Constitution require the state to encourage local government institutions. Each province is constitutionally required to establish a local government system and to devolve power. The president is the head of state and represents the unity of the republic. The president as a symbol of a united federation must be satisfied prior to provincial autonomy being assailed. This can only be done in exceptional circumstances. The emergency provisions enable federal rule over a province if the president is satisfied that certain conditions exist. The president does not have authority to impo­­se an emergency whenever he deems fit. The eme­rgency provisions are not like the erstwhile Arti­cle 58(2)(b). The president was empowered under this Article to dissolve an elected government in his discretion. The emergency powers are not exp­ressed to be discretionary powers of the president. Could the president have refused to sign the recent emergency proclamation notwithstanding the advice of the federal government? He could certainly have asked the government to look at matters again. Did he have discretion to refuse if after reconsideration the government insisted? The term “discretion” is not used in the relevant Articles of the Constitution. The conventional legal view is that absent a power being expressed as discretionary, the president is bound to act on the advice of the government. In the case of the emergency provisions the president is required to be “satisfied”. This suggests a mental state of an individual office holder. It could be argued that a mental state cannot be imposed through governmental advice. Regardless, in the present emergency, this appears to be a moot point since the president did not even ask the government to re­­c­onsider its advice. We do not know the nature of the material made available to the president to satisfy him beyond what is contained in the proclamation. Its contents are in substance assertions and non-speaking. They do not provide cogent reasons. If the stipulated conditions for an emergency do not exist, the proclamation is unlawful. It is incorrect to state that because emergency powers exist in the Constitution, the exercise thereof is constitutional. The existence of a power and its lawful exercise are two distinct matters. Emergency powers cannot be used to deprive any citizen of Pakistan of the right to a fair trial. The emergency provisions enable suspension of operation of a provision of the Constitution only relating to a body or authority in the province. They do not permit the suspension of Article 10-A of the Constitution which provides for a fair trial and due process. The federal government’s order pursuant to the proclamation states that Article 175(3) shall not apply to the designated districts. This Article provides for separation of the judiciary from the executive. The constitutional framework does not contemplate such suspension. The emergency provisions also do not authorise the subdivision of a province by creating a separate governance structure for certain designated parts absent provincial legislative consent. Where certain federally administered areas have been made part of a province through a constitutional amendment (as is the case with Fata), the impact of this amendment cannot lawfully be undone through a proclamation of emergency which is an executive action. Depriving people of a territory of rule by their representatives is undemocratic and diminishes the federation. When imposed on an unwilling provincial government, it pits the centre against one of its parts and weakens the whole. Normal constitutional functioning should be restored as soon as possible. The real question that needs consideration is whether currently Pakistan is truly operating as a federal republic. That is what is contemplated by the first Article of our Constitution. If the compact that binds the territories that comprise our nation is broken, what credible vision can the rulers offer the people in these territories? Govt to end slab system punishing power consumers crossing 200-unit threshold

ISLAMABAD: Power Minister Awais Leghari told the Senate on Tuesday that the government has decided to scrap the electricity slab system that punishes consumers for crossing the 200-unit threshold even by one unit, and to provide targeted subsidy only to those deserving. Under the current mechanism, consumers using up to 200 units per month fall under the “protected” category and avail a subsidy of around Rs13 per unit. However, any consumer exceeding the limit by even one unit has to pay up to Rs33 per unit and is then not allowed to avail the facility for six months. Speaking in the Upper House, Leghari said work was being expedited to abolish the system under which a consumer crossing 200 units has to pay a much higher cost per unit and remains ineligible for relief for the next six months. The minister was responding to a calling-attention notice moved by PML-N Senator Abid Sher Ali regarding tariffs charged by distribution companies (Discos)and the financial burden on consumers. Ali highlighted that when monthly consumption increased from 200 to 201 units, the tariff jumped from Rs15 to Rs30 per unit, forcing a consumer to pay bills under the 400-unit slab for just one extra unit. “The cruel slab system effectively punished the poor and drained the common man for months,” he said. Acknowledging it as a genuine problem, Leghari said the ministry was seriously working to abolish the six-month bar. He said the government was considering two options: raising the threshold from 200 to 250 units; or abolishing the six-month penalty by allowing consumers to get the protected category benefit for the month in which they restrict consumption to 200 units. “It will take one and a half months to finalise the plan,” the power minister outlined, adding that the government was working on a new system to provide relief based on income rather than consumption. He pointed out that the number of protected consumers had increased from 10 million to 20-25m during the last two years due to the use of solar energy. Many of them, including consumers using 700 to 800 units, were limiting grid consumption to 200 units by installing solar panels and availing relief meant for lower-income households. “It would not be appropriate to offer equal discount to everyone using less than 200 units,” Leghari said, adding that the government was providing a 65 per cent subsidy to protected consumers which was being availed by rich and poor alike. The new mechanism would restrict subsidy to those living below the poverty line and lower-middle income groups, he explained. Turning to the power sector’s financial situation, Leghari said circular debt had declined to around Rs1.67 trillion and was no longer increasing every year. The government had set a target to eliminate it within the next four years under the reprofiling of the financing scheme, and the debt servicing surcharge of Rs3.23 per unit would also be abolished during the same period, the minister added. Leghari recalled that when Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif took office in 2024, the annual losses of Discos, including theft and non-recovery, were Rs591bn. The losses had come down from Rs360bn last year to Rs325bn this year due to efforts to improve efficiency. He maintained that the Power Division was getting Rs1.18tr in budgetary support from the finance ministry in 2024, which was expected to come down to between Rs700bn-750bn this year, reducing the burden on the annual budget by about Rs200bn. However, Leghari acknowledged that since consumers did not bear the losses of companies, and it was the finance ministry that bore the burden, the benefit of reduced losses had not yet been passed on to consumers. He said preventing electricity theft remained the government’s top priority, but consumers would not be burdened arbitrarily. Justice Ayesha Malik challenges assumptions blaming women for 'inviting' rape

The judge was addressing participants at the launch of a bench book on the adjudication of rape cases for judges of gender-based violence (GBV) courts in Islamabad. The event was held in collaboration between the Ministry of Human Rights (MoHR), the Legal Aid Society (LAS) and the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA). Delivering the keynote address at the event, Justice Malik expressed concern that sexual violence had become so routine that it no longer provoked sufficient outrage. She challenged assumptions that blamed female victims of sexual violence by suggesting that they ‘invite’ rape, calling for a fundamental re-examination of attitudes towards women that allowed such assumptions to persist. The bench book provides a practical reference to support fair trials and judicial decisions grounded in law and evidence, according to a statement by the MoHR. It was launched alongside a national study on low conviction rates in GBV cases. The two publications address connected challenges across the criminal justice process, the statement said. Director general of the MoHR’s Child Protection Institute, Rabeea Hadi, presented the study on low conviction rates, prepared by the ministry alongside the Senate’s Functional Committee on Human Rights. The study examined “where cases falter, from the first report through investigation, medico-legal examination, prosecution and trial”. Meanwhile, LAS Director of Gender, Inclusion and Development, Advocate Maliha Zia, presented the structure of the bench book and its intended use during court proceedings. The bench book brings together applicable law and judicial guidance in 10 chapters following the sequence of a trial, and supports judges in assessing evidence that is placed before them, according to the rights ministry. The chairperson of the Special Committee on the Anti-Rape (Investigation and Trial) Act 2021, Senator Ayesha Raza Farooq, addressed the practical application of the act. “The bench book takes what Parliament enacted, together with what the superior courts have held, and places it within reach of the judge who has to apply it,” she said. LAS Chief Executive Officer Barrister Haya Emaan Zahid illustrated the survivor’s journey by highlighting the pressures survivors face and the need to assess delayed reporting, gaps in medical evidence and family power dynamics in their proper context. UNFPA Pakistan’s Representative Dr Luay Shabaneh linked fair adjudication to Pakistan’s international human rights commitments, noting: “A judgment that is correctly reasoned protects everybody in and outside the courtroom.” The British High Commission’s Senior Governance Adviser Mary Kate Thomson highlighted that public trust was shaped by how everyday cases were handled, and influenced whether survivors came forward. Opening the event, Abdul Khalique Shaikh, secretary of the Ministry of Human Rights, affirmed the ministry’s custodianship of both documents. “The honest position is that the word on paper and the practice on the ground have not yet converged,” he said, calling for attention to be drawn to how legal protections were delivered in practice. The event brought together the judiciary, prosecutors, parliamentarians, senior government officials, legal practitioners and development partners, “with a shared focus on translating legal protections into consistent practice throughout the justice process”, the ministry’s statement said.
