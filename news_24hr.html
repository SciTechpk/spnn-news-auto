Terrible 9/11

THE 11th day of September, 2001 (9/11), started out as an ordinary day in bustling New York. As residents headed to work, the first plane struck one of the twin towers of the World Trade Centre at 8.46 am. Minutes later, another plane hit the second one. A third plane hit the Pentagon building while a fourth was downed in Pennsylvania. This was a massive terrorist attack against what was then the world’s sole superpower. An ordinary day had turned into a watershed moment for global geopolitics. So much changed so rapidly. First, transnational terrorism emerged on top of the global priority list. Prompted by the US, the UN Security Council ado­p­ted on Sept 28, 2001, Resolution 1373, which bound all member states to prevent, suppress, and criminalise the financing of terrorism. A few years later, Resolution 1540 was adopted by the UNSC, obliging member states to prevent non-state actors from acquiring weapons of mass destruction. Thus evolved a comprehensive global ecosystem against non-state actors carrying out terrorist attacks anywhere in the world. Second, the US and Nato countries launched a ferocious attack against Taliban-led Afghanistan, which had given refuge to Al Qaeda, the group the US held responsible for 9/11. This embroiled Washington in a war that became its longest since the end of World War II. With Pakistan’s help, the US was able to eliminate the Al Qaeda leadership, except for Osama bin Laden, who was killed later in 2011. However, the US war against the Taliban dragged on with no success. The US tried to blame Pakistan for its military defeat in Afghanistan because of the support that the Taliban had allegedly received. Pakistan’s view all along was that this issue should be resolved via political means. The US later took Pakistan’s advice and concluded a peace deal with the Taliban in February 2020, but did not take the second piece of advice regarding a responsible withdrawal. Instead, it abruptly withdrew its troops from Afghanistan in August 2021, creating a vacuum that was filled by the Taliban rather than an interim government comprising all Afghan factions. Further, the US left behind massive arms and ammunition, which have since been used for terrorism by entities like the TTP and BLA. Sadly, 25 years after 9/11, Afghanistan is once again a sanctuary for global terrorists. Third, barely two years after 9/11, the US decided to invade Iraq to destroy alleged weapons of mass destruction. This attack set a dangerous precedent of using force illegally (not sanctioned by the UNSC) and pre-emptively, ie, merely on suspicion. This precedent has since been followed by other powers against their adversaries, as seen in the Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2014 and 2022 and India’s aggression against Pakistan in 2016, 2019, and 2025. Armed conflicts have now expanded to every corner of the world. According to the ICRC, there are 130 active armed conflicts worldwide. The events of 9/11 changed so much so rapidly. Fourth, Pakistan became the front-line state in the US-led global war against terrorism, mainly because America needed Pakistan’s help in fighting Al Qaeda based in neighbouring Afghanistan. Pakistan ended up not only having to deal with infiltrating terrorists and extremists from Afghanistan but also prosecuting extremist outfits that had taken root in the country ever since the days of the so-called Afghan jihad of the 1980s. Pakistan thus found itself “in the line of fire” — the title of Gen Musharraf’s book. Fifth, the events of 9/11 provided India a self-serving opportunity to team up with the West against so-called radical Islam and project itself as a victim of what New Delhi alleged was Pakistan-sponsored terrorism in Indian-occupied Kashmir. India also used the occasion to cast the Kashmiri struggle for the right to self-determination as transnational terrorism. Sixth, the new concept ‘responsibility to protect’ was born, with powerful countries arguing that if a state’s leadership was unable or unwilling to protect its own people, then it became the responsibility of the international community to protect them. Using this concept, the West intervened in several Arab countries to bring about regime change. This was described as ‘Arab Spring’, but, as time showed, the events destabilised the entire Middle East and turned out to be an ‘Arab autumn’. The terrorist incidents of 9/11 profoundly changed global and regional geopolitics. The era of globalisation became the first casualty. The use of force emerged as the main arbiter of international disputes. The definition between freedom struggle and terrorism was blurred. Multilateralism was marginalised and international law violated with impunity. The world disorder that one sees today is rooted in what happened on 9/11 — that fateful September day. Pakistan: 25 years post-2001

THE terrorist events of Sept 11, 2001, changed the world and brought about a new security order. Social engineering took place on a large scale, especially within Muslim societies. Conflict erupted across Africa and Asia, often linked to terrorism. These developments triggered large-scale migration towards the West and strengthened the far right in Western societies. Populism took multiple forms. In short, the world changed course, and an era of increasingly controlled societies emerged, affecting technology, the economy, mobility and politics. The 25th anniversary of the tragic incident has triggered a debate: what has the world gained and lost since these events, and what kind of world em­­erged in their aftermath? This question is equally important for Pakistan, as it became a front-line state in the global war against terrorism. Pakistan’s 25-year experience can perhaps be summarised in one sentence: the country has fai­led to defeat terrorism completely, lost Afgha­nis­tan, witnessed weakened internal cohesion, experienced the rise of authoritarianism, and its economy remains in disarray. The return of the Taliban to power in Afghanistan in 2021 contributed to renewed challenges, particularly from the banned TTP. Meanwhile, Balochistan remains another major security theatre, with separatist groups targeting security forces, infrastructure, and Chinese interests. Economically, Pakistan received billions of dollars in US assistance and Coalition Support Funds but failed to convert this geopolitical opportunity into lasting economic transformation. Terrorism imposed enormous costs through loss of life, reduced investment, damaged infrastructure, and increased security expenditure. However, Pakistan has also achieved significant strategic successes. Before 9/11, Pakistan was under sanctions because of its nuclear tests. The Kargil conflict had contributed to political instability and military rule, and Pakistan experienced some global isolation. After September 2001, however, Pakistan emerged from isolation to become a front-line ally of the US in its global ‘war on terror’. Pakistan was later designated a major non-Nato ally. However, this partnership came to be characterised by mistrust, particular­ly over Afghanistan, terrorist sanctuaries, drone strikes, and the 2011 Abbottabad operation. It ended the ability of terrorist groups to exercise territorial control in areas such as Swat and the former tribal districts. In foreign relations, Pakistan strengthened its strategic partnership with China and expanded ties with the Gulf countries and Türkiye, but its relations with Afghanistan have remained deeply problematic. While it took nearly two decades to fully abandon the use of radical and militant proxies on the external and internal political fronts, two factors played an important role. First, international pressure forced the establishment to take measures against militant groups such as Lashkar-e-Taiba and Jaish-e-Mohammed. Second, many members of such groups became disillusioned and joined the TTP, Al Qaeda, and IS-K, making it increasingly difficult for security operators to treat and manage these networks separately. Although these groups are no longer openly active in militancy, they still shoulder political and diplomatic baggage. India frequently exploits this issue to confront Pakistan at the diplomatic level and has used it to build pressure against Pakistan, contributing directly to the military confrontation last year. However, the international community increasingly recognises Pakistan’s efforts to overcome its terrorism problem, and India’s terrorism narrative against Pakistan appears to be losing some of its effectiveness internationally. Over the last 25 years, terrorism has emerged as a core problem for the country, while counterterrorism has become one of its key strengths. Pakistan remains among the countries most affected by terrorism. Security concerns have negatively impacted Pakistan-China relations, especially CPEC. At the same time, counterterrorism has provided an avenue for cooperation between the US and Pakistan, and since President Donald Trump took office, the two have developed greater synergy against IS-K and in dealing with the Taliban, apart from their multilevel engagement in the Gulf region. Counterterrorism has created an imbalance of power, as civilian governments gradually handed over internal and external security affairs to the establishment and re­­frained from interfering in order to avoid friction. As a result, political engineering became more convenient for the establishment. Social and political engineering have also changed society and left it without direction. Terrorism remai­­ns a major obstacle to attracting foreign investment into the country, yet Pakistan’s counterterrorism capabilities have become a strength that allows it to engage with Western and Asian countries. From Gen Pervez Musharraf onwards, military leaders have tried to take credit for the counterterrorism efforts. Yet it is paradoxical that very few acknowledged terrorism as an indigenous problem rooted partly in previous state policies of nurturing militant proxies. In alliance with the clergy, these policies created an infrastructure of extremism in the country. Religious groups were used for multiple purposes — from advancing nationalist projects to acting as pressure groups. Their perception of terrorism was simplistic, and simplistic approaches produced poor policies and flawed strategies. During these years, Pakistan lost 75,366 people, including 3,189 soldiers, 2,598 FC personnel, 3,599 police officers, and 26,547 civilians, apart from casualties in other security institutions. Pakistan launched 2,961 military operations, both medium- and large-scale, against terrorists, in addition to several other types of military operations against terrorism. The country has established dedicated counterterrorism institutions, including Nacta (lately in the news for another reason), counter-extremism centres, NFTIC, and others. Pakistan has also produced 13 policy documents, ranging from the National Action Plan and National Security Policy to Paigham-i-Pakistan. He said that during his visits, he realised there was a disconnect between the district judiciary and the National Judicial Policy Making Committee (NJPMC). The NJPMC was set up under an ordinance in 2002 to coordinate and harmonise judicial policy within the court system and in coordination with the Law and Justice Commission of Pakistan. In his remarks, CJP Afridi said the body had the authority to formulate policies, and sanction projects originating from the district judiciary and financed by the Access to Justice Fund. He said that projects sent by district courts previously took three to four months to secure approval, while some were never completed due to ballooning costs. The CJP said the NJPMC subsequently decided to appoint additional secretaries for each province to serve as a “link” between the committee, high courts and the district judiciary. He said a 21-day timeline had also been set for approval of projects and disbursement of finances under the Access to Justice Fund. In his remarks, CJP Afridi said the district judiciary was asked to submit a “wishlist” of its most pressing needs, which identified four priorities: uninterrupted electricity, internet connectivity, essential facilities for women and clean water. He said the NJPMC decided to allocate funds for the four projects. He said that Rs900 million had been disbursed from the Access to Justice Fund over the previous 20 years, but Rs3.4 billion was released in one year for the aforementioned four projects. The CJP said the NJPMC had also taken several steps to improve the working conditions and facilities available to judicial officers across the country. Justice Afridi said the NJPMC had also introduced changes in the code of conduct for judicial officers and established a set procedure to deal with any “extraneous influence”. “As far as you are concerned, be assured that you are not alone. This assurance is only for those judicial officers who are honest … if we find out that you are making those approaches, this assurance of the CJP will not be extended to you … I expect that a judicial officer will be independent and not approachable by any extraneous influence,” he said. He said Balochistan faced difficulties in transporting solar equipment to remote areas, but the chief justice of the province had assured him that the work would be completed within 30 days. CJP Afridi said the same facilities should also be available to bar associations, adding that where electricity was available in courts, it should not be restricted to the courtrooms but should also serve the bars.
