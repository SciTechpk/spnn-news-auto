The nuclear danger at sea

Dawn’s October 23 editorial rightly warned about India’s nuclear-armed submarine patrols. The danger is larger than a new vessel at sea. It is a sea-based nuclear force growing alongside precision-strike capabilities, missile defence and a false confidence in initiating military action. Imagining that these developments confer a first-strike advantage or create a safe space for war is imprudent. Pakistan has the capability and resolve to preserve the strategic balance. Retired Lt-Gen Mazhar Jamil, Adviser to the National Command Authority, warned at the International Institute for Strategic Studies on October 13 that continuous at-sea deterrence further weakens the credibility of India’s declared no-first-use policy and points towards an offensive strike posture. He added that unannounced submarine-launched ballistic-missile tests, with operationalised weapons aboard submarines, raise the risks of misperception and escalation. Pakistan reserves the right to take all appropriate measures to protect its security and the regional balance. At the Islamabad Non-Proliferation Conference a week later, retired Lt-Gen Khalid Kidwai, also an adviser to the National Command Authority, reinforced the warning, saying Pakistan would review its options if India sought to impose a strategic imbalance. In June 2026, the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (Sipri) estimated that India had a stockpile of 190 nuclear warheads and 12 of these deployed on a nuclear-powered ballistic-missile submarine for occasional deterrence patrols. That estimate is too low and arrives seven years late. Prime Minister Modi announced the INS Arihant’s first deterrence patrol in 2018 and the Indian Navy again patrolled again during the 2019 crisis. India has three operational ballistic-missile submarines (SSBN), with a fourth expected in 2027. Next year, the four SSBNs will have a combined capacity to launch missiles carrying up to 72 warheads from 24 tubes. A larger fleet of 12 nuclear ballistic and attack submarines is envisaged in the 2030s. Aggressive posturing India’s professed no-first-use pledge cannot be judged without taking into account its capabilities and political conduct. A ready, concealed launch platform gains a different meaning when combined with counterforce ambitions, missile defence and talk of pre-emption. General Kidwai’s assessment about a possible strike at ‘zero notice’ should be read in that setting. Pakistan has never allowed its deterrence capability to be neutralised by India’s posture that is taken as routine modernisation. Strategic stability rests on a credible balance and Pakistan will maintain it under all circumstances. General Zubair Hayat, former Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff Committee, argued at the Islamabad Non-proliferation Conference that the old international order is giving way without an equitable replacement. His strategic point applies here: strategic exemptions for a favoured state, followed by alarm at its neighbour’s balancing measures, do not produce equal security. The nuclearisation of the Indian Ocean did not begin when Pakistan raised its concerns. The Indian Air Force grounded itself, and the Indian Navy’s carrier strike group cut a hasty retreat to safety.
