Beyond New Delhi: Bangladesh redraws its foreign policy

For fifty-four years, the story of Bangladesh’s foreign policy was written mostly in one direction: towards New Delhi. It made sense, in its way. India had helped midwife the country’s birth in 1971, and the governments that followed, especially the long Awami League tenure under Sheikh Hasina, built a relationship with India so close that Bangladeshi bureaucrats trained in Indian academies, security services coordinated quietly with their Indian counterparts, and trade routes bent towards Indian ports almost by default. It was a partnership. It was also, for many ordinary Bangladeshis, a cage. That cage door swung open in August 2024. A student-led uprising toppled Hasina’s government, and in the vacuum that followed, an interim administration led by Nobel laureate Muhammad Yunus began doing something Dhaka had not seriously attempted in half a century: looking around the room at options other than India. Pakistan, once the unmentionable neighbour, became one of them. This is not a small story, and it is not, as some in Delhi’s commentariat have framed it, a conspiracy or a betrayal. It is something more interesting and more legitimate: a mid-sized nation exercising the same right every sovereign state claims for itself — the right to diversify, hedge and choose its partners on merit rather than inheritance. The facts on the ground are not speculative. In late 2025, Pakistan’s then chairman of the joint chiefs of staff committee, General Sahir Shamshad Mirza, travelled to Dhaka and met Yunus himself, alongside Bangladesh’s National Security Adviser and Pakistan’s High Commissioner. The conversation covered trade, investment and defence cooperation — the ordinary vocabulary of two states rebuilding a relationship from scratch, rather than the language of grievance. It was a meeting that would have been unthinkable a few years earlier, and it happened anyway, because both capitals decided the 1971 war did not have to be the last word in their relationship forever. Then came logistics. Direct shipping and air links between Karachi and Dhaka resumed in early 2026, ending a 14-year halt that had forced goods and people through expensive, indirect routes. Pakistan has formally proposed that Karachi Port serve as a transit point for Bangladesh-China trade, a potentially useful option for a country seeking to reduce its dependence on any single land or sea corridor. None of this required Bangladesh to sever a single tie with India. It simply gave Dhaka a second door out of the room. And in February 2026, Bangladeshi voters delivered a parliamentary victory to the Bangladesh Nationalist Party, closing the interim chapter and installing an elected government that has, so far, continued rather than reversed the diversification Yunus began. That is not the profile of a policy imposed from outside. That is the profile of a policy that has, at minimum, enough domestic support to survive an actual election. Every serious foreign policy tradition, from Bandung to the modern language of “multi-alignment” used in New Delhi’s own strategic circles, treats diversification as a sign of maturity, not disloyalty. Vietnam balances China and the United States. India itself buys Russian oil and American weapons in the same year. Bangladesh doing the equivalent — keeping Indian trade ties alive while opening channels to Pakistan, China and Turkey — is not radical. It is simply Bangladesh graduating into the same club. For Pakistan, the opportunity here is real, but it demands restraint, not triumphalism. The goal cannot be to replace India in Bangladesh’s strategic imagination; it should be to build something narrower and more durable: a working relationship based on trade volume, defence-industry cooperation and a shared voice in forums such as the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, rather than on nostalgia or shared grievance against a common rival. Pakistan has spent years quietly inviting Bangladeshi officials to training programmes and institutional exchanges that went largely unanswered during the Awami League years. That patience is now paying a modest, overdue dividend. Islamabad should bank it soberly. Critics inside and outside Bangladesh warn that leaning towards Islamabad, and towards Beijing’s defence and technology ecosystem by extension, could reopen the wounds of the Liberation War or drag Dhaka into someone else’s rivalry with New Delhi. These are not frivolous concerns, and Pakistan does itself no favours by dismissing them. The honest answer is that friendship carries risks in every direction. Over-dependence on India carried its own risks for decades, ones Bangladeshis lived with quietly until 2024 made the cost visible. A Bangladesh that trades with everyone and is owned by no one is a healthier Bangladesh than one locked into a single patron, whichever capital that patron sits in. Strip away the geopolitics-as-scoreboard framing that dominates so much commentary on this shift, and what is left is a simpler story: a country that spent five decades as someone else’s periphery is trying, however imperfectly, to become the subject of its own foreign policy rather than an object of someone else’s. Pakistan is one of several doors that opened when the old order in Dhaka fell. Turkey is another. China is a third. The United States and the wider West remain very much in the picture too. Bangladesh’s voters, not Islamabad or Beijing, will ultimately decide how far this recalibration goes through the ballot box, as they already did in February 2026. That is exactly how it should work. A more autonomous Bangladesh, comfortable enough in its own sovereignty to talk to Pakistan without treating the conversation as treason, is not a loss for the region. It is what a genuinely post-colonial South Asia was always supposed to look like.
