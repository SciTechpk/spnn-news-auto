Benchmark Brent crude oil futures rose past $100 a barrel on Wednesday, hitting a more than six-week high and breaching the symbolic threshold for the first time since July 24 as intensifying conflict in the Middle East heightened concerns about oil flows from the region. Brent crude futures were up $2.01, or 2.05 per cent, at $99.93 a barrel by 01:02pm PKT, after earlier touching $100.19, while US West Texas Intermediate crude was up $1.49, or 1.60pc, at $94.52 a barrel. Brent crude prices have risen by a quarter since early last month as hopes fade for a permanent resolution to the six-month-old US-Iran conflict. Since the Iran war began on February 28, Brent has surged as high as $126.41 a barrel, a peak reached on April 30. This week, attacks by Houthis on Saudi energy facilities set oil installations ablaze, threatening a significant expansion of the conflict. The attacks also threaten crude shipments via the Red Sea, which has been a key alternative route to the crucial Strait of Hormuz, where oil flows have been severely curtailed since the February 28 start of the Iran war. Supply risks mount “Market participants appear to be pricing in a more prolonged conflict in the Middle East as well as the risk that the latest escalation in military strikes disrupts oil flows from the Middle East,” said Hamad Hussain, senior climate and commodities economist at Capital Economics. “The key risk is whether the recent attacks on oil tankers lead to fewer ship-to-ship transfers taking place in the Gulf of Oman, which have so far played a key role in providing oil to global markets and keeping a lid on prices.” A growing number of banks, including Goldman Sachs, Bank of America and HSBC, have raised their crude price forecasts in recent days. Explained: Why is everyone so mad about Karachi province?

Talk of Pakistan’s territorial reconfiguration is back in circulation again with mention of the division of Sindh producing the most bile. It was all started by the interior minister who told businessmen in Islamabad last month that he mourned the collapse of Pakistan’s “outdated governance system”. Urgent reforms were needed, he argued, if Pakistan wanted to avoid blowback from “cockroaches” (Gen-Z agitating in India). Our salvation lay in the decentralization of governance which could be achieved by creating new administrative units (read: provinces). People who make a case for new provinces argue that the existing ones are too large to manage and there is too much distance between the government and the people. Smaller units would be more efficiently governed and would thus bring the seat of power closer to the people. Some commentators took his remarks rather seriously and started to earnestly do the math, running numeric options for a new ‘right-sized’ and reconfigured Federation of Pakistan: 5, 8, 10, 12 provinces, depending on which flavour of administrative reform you liked. South Punjab, Greater Karachi, Seraiki province, Hazara province. And secretly everyone wondered if the minister’s rhetoric had wafted down from the rarefied exosphere of the supra-political elite. Three groups have made their preferences clear over the question. The Establishment seems to be in the mood for ‘right-sizing’ the state. Political parties have expressed mixed views. And nationalist parties, particularly from Sindh, have opposed it. Sindh has been the angriest. Its house of elected representatives passed an unequivocal resolution against any proposal to change the boundaries of the province. What explains this reaction and what is the history to this sentiment? Desh-making When Partition took place, Sindh had already operated as a fully-fledged province for a decade after it was separated from Bombay in 1936. And with independence in 1947, it regained the old feeling of distinct territorial existence which it had experienced before the arrival of the British in 1843. This rejuvenated consciousness tinged with pride of belonging to one of the oldest civilizations in the world, the Indus Valley, made Sindhis particularly sensitive to their restored ethno-territorial identity. That is why today you cannot mention a ‘Karachi province’ without picking the scab off a very old wound. Partition caused the first injury in 1947 when it shook the foundations of Sindh’s demography and power structure. In a blink of an eye, its middle class mainly made up of Hindus emptied out as they fled to India and were replaced by Muhajirs coming from India. And then the federal government decided to take Karachi as the country’s capital, strip it of its provincial capital status and name Hyderabad instead. This was the catalyst for Sindhi nationalism, which went on to challenge the new State’s national project by insisting on ethnic identity and territoriality. Meanwhile, the second injury to the identity of Sindh came in the shape of One Unit (1955). With the stroke of a pen, the federal government created the two provinces of East and West Pakistan to establish what was at best an artificial parity between the two wings of a country. The creation of a mega province in West Pakistan was a disaster as it gave rise to resistance in the shape of ethnic nationalisms in the three minority provinces of Pakistan. Suppression of the sub-national identity of language as a primordial loyalty of people led to a fully-fledged nationalist movement in East Pakistan. Not surprisingly, the National Awami Party as a representative of Pakhtun and Baloch nationalisms and the Pakistan Peoples Party (PPP) as a representative of Sindhi nationalism formed governments in these provinces after the 1970 elections (and creation of Bangladesh in 1971). The message was clear. You tinker with ethnolinguistic identity, you give rise to a tsunami of mass mobilization. Spearheading this agitation were the famous ‘sons-of-the-soil’ such as G. M. Syed, Ibrahim Joyo, Shaikh Ayaz and Rasul Baksh Paleejo. Finally, after twenty years of mass resistance, General Yayha was obliged to undo the decision and make Karachi the capital of Sindh back again in 1970, and bring an end to the One Unit. The late 1970s onwards, Sindh has stood out from the other provinces because it has had unique competing nationalist movements. In fact, Sindh remains the most ethnically polarized province of Pakistan, making the question of territorial reorganization all the more difficult to grapple with. Muhajirs have strongly reacted to policies such as the 1972 declaration of Sindhi as an official language, a 60:40 quota for civil service and educational institute admissions favouring rural Sindh and the nationalisation of industries and universities. Thus the PPP and Sindhi nationalist groups sit at one end of the opinion spectrum and the Muttahida Qaumi Movement (MQM) at the other. Each player’s position Massive ethnic outbidding between the PPP, the MQM and the Sindhi nationalists is taking place with the latter including offshoots of G. M. Syed’s Jeay Sindh Mahaz. While these parties have not dominated at the ballot box, they represent the spirit of Sindhi nationalist consciousness and act as an important pressure group on the PPP as a frontline actor in the domain of governance. The PPP has been in power in Sindh since 2008. It is a coalition partner in the ruling dispensation in Islamabad. Since its inception, it has been able to connect Sindhis to the state through its electoral success. It has been able to present Sindh’s grievances at federal platforms such as the Council of Common Interests and National Finance Commission Awards to split funds among the provinces. It oils the security mechanism of the interests of Sindh within and beyond the provincial boundaries. The Sindhi nationalist parties have, however, focused on identity construction through grassroots politics. They are weak electoral entities but their influence on shaping Sindh’s imaginary is strong. For instance, their opposition to the 2012 Local Government Act that introduced a separate local bodies system in Karachi compelled the PPP government to re-think its policy. In early 2025, a controversy erupted with the proposed construction of canals in south Punjab that would have diverted water away from Sindh. Under mass public pressure, the PPP government was obliged to listen to the nationalist parties. Meanwhile, the MQM has learnt that it can never be in a dominant position in Sindh due to its demographic limitations at one-fourth of the population. Since 1988, it has been a coalition partner of several governments and it has generally looked towards Islamabad for support. It has been unhappy with the empowerment of provincial governments through the 18th Amendment because this means more power in the hands of the provincial majority Sindhis at the cost of the largest minority of Muhajirs. These misgivings aside, however, the promise of devolution remains unfulfilled almost a decade and half after the passage of the amendment. Provincial governments have avoided transferring administrative and fiscal powers to local governments. The limited powers of local governments have grossly alienated the MQM. On several occasions, this party has demanded a federal takeover of Karachi in the name of good governance and efficient service delivery. How could it be done legally? Article 239 (4) of the Constitution says any change in the territorial make-up of a province needs the approval of two-thirds of a provincial assembly as well as the National Assembly and the Senate. In today’s divided political landscape, it seems impossible. This is why a referendum has been mentioned as a way to acquire ‘popular’ consent for smaller provinces. The history of referendums and their like conducted in Pakistan by the military governments of Ayub, Zia and Musharraf does not, however, inspire much confidence especially as they were rigged. What people are really scared of The proposed division of Sindh has galvanized shades of public opinion. The nationalist parties opted for street mobilization. The PPP led the passage of a resolution in the Sindh Assembly. It seems that the creation of new provinces will have the most catastrophic consequences for Sindh. One frightening scenario is a population transfer that invokes the memory of 1947. Since Muhajirs are concentrated in urban centers, they might decide to migrate to a Muhajir-majority province (if created). This would lead to cross-migration across the boundaries steeped in blood. Indeed, it would give rise to a storm of discontent in all parts of Sindhi society, beyond the Sindhis and Muhajirs. Is the State ready to handle such a storm? Bangladesh, which once came close to eliminating measles, is facing its worst outbreak on record, with 999 suspected and confirmed measles-related deaths reported since March as hospitals struggle with overcrowding, shortages and a continuing influx of sick children. The prolonged outbreak, despite a nationwide emergency vaccination campaign launched in April, follows disruption to routine immunisation in 2024 and 2025, experts said, when the country was going through political turmoil after a deadly uprising against then-prime minister Sheikh Hasina. Bangladesh is currently experiencing the biggest measles outbreak in the world, according to a list on the United States Centres for Disease Control and Prevention website. Health officials and experts said missed vaccinations and vaccine supply problems as a result of the political upheaval had left children vulnerable, particularly infants under nine months who are too young to get routine vaccinations, helping fuel transmission across age groups. Since the outbreak emerged in March, Bangladesh has recorded more than 166,000 suspected measles cases, including 19,835 laboratory-confirmed infections, according to the health ministry data. The outbreak has been linked to 999 deaths, including 100 confirmed measles fatalities, while more than 146,000 suspected patients have been hospitalised. Immunity gaps Experts blamed the outbreak on weak programme oversight, insufficient monitoring and procurement policy changes under the interim government that replaced Hasina, disrupting vaccine availability and leaving many children unprotected against the highly infectious disease. Health Minister Sardar Md. Sakhawat Husain told parliament on Monday the procurement changes triggered a vaccine shortage while the 2024 postponement of a vaccination programme and the cancellation of a nationwide measles-rubella campaign the following year left more children vulnerable to infection. The World Health Organisation (WHO), Unicef and other health agencies have also pointed to the disruption to immunisation schedules. Under-fives accounted for about 80 per cent of cases during the early stages of the disease’s spread.
