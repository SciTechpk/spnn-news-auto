Emergency, governor's rule imposed in KP as president approves cabinet decision

Key developments: Emergency imposed under Article 232, governor’s rule under Article 234 Cabinet recommended move due to KP govt’s ‘deliberate disengagement from the fight against terrorism’ Technocratic council formed; Fakhr Alam appointed as KP chief secretary PTI’s Gohar condemns decision as undemocratic, says peace cannot be established by weakening provincial autonomy Governor Kundi calls for unity for ‘new chapter’ in KP’s development ISLAMABAD: The federal government on Sunday imposed an emergency and governor’s rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa following the president’s approval of the recommendations by the federal cabinet. The measures have been approved under Articles 232 and 234 of the Constitution, the President House said in a statement. The two provisions pertain to the proclamation of emergency and governor’s rule. Following the imposition, internet disruptions were being reported from parts of Peshawar, with Dawn unable to contact its correspondents there. According to sources in the telecom sector, mobile network and data services have been slowed or shut down in selected areas of some cities. However, there is no province-wide shutdown of mobile data or internet. The federal government also authorised the deployment of Pakistan Army troops and assets in aid of civil power in KP. On the ground, shipping containers were placed around the city of Peshawar to block several roads, with heavy police deployment. The decision to impose the emergency provisions came during an emergency meeting of the federal cabinet chaired by Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif, where he was briefed on the security situation in KP. A statement issued by the Prime Minister’s Office (PMO) said cabinet members reviewed the situation and took note of the “provincial government’s deliberate disengagement from the fight against terrorism”. The members proposed, given the “gravity” of the situation, that the PM advise the president to exercise “his constitutional authority and impose an emergency in the province under Articles 232 and 234 of the Constitution”. The emergency imposition comes a day after PM Shehbaz met President Zardari and discussed the country’s overall security situation. That meeting came amid heightened tensions between the PML-N-led federal government and the KP government led by the PTI — which is in opposition in the Centre. They have been at odds over various issues, particularly on the counterterrorism policy, which was one of the main reasons behind the breakdown of their Oct 3 talks. The PTI has said it was being left out of security decisions concerning KP, which the Centre has denied. It also calls for reviewing the counterterrorism policy and opposes military operations. On the other hand, the Centre has been criticising the KP government’s efforts to combat terrorism in the province and objects to its stance on dialogue with the Afghan Taliban. It may be recalled that in July 2024, when Afridi’s predecessor Ali Amin Gandapur was the chief minister, the KP Assembly passed a resolution urging the federal government to consult with it and the provincial government before launching a new military operation. The resolution, passed by a majority vote, cautioned that proceeding without provincial consent could hinder the offensive’s success and result in adverse consequences, for which the federal government would be responsible. In December that year, the assembly voted unanimously against a potential military operation in parts of Khyber and Bannu districts. About a year later, after Afridi became the KP CM, the provincial assembly convened a grand jirga with Governor Faisal Karim Kundi and other parties in attendance. The moot unanimously demanded that the Centre prioritise negotiations with Afghanistan and consult the KP government over Afghan policy. Constitutional provisions Emergency Article 232 of the Constitution provides for the proclamation of emergency in a province. It says, “If the president is satisfied that a grave emergency exists in which the security of Pakistan, or any part thereof, is threatened by war or external aggression, or by internal disturbance beyond the power of a provincial government to control, he may issue a Proclamation of Emergency.” However, invoking this article “due to internal disturbances beyond the powers of a provincial government to control” requires a resolution from the provincial assembly of that province. In case the president “acts on his own” — which is not currently the case — the Proclamation of Emergency is to be placed before both houses of Parliament for approval by each house within 10 days. According to this provision, parliament “shall have power to make laws for a province, or any part thereof, with respect to any matter not enumerated in the Federal Legislative List” during the time of the proclamation of an emergency. Nothing in this Article shall restrict the power of a provincial assembly to make any law which the Constitution empowers it to make, but if any provision of a provincial law is repugnant to any provision of an Act by Parliament, the latter would prevail and the provincial law would be void as long as the parliamentary Act is in effect. Governor’s rule On the other hand, Article 234 deals with the imposition of the governor’s rule “in case of failure of constitutional machinery in a province”. For imposition of the governor’s rule, the president simply requires a request from the provincial governor, it says. “A proclamation issued under this Article shall be laid before a joint sitting and shall cease to be in force at the expiration of two months, unless before the expiration of that period it has been approved by resolution of the joint sitting and may by like resolution be extended for a further period not exceeding two months at a time,” the Constitution reads. However, it adds that no such proclamation shall in any case remain in force for more than six months. Article 233, which provides powers to the president to suspend certain fundamental rights during the emergency period, was not mentioned in the summary signed by President Zardari. Riyadh airport hit by fresh Houthi strike day after deadly attack

A fresh Houthi strike hit Riyadh’s international airport on Sunday, a day after another attack claimed by the Yemeni group killed at least a dozen people and forced authorities to suspend operations at the facility. Saturday’s attack, the deadliest on Saudi soil in more than a decade, was a sharp escalation in the Yemen war, where Riyadh backs the internationally recognised government against the Houthis. The group on Sunday claimed multiple attacks over the last 24 hours, including on the airport, and threatened to keep targeting the kingdom’s transit hubs, calling them “a theatre of operations for our forces”. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said the group “carried out four qualitative operations in the past 24 hours using ballistic and cruise missiles and drones”. Saree said they targeted the Riyadh airport multiple times, as well as the Dammam airport and two facilities belonging to the Saudi state oil company Aramco. Hours later, the Saudi coalition fighting the group reported that a projectile struck King Khalid International Airport, sparking a fire. Air defences also intercepted a missile nearby, it added. The civil aviation authority said the attack caused an unspecified number of injuries, and that a damage assessment was underway. AFP journalists and locals reported hearing the sound of multiple blasts in the Saudi capital on Sunday. “The birds went into a frenzy after the sound. The whole house shook,” said one resident of the Al-Aarid neighbourhood in the north of the city. ‘Full-fledged war crime’ The Houthis have stepped up their attacks on the Riyadh airport — where three people were also killed on Thursday — as well as on other key targets in the world’s top crude oil exporter. And although Saudi Arabia has poured billions into its military, Houthi attacks continue to slip through. “Saudi air defences have been able to intercept some Houthi missile and drone attacks, but Saudi Arabia is so large that it’s simply impossible for them to consistently defend themselves against Houthi attacks,” said Thomas Juneau, security analyst at Ottawa University. The anti-Houthi coalition called Saturday’s attack a “full-fledged war crime” and vowed to “respond decisively”. Houthi media reported two Saudi strikes in Yemen’s Houthi-controlled Sanaa governorate on Sunday. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, whose country does not have diplomatic relations with Saudi Arabia, also denounced the “horrific terrorist attack”. Four Saudi citizens, two Bangladeshis, one American, one Egyptian, one Jordanian, one Syrian, one Palestinian and one Sudanese were killed, the civil aviation authority said, with 309 wounded. The Indian embassy in Saudi Arabia also said one of its citizens died on Sunday after being wounded the day before. A Riyadh-based Western diplomat told AFP the official number of injured may not give a full picture, as it did not appear to include those with minor wounds. Governor’s rule and emergency imposed in KP — here’s what it means

The federal government imposed an emergency and governor’s rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa amid growing tensions with the PTI-led provincial government, including disagreements over counterterrorism policy amid a deteriorating security situation in the province. The decision was taken under Articles 232 and 234 of the Constitution of Pakistan. Here is what it means. Governor’s rule Article 234 of the Constitution of Pakistan stipulates that, if the president believes that a provincial government cannot function in accordance with the Constitution, the federal government can assume control of the province. This can be done either directly or by assigning the relevant functions to the provincial governor — hence the term governor’s rule. Under this arrangement, the powers of the provincial assembly can be exercised by Parliament. However, neither the president nor the governor can assume the powers of the high court or suspend its operations. Moreover, governor’s rule can be imposed for an initial period of two months, with a joint sitting of Parliament able to approve an extension of up to two months at a time. In total, governor’s rule can remain in place for a maximum of six months. While a province is under governor’s rule, its government or the chief minister cannot exercise their powers, but “I don’t see how it brings the government to an end”, said lawyer Mirza Moiz Baig. He added, “The government would come back into existence upon the end of the emergency”. “Once the proclamation expires, the Chief Minister and his Cabinet are restored,” lawyer Rida Hosain told Dawn. State of emergency Meanwhile, under Article 232, which pertains to the proclamation of an emergency, the provincial assembly continues to function as usual. However, if a provincial law conflicts with a federal law enacted by Parliament, the latter takes precedence. Lawyer Basil Nabi Malik, speaking to Dawn, explained that the chief minister would remain in office during a state of emergency. However, the federal government could assume certain functions of the provincial government. An emergency also allows the federal government to make provisions it considers necessary and suspend certain constitutional provisions relating to provincial bodies. However, as with governor’s rule, the federal government cannot assume the powers of a high court. It also empowers Parliament to confer powers and impose duties on federal government officers in the province. It further stipulates that laws passed by Parliament solely on account of the emergency will cease to have effect six months after the emergency ends. The proclamation of emergency lapses after two months unless it is approved by a resolution of a joint sitting of Parliament.

Timeline: A concise history of emergency rule in Pakistan

The federal government on Sunday imposed an emergency and governor’s rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa following the president’s approval of the recommendations by the federal cabinet. The measures have been approved under Articles 232 and 234 of the Constitution, the President House said in a statement. The two provisions pertain to the proclamation of emergency and governor’s rule. Article 232 of the Constitution deals with “proclamation of emergency on account of war and internal disturbance”, while Article 234 of the Constitution, more commonly known as “governor’s rule”, empowers the federal government to take control of a province in case of “failure of constitutional machinery”. This isn’t the first time the Centre has used its powers to declare emergency in a province or even send the respective provincial government packing. Here’s a brief history of emergency rule in Pakistan. January 1949 to April 1951 The first governor’s rule was imposed within a short span of the country’s establishment, in the heartland of Punjab. In January 1949, then Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan visited Lahore, where Chief Minister Nawab Mamdot told him that he had earned the confidence of a majority of the MPAs in the provincial assembly. The development had come about due to a disagreement between Mamdot and former CM Mumtaz Daulatana. However, Liaquat Ali Khan noted factual discrepancies in the document shown by Mamdot. He dismissed the Mamdot government, dissolved the assembly, and imposed governor’s rule in the province. December 1951 to May 1953 — Sindh A governor’s rule of 1 year and 144 days lasted in Sindh after Chief Minister Muhammad Ayub Khuhro was dismissed amid allegations of corruption and maladministration. Mian Aminuddin administered the province directly under Governor’s rule, which ended following the 1953 provincial elections, after which Pirzada Abdus Sattar of the Pakistan Muslim League took over. February-April, 1973 — Balochistan On February 14, 1973, Prime Minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto dismissed the Balochistan provincial government led by Chief Minister Sardar Attaullah Mengal and removed Governor Mir Ghaus Bakhsh Bizenjo, citing alleged involvement in unrest and the discovery of weapons at the Iraqi embassy in Islamabad. The next day, Nawab Akbar Khan Bugti was appointed as the new Governor of Balochistan, and direct central/governor’s rule was enforced. The federal government also subsequently deployed the Pakistan Army under General Tikka Khan to conduct operations in the mountainous and tribal regions, particularly targeting areas like Lasbela and Marri. The governor’s rule ended in late April the same year, paving the way for Jam Ghulam Qadir Khan to form a new provincial ministry. February-May 1975 — Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Governor’s rule was imposed in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (then known as the North-West Frontier Province) from February 16, 1975, to May 3, 1975, for a total duration of 76 days under Article 232 of the 1973 Constitution due to an emergency. Officials had cited external disruptions beyond local control. At the time, the federal government under PM Bhutto had directed the governor to assume all government powers, ending the cabinet of Chief Minister Sardar Inayatullah Khan Gandapur. The province was overseen by Major General (R) Syed Ghawas until direct rule concluded on May 3, 1975, with Nasrullah Khan Khattak assuming the office of Chief Minister. June-August 1988 — Sindh The second instance of a governor’s rule in Sindh’s history lasted 68 days, from June 24, 1988, to August 31, 1988. The caretaker government of Chief Minister Akhtar Ali Ghulam Qazi of the Islami Jamhoori Ittehad was dismissed. Qazi was replaced by Pakistan Army’s four-star general Rahimuddin Khan as governor. This development came closely after President General Zia-ul-Haq’s nationwide dissolution of Prime Minister Mohammad Khan Junejo’s federal government and all provincial assemblies on May 29, 1988. February-April 1994 — Khyber Pakhtunkhwa One of the country’s most controversial political episodes, the governor’s rule imposed in KP from February 25, 1994 to April 24, 1994 came after Prime Minister Benazir Bhutto suspended the PML-N ministry in the province, led by Sabir Shah. Executive authority was temporarily transferred directly to the provincial Governor, Major General (Retd) Khurshid Ali Khan. When the governor’s rule was lifted, Aftab Ahmad Khan Sherpao of the PPP was sworn in as the new chief minister of the province February-March 2009 — Punjab President Asif Ali Zardari imposed governor’s rule for two months, dismissing PML-N’s government after the Supreme Court disqualified Nawaz Sharif and Shehbaz Sharif. Zardari appointed Salman Taseer as the governor of Punjab. The provincial assembly was not suspended, but then-CM Shehbaz and his cabinet members were barred from holding office. The governor’s rule was lifted after a month. January-March 2013 — Balochistan PPP’s Prime Minister Raja Pervez Ashraf dismissed Balochistan’s provincial government, yielding to nationwide protests. His announcement came as thousands of Hazara Shias staged a sit-in in freezing cold on Alamdar Road for three days, along with the bodies of dozens of people killed in twin bombings in Quetta The carnage had resulted in over 90 fatalities. The provincial government of Chief Minister Nawab Aslam Raisani was sacked, and Governor Nawab Zulfiqar Ali Magsi took charge as the chief executive of the province. Nationwide emergencies Nationwide emergencies have been imposed several times as well, including martial laws, with the first one just a few years after Pakistan’s creation, in 1954. The longest periods in the country’s history when a nationwide dismissal of parliament persisted were in the martial laws of 1977–1985 under General Zia-ul-Haq, and 1999–2007 under General Pervez Musharraf.

Part 10 of the Constitution covers emergency provisions and comprises six articles — Article 232 to Article 237. Three of these articles are of prime importance. It is usually a province which bears the brunt of an emergency proclamation. This is why these provisions have assumed importance during this time of confrontation between the federal government and the Punjab provincial government. Article 232 deals with the emergency, which is pronounced in two distinct situations. One, war or external aggression and, two, internal disturbances beyond the power of a province to control. If an emergency is to be pronounced because of internal security conditions of a province, then the provincial assembly concerned is required to pass a resolution. The president can also proclaim an emergency, but in this case the proclamation would need to be placed before parliament, whose two Houses should approve it within 10 days. During the emergency under this article, the provincial assembly can continue to work, but a law passed by parliament for the province will have precedence over provincial laws. The executive authority of the federal government extends to the province which will be exercised either by the president or the provincial governor. The powers of the courts will, however, remain unchanged during the emergency. The proclamation of an emergency needs to be placed before a joint sitting of parliament within 30 days of the proclamation. Its validity is for two months, unless the joint sitting approves an extension through a resolution. If the joint sitting disapproves the proclamation, it will cease to be in force. An important aspect of the emergency under this article is that parliament, during the emergency, may extend the term of the National Assembly for a period of up to one year, but this extra term cannot extend beyond six months after the proclamation ceases to be in force. Article 233 explains that the president will have the power to suspend certain fundamental rights under the emergency proclamation. Article 234 deals with the power to impose emergency in case of failure of the constitutional machinery in a province. This probably is the article which provinces should be most concerned about. The imposition of this emergency may happen after the president receives a report from the provincial governor and the president is satisfied that a situation has arisen in which the provincial government cannot carry out its duties out in accordance with the Constitution. In case the two Houses of parliament pass a resolution separately demanding that such an emergency be imposed, the president is bound to proclaim the emergency. The powers of the courts, however, will remain intact even during the emergency. A proclamation issued under Article 234 has to be laid before a joint sitting of parliament and is valid only for two months, unless the joint sitting extends the validity for a further period of two months. The maximum validity of such a proclamation is six months. The proclamation under this article may declare that the powers of the provincial assembly are exercisable by or under the authority of parliament. The joint sitting of parliament can also confer power on the president to make laws for the provincial assembly. The president, when parliament is not in session, may also authorise expenditure from the provincial consolidated fund to be later sanctioned by a joint sitting of parliament. Article 235 deals with proclamation of a financial emergency by the president if he is satisfied after consultation with the governor or governors that a situation has arisen whereby economic life, financial stability or the credit of Pakistan or any part of it is threatened. While such proclamation of emergency is in force, the executive authority of the federation extends to giving directions to any province to observe such principles of financial propriety as may be specified in the directions. These directions may include reduction of salaries and allowances of all or any class of persons serving in connection with the affairs of a province or the federation. This proclamation is also required to be laid before a joint sitting of parliament and may be extended up to six months as in the case of proclamation under Article 234.
