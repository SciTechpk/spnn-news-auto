At ECO moot, Dar calls for translating 'collective commitments into concrete action'

Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar closed out the 30th meeting of the Economic Cooperation Organisation (ECO) Council of Ministers (COM) in Islamabad on Monday with a call for countries to translate their commitments into action. Pakistan, which holds the ECO chairmanship for 2026-27, hosted the meeting under the theme “Reviving Regionalism for a Resilient ECO”. The deputy premier, who chaired the meeting, expressed his “sincere appreciation to all delegations” for their constructive engagement during the meeting. “The deliberations today have reaffirmed our shared commitment to regional economic cooperation and integration,” Dar said. He termed the adoption of the Strategic Goals of Economic Cooperation 2035 an “important milestone in charting the future course of our organisation over the next decade”, saying that it provided a shared framework to advance regional priorities alongside the Islamabad Communiqué. “Our task now is to translate our collective commitments into concrete action,” he added. “This will require clear priorities, realistic timelines, and effective follow-up mechanisms.” He highlighted regional cooperation as a central pillar of Pakistan’s foreign policy and noted that the country had consistently advocated a “stronger, more effective and responsive” ECO that harnessed “geographical proximity, historical bonds and economic complementarities”. Dar underscored Pakistan’s commitment to advancing the ECO agenda as the chair of the organisation and to working with all institutions and member states to strengthen regional connectivity, facilitate intra-regional trade and promote mutually beneficial economic cooperation. He looked forward to welcoming the countries back for the 31st COM meeting in 2027, saying, “Let us build on the momentum generated here so that our next meeting can take stock of tangible progress in advancing our shared priorities.” In addition, he wished Iran success in hosting the upcoming 18th ECO summit on Dec 3-4. The deputy premier thanked all member states, as well as the secretary general and his team, ECO permanent representatives, and senior officials for their support. Later in the day, Dar held a media briefing on the meeting alongside ECO Secretary General Asad Majeed Khan and Information Minister Attaullah Tarar. Joint communiqué The COM meeting concluded with the adoption of two documents, including the Strategic Goals of Economic Cooperation 2035 and the Islamabad Communiqué. Pakistan’s mediation efforts hailed In the Islamabad Communiqué, ECO member states expressed concern over the “challenging geopolitical environment in the region, emphasising the importance of restraint, dialogue and diplomatic engagement, and recognising the important role” of Iran as a founding member of ECO. They expressed support for the mediation efforts undertaken by Pakistan and Qatar, as well as for the recent initiative put forward by Iran “aimed at achieving a dignified resolution of the conflict and ensuring the complete and permanent termination of the current war”. The ECO states commended Pakistan’s “consistent and constructive diplomatic support for ECO Member States and its mediation efforts towards de-escalation during the recent Iran-United States crisis”. They reaffirmed the importance of dialogue, diplomacy, respect for international law and promoting mutual trust. stability and the peaceful resolution of disputes, and calling for continued cooperation within the ECO framework towards lasting peace and stability in the region. The communiqué also highly appreciated Qatar’s “important role as a trusted facilitator of international diplomacy, most notably demonstrated by its mediation efforts at the 81st United Nations General Assembly”. The members commended ECO for “demonstrating that regionalism can remain effective and operational even under conditions of acute geopolitical stress”. The communiqué expressed concern over the humanitarian situation in Gaza after flooding on Nov 6 as winter begins. It urges priority delivery of food, medicine, fuel, heating equipment, tents, shelters, blankets and winter clothing. It also called for an immediate end to “policies of forced displacement and the deliberate deprivation of food” and full implementation of the October 2025 Gaza ceasefire agreement. The organisation rejected “unilateral coercive measures, sanctions and economic pressures and their extraterritorial economic, technological, financial or trade impacts”. It said they are contrary to the UN Charter and international law, adversely affect international peace and security, and undermine the provision of supply chains for food, pharmaceutical, energy and humanitarian assistance. The joint statement also mentioned Afghanistan, a member of the ECO, though seemingly not present at the meeting. ECO acknowledged the need for “continued consultation with member states to address the issues of Afghanistan’s engagement with the organisation in relevant areas, with a view to achieving the country’s full and effective participation once circumstances permit”. They stressed the need for “achieving meaningful progress on concerns of member states for fostering regional cooperation and laying the basis for Afghanistan’s participation in any ECO activities and processes”. Economy and trade The meeting expressed support for various documents adopted in the past, including ECO Vision 2025 and the 2021 Ashgabat Consensus for Action. They also welcomed various UN resolutions and initiatives. Members were urged to speed up amendment and implementation of the ECO Trade Agreement (ECOTA), with non-parties to the pact invited to consultations. The communique called for timely implementation of Turkmenistan’s feasibility study on the establishment of joint trade zones within the ECO region. They pointed to the Turkmenistan–Uzbekistan zone as a model and to a proposed trade market at the Altyn Asyr–Incheh Boroun border crossing between Iran and Turkmenistan. It urged joint measures on foreign direct investment, free economic zones, joint manufacturing, e-commerce and digitised customs “single window” systems. The ECO further stressed “expeditious conclusion” of the draft ECO Trade Facilitation Agreement and tasked the Joint Working Group with intensifying its technical negotiations toward the pact’s early finalisation. Pakistan has offered to host the 4th JWG meeting in the third quarter of 2026. The ministers called for more diversified financing, including through the ECO Trade and Development Bank (ECOTDB), whose membership they want expanded. Members were urged to fully implement the ECO Transit Transport Framework Agreement to “unlock the region’s full potential and enhance regional connectivity, trade, and economic development”. The ministers highlighted the ECO Sustainable and Resilient Transport Strategy 2026–2035 as a flagship framework. They also called for transport and transit corridors and better seaport access for landlocked members. The ECO members welcomed Shusha as ECO Tourism Capital 2026 and noted the schedule of future capitals: Lahore (2027), Mary (2028), Karakol (2029) and Aktau (2030). They also welcomed the operationalisation of the ECO Clean Energy Centre in Baku, as well as the “ECO Five-Year Roadmap on minerals cooperation for 2026–2030”, described as the first overarching framework for regional cooperation on minerals, including critical minerals. AI and technology The Islamabad Communiqué acknowledged UN actions on AI, including the first Global Dialogue on AI Governance in Geneva on July 6–7. It underlined the need to bridge the AI capacity gap and promote “safe, secure and trustworthy artificial intelligence systems in the non-military domain for sustainable development”. The statement also recognised the “transformative potential of emerging technologies”, such as AI, blockchain, quantum computing, 5G and robotics. It called for harnessing these technologies for regional prosperity, while noting the need to share experience on mitigating associated risks. Members were encouraged to cooperate on AI through exchanges of best practices, joint capacity-building and training under the ECO Science Foundation, aiming at bridging the “digital and artificial intelligence divide”. The ministers also noted the significance of the ECO Regional ICT Strategy 2026–2030, stressed the security of critical digital infrastructure, and called for cooperation among members in ICT security. They emphasised the “importance of ensuring equal, fair, transparent and non-discriminatory access, particularly for developing countries, to global markets for ICT security products, technologies and services, as a confidence-building measure”. The ECO members encouraged exploring cooperation on the peaceful uses of outer space, including satellite services, and space-based services in support of regional connectivity, disaster risk reduction and environmental monitoring. Lawyers submit affidavits detailing Nov 11 meeting with Imran, Bushra at Adiala jail

ISLAMABAD: Lawyers representing former prime minister Imran Khan and his wife, Bushra Bibi, have submitted affidavits to the Islamabad High Court (IHC), detailing their Nov 11 meeting with the incarcerated couple at Adiala jail. Meanwhile, Bushra has filed a fresh criminal miscellaneous application seeking the production of her complete medical record before the court and directions for her immediate transfer to a specialised hospital. The application, filed under Section 561-A of the Code of Criminal Procedure in Criminal Appeal No. 64 of 2025, seeks to place additional facts concerning Bushra’s deteriorating eyesight, alleged deficiencies in medical facilities and unhygienic conditions at Adiala jail on the judicial record. The application was diarised at the IHC on Nov 16, 2026, under diary number 22497/2026. It has been filed in connection with her pending appeal against her conviction in the £190 million Al-Qadir Trust case. Senior Advocate Sardar Muhammad Latif Khosa and Barrister Salman Safdar, who represent Imran and Bushra, submitted separate affidavits recounting their approximately three-hour meeting with the incarcerated couple on Nov 11. According to the affidavits, the meeting was arranged pursuant to an order passed by the IHC on November 10, directing the jail authorities to facilitate a meeting between Imran, Bushra and their lawyers at Adiala jail. The lawyers stated that they met the couple from 4pm to 7pm. During the first hour, Imran conveyed his instructions regarding the pending legal proceedings and his medical condition, while the subsequent discussion focused on Bushra’s deteriorating eyesight and the medical complications she was facing. The affidavits stated that Bushra informed the lawyers that she had been diagnosed with a mature cataract and that another surgery was scheduled to be performed on or around November 15, 2026. She also removed her dark grey sunglasses and showed the condition of her eyes to both lawyers, informing them that, according to the attending doctors, oil needed to be extracted from her eye. The lawyers stated in their affidavits that Bushra complained of severely impaired vision in both eyes following her previous surgical procedure. She also told them that she could see only to a limited extent while wearing spectacles and was virtually unable to see without them. Even with spectacles, she could perceive the lawyers sitting in front of her merely as indistinct dark objects, while her field of vision remained restricted to objects directly in front of her. She was unable to properly see objects on either side and had to estimate the position of objects and obstacles while moving around. The affidavits further stated that the former first lady had been experiencing persistent eye pain for several days and was taking two tablets each of Panadol and Tonoflex daily for pain relief. She also complained that despite repeatedly informing the jail authorities about the pain, specialist doctors attended to her only after a delay of approximately seven to eight days. According to the affidavits, the attending doctors had cautioned her that any infection following the impending eye surgery could result in complete and permanent loss of her eyesight. The lawyers also brought to the court’s notice the alleged unhygienic conditions of the room in which Bushra is confined at Adiala jail. According to the affidavits, she informed them that although she was accommodated in a large hall-type room, its sanitary conditions were seriously deficient. She alleged that rats and mice frequently entered the room, which was also infested with cockroaches, centipedes and other insects. As many as seven small snakes had reportedly been found inside the room and were subsequently killed and removed by a jail employee identified as Sibtain. The affected portions of the room were later cemented, according to the affidavits. The superintendent of the jail, however, refuted the reports of snakes in the room in a statement on Monday. Saying that the jail administration had taken notice of the reports in the media and circulating online, he added that the administration “strongly refutes” the claim, terming it “baseless, fabricated and contrary to the facts”. “All necessary actions are taken to provide health, protection, cleanliness and other conveniences to prisoners in Central Jail Adiala, including women prisoners, in accordance with the jail manual and rules and regulations,” the statement added, including the spraying of germicides and insecticides for an environment free of insects and harmful animals. Bushra also complained of frequent insect bites and resultant skin rashes, expressing apprehension that the unhygienic environment could expose her to an infection following eye surgery. The affidavits further stated that the duty medical officer at Adiala jail was a gynaecologist rather than an ophthalmologist, raising concerns about the availability of specialised eye care at the prison. Bushra further informed the lawyers that she had experienced bleeding following her previous surgery, which she attributed to unhygienic living conditions, inadequate medical supervision and a lack of specialised medical expertise. The fresh application states that Bushra had previously approached the IHC through a criminal miscellaneous application, seeking directions for the production of her complete medical record and provision of appropriate medical treatment. However, the applicant contends that an earlier meeting with her lawyer on Nov 2, 2026, lasted only around 40 minutes after a gap of approximately 11 months, leaving her unable to explain the full extent of her medical complications. The application also refers to a report submitted by the Islamabad advocate general to the Supreme Court on Aug 17, 2026, regarding the medical treatment provided to Imran and Bushra. According to the application, medical records furnished by the Adiala jail superintendent had revealed that Bushra’s eye condition required urgent medical intervention. Consequently, she had twice been taken outside the prison for surgery and medical examination. The application further claimed that during the Nov 2 meeting, Bushra complained that she was unable to tolerate sunlight or artificial light and had difficulty opening her eyes and seeing clearly. She also expressed dissatisfaction with the medical treatment being provided at the jail and said she wanted to be examined and treated by doctors of her own choice. The application alleges that despite medical advice recommending major surgery, the jail authorities had not provided Bushra, her family or her legal team with the relevant medical notes, diagnostic reports or prescriptions. Instead, they had verbally informed them about the need for another major surgical procedure around Nov 15 and the risk of permanent loss of eyesight if it was not performed. In her latest criminal miscellaneous application, Bushra has requested the court to take on record the additional facts and circumstances disclosed during the Nov 11 meeting and consider them alongside the grounds raised in her earlier application. She has specifically sought directions for the respondents, including the state, Adiala jail superintendent, deputy superintendent and NAB chairman, to produce her complete medical record before the court. The applicant has also requested the court to ensure the timely provision of all necessary medical and surgical treatment and direct her immediate transfer to a specialised hospital for appropriate medical care. The application maintains that the additional facts concerning her deteriorating eyesight, impending surgery and conditions inside the jail warrant urgent judicial intervention to protect her health and prevent irreversible loss of vision.

I made Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad take the 10-minute park test

The ten-minute park test asks whether people can reach a public park or playground on foot from their homes. Its purpose is to identify neighbourhoods without everyday access to recreation and help direct investment towards them. In its population-based form, the score is the proportion of residents living within that walking distance. It tells us something that the amount of green on a satellite image cannot: whether public open space is within reach of the people who need it. The international examples are instructive. In the United States, Trust for Public Land reports that 100 per cent of San Francisco residents and 99 per cent of New Yorkers live within a ten-minute walk of a park. In Los Angeles, the figure is 62 per cent. These examples suggest that density need not prevent widespread access to parks. This approach prompted me to run an exploratory test for Lahore, Karachi and Islamabad, using OpenStreetMap streets, paths and mapped green spaces. This exercise measures the share of mapped street and path length within an 800-metre walking route of a counted park. (It does not yet measure the proportion of residents served, so its percentages cannot be compared directly with the American figures. Nor does it measure tree cover, shade or the quality of a park.) The results are 41 per cent for Lahore, 38 per cent for Karachi and 35 per cent for Islamabad. Within those totals, access to parks of at least ten acres accounts for just six, four and six per cent, respectively. This includes 4% within reach of a park of at least ten acres. The inset shows central Karachi in greater detail. The maps were re-rendered from the archived geographic data; these are not enlargements of the social-media images. These estimates use generous assumptions: spaces with unspecified access were included, entrances were inferred, and gates, opening hours, fees and crossing conditions were not comprehensively verified. Missing parks or paths can also depress the estimates. The boundaries cover Lahore district, Karachi’s seven districts and Islamabad Capital Territory, excluding Rawalpindi. Rural roads and hill paths affect the results. These are preliminary access maps, not a ranking of resident experience. Eight hundred metres can also take considerably longer than ten minutes for a child or someone with limited mobility. Lahore’s map shows how much its older urban fabric still contributes. The inner city and areas around the Walled City retain close relationships between neighbourhoods and gardens. Earlier planned suburbs, including Model Town and Gulberg, preserve pockets of this character. The larger-park access clusters around places such as Greater Iqbal Park, Bagh-i-Jinnah, Jilani Park and Model Town Park show the enduring value of setting aside substantial public space. But a major destination park cannot replace a neighbourhood playground. Karachi’s four per cent figure for large-park access makes that distinction especially relevant. Families need places children can use after school without requiring a journey across the city. Lahore’s outward expansion raises a question about the kind of city we are building. As farmland gives way to housing schemes and roads, longer distances and disconnected streets can make routine activities dependent on motorised travel. Preserving the city of gardens requires land-use decisions that put homes and public spaces within walking distance.Islamabad illustrates how road design can undermine that relationship. Doxiadis’s masterplan has its critics, but it recognised the importance of neighbourhood parks, playgrounds and pedestrian routes. For families living in small houses or flats, a public park provides space they cannot purchase within their own boundaries. The presence of greenery does not compensate for a road network that makes reaching it difficult. Islamabad’s Fatima Jinnah Park (F-9) should be an everyday resource for residents of the G sectors across Jinnah Avenue. In January 2026, reporting documented the difficulties faced by G-9 residents crossing the road while a pedestrian underpass remained unfinished. The report concerned a specific construction period, but the planning question is lasting: can a child, an older person or a wheelchair user reach a park entrance safely and directly? Roads that facilitate driving while imposing detours and danger on pedestrians create a social barrier. Public investment should not make Islamabad’s amenities easier to enjoy for those with cars while making them harder to reach for those without. Politicians, bureaucrats and planners too often assess the city from a moving vehicle. This is urban planning through the windshield: a signal-free road appears to be progress, while the loss of a safe pedestrian crossing goes unnoticed. There have been significant investments in a different direction. In my opinion, current Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif deserves credit for advancing modern mass transit through Lahore’s Metrobus, the Rawalpindi–Islamabad and Multan systems, and the Orange Line project. Chief Minister Maryam Nawaz’s expansion of Punjab’s public bus fleet, including electric buses, builds on that direction. Affordable, reliable public transport can be a powerful equaliser, widening access to employment, education and recreation. That investment needs to extend to the walk at either end of the journey. Bus stops and park entrances should connect to continuous footpaths and safe crossings. New development should reserve usable public space close to homes, while existing neighbourhoods need smaller parks and playgrounds alongside major destinations. Greater density around public transport can shorten distances, provided public-space provision grows with the population. Cities should now improve these preliminary maps with verified entrances, public-access conditions and population data, and use them to direct spending towards underserved neighbourhoods. Every road project should be assessed for its effect on walking access before construction begins. Symbol of neglect

NOTHING quite prepares you for the sensory overload you experience when you move from the pristine and polished Islamabad to the battered and potholed Karachi. To the discerning eye, Karachi looks like a low-resolution version of Islamabad, that too in sepia tones. The vibrancy of the “City of Lights” is mellowed by the haze of suspended smoke and dust, further muted by the drabness of its infrastructure. Its skyline, characterised by decrepit buildings woven through a not-so-intricate web of overhead wires, pales in comparison with Islamabad’s lush green, hilly landscape. The sounds of each city also stand in sharp contrast. While the latest and most luxurious private cars noiselessly cruise through Islamabad’s roads, Karachi’s streets are swarmed with motorbikes, rickshaws, buses, and trucks, each incessantly blaring its horn to force its way through traffic. Many private vehicles on the road bear visible signs of abuse inflicted by other heedless drivers. Both cities have their distinct olfactory signatures as well: while Islamabad exudes privilege and dewy freshness, Karachi reeks of injustice, neglect, and just pure garbage. The stench emanating from gutters, garbage sites, and at times, the sea is so overpowering that even the confines of an air-conditioned car fail to offer any respite from the pungency. Karachi’s disparity is unfortunate given its economic footprint. On the development front, as Islamabad witnesses the construction of new underpasses and bridges on a near-monthly basis, Karachi’s infrastructure crumbles under the weight of its burgeoning population, exacerbated by the governance conundrum it faces. The city remains in shambles, ranking 170th out of 173 cities on the EIU’s Global Liveability Index 2026. This disparity is particularly unfortunate given Karachi’s enormous economic footprint, not to mention its remarkable capacity to embrace people from diverse backgrounds. The city accounts for nearly a quarter of Pakistan’s GDP, compared to Islamabad’s estimated 1-2 per cent. Yet, despite being nearly four times larger in area and having more than 10 times Islamabad’s population, Karachi receives a smaller share of development allocations. Whatever resources are earmarked for the city are further fragmented between the Karachi Metropolitan Corporation (KMC) and provincial authorities, making coordinated urban development all the more difficult. The problem, however, extends far beyond the quantum of development spending. While several new development schemes have been announced for Karachi, those still enduring the (now) five-year-long nightmare of construction along University Road know all too well that such projects bring far greater disruption than promise. Competing political interests, overlapping institutional mandates, and the persistent power struggle between provincial and local authorities undermine the effective and timely implementation of development projects. Karachi is not simply a poorly developed city; it is an economically indispensable one whose governance arrangements systematically undermine its capacity to convert economic activity into better urban outcomes. The political economy underpinning the disparity between the two cities is hardly difficult to discern. Unlike Islamabad, where the Capital Development Authority provides a relatively centralised structure for urban planning and development, Karachi is governed through a maze of overlapping jurisdictions, with municipal authorities, cantonment boards, development authorities, and provincial departments each vying for greater control. Competing interests and distorted mandates make it difficult to establish who is ultimately responsible for the city’s development, leaving its residents to bear the cost of institutional dysfunction. Moreover, a weak municipal revenue base and limited capacity for own-source revenue mobilisation leave local governments heavily dependent on provincial transfers, further entrenching their fiscal dependence and weakening their capacity to deliver urban services. It is deeply disheartening to see a city once synonymous with opportunity, enterprise, and the promise of a better life increasingly become a symbol of neglect, deprivation, and urban decay. Karachi needs more than sporadic development schemes and infrastructure projects. It needs empowered local governments, clearly defined institutional responsibilities, and predictable fiscal transfers that allow its elected representatives to plan and deliver services in line with the city’s needs. For a city that contributes so much to the country’s economy, the least it deserves is a governance system capable of delivering the most basic urban amenities. Karachi deserves better. Taxpayers’ burden

IN challenging times, ordinary citizens and businesses may be called upon to shoulder higher tax burdens to meet the unavoidable costs of maintaining a sovereign state. But citizens should not be expected to bear the burden of costs that are largely avoidable. When debt servicing consumes 125 per cent of the FBR taxes retained by the federal government, 68.5pc of its total revenues, and nearly three times the defence budget, such an extraordinary burden demands closer scrutiny. Is our public debt being managed prudently and efficiently? In Pakistan, where a large proportion of the population comprises practising Muslims, many people prefer to keep their savings in bank accounts that pay no interest. As a result, nearly 40pc of all bank deposits are held in current accounts, on which banks pay no interest. This gives Pakistani banks access to an exceptionally cheap source of funds, which they invest in government securities at exorbitantly high returns. According to the State Bank of Pakistan, banks paid an average interest rate of just 5.53pc on their total deposits in September 2026, including current accounts that pay no interest. Yet, in October 2026, the government was borrowing through 12-month treasury bills at 12.79pc — a gross difference of 7.26 percentage points. Is our public debt being managed prudently and efficiently? A prudent borrower would certainly have sought to negotiate a much narrower margin, perhaps 1-2 percentage points. Yet our government continues to borrow at substantially higher rates, raising serious questions about the management of public debt. Ultimately, taxpayers are asked to bear the cost of such generosity. The burden falls on petrol and diesel consumers paying the Petroleum Development Levy, as well as investors paying effective income tax rates of 50-60pc. High returns on relatively low-risk government securities have encouraged banks to attract more low-cost deposits, particularly current accounts. By June 2026, these accounts held Rs15.3 trillion, nearly 40pc of total deposits, up from 37pc a year earlier. SBP confirms that the Rs3.67tn increase in deposits during the first half of 2026 came mainly from current accounts. While much of Pakistan’s productive economy has struggled, bank deposits have tripled from Rs13.35tn in December 2018 to nearly Rs41tn in September 2026. But where has this enormous pool of savings gone? Into new industries, businesses and jobs, or increasingly into high-yield government securities, earning banks substantial returns at taxpayers’ expense? According to SBP, commercial banks held Rs40.36tn in government securities in September 2026. Applying the prevailing gross interest-rate differential of 7.26 percentage points to these holdings gives an estimated annualised differential of approximately Rs2.93tn. If government borrowing rates were limited to 1-2 percentage points above banks’ average deposit cost, the estimated annualised gross savings could reach Rs2,100-2,500 billion, subject to debt maturities and repricing. Even part of these savings could ease the lives of millions of households and thousands of struggling entrepreneurs by reducing the Petroleum Development Levy, multiple taxes on energy bills, and high income tax rates on salaries and businesses. Such a policy would also reduce SBP’s annual interest earnings by approximately Rs118bn, which would be just a fraction of the overall savings. What needs to be done? The government should introduce a law that limits the interest paid to commercial banks on government securities to a reasonable 1-2 percentage points above what banks pay their depositors on average. The implementation process should ensure that monetary policy, government borrowing operations and financial stability are not disrupted. In order to bring transparency into debt management operations, the law should require the Ministry of Finance and SBP to publish quarterly reports showing how much the government borrows from banks and other investors, the interest it pays, and the cost of funds to banks. These reports should also disclose how much the government actually saves through lower borrowing costs. The potential annual savings of Rs2,100-2,500bn, once realised, should be passed on to taxpayers and businesses that have long borne the burden of excessive taxation, rather than being absorbed by increased government expenditure. The priority should be to reduce the Petroleum Development Levy and General Sales Tax on electricity bills, thereby lowering fuel and transport costs, easing the cost of living and production, and reducing inflationary pressures. The government should also double the annual income-tax exemption threshold from Rs600,000 to Rs1.2 million, providing much-needed relief to salaried individuals and low-income taxpayers. The government should also restore the Final Tax Regime for exporters, making tax deducted from export proceeds their final income tax liability, even if the rate remains 2pc. This would simplify taxation, eliminate uncertainty and improve the competitiveness of Pakistani exporters by bringing their tax treatment closer to that of competitors in peer economies. Equally important is the complete abolition of the super tax, which reportedly generates around Rs400-500bn annually, but has become a major deterrent to new investment. The highest effective income tax rate on corporate, business and other incomes should be capped at 35pc, making Pakistan more attractive to investors while leaving greater disposable income available for savings, consumption and investment. Many such measures could be financed through the proposed annual debt-servicing savings of Rs2,100-2,500bn, provided the government pursues the necessary reforms with determination and ensures that the resulting fiscal space is used to reduce the tax burden rather than expand public expenditure. These measures would provide a much-needed stimulus to economic growth, encourage investment and job creation, and help alleviate poverty, which has become one of the country’s most pressing challenges. Reviving economic growth requires reducing the excessive tax burden on ordinary citizens and businesses — a burden that stifles investment, discourages enterprise and holds back economic activity.
