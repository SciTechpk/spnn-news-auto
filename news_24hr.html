Congress party leaders have in turn accused Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s BJP of trying to create a spectacle and give the book a “political spin”. Titled Belonging and released on Tuesday, the memoir captures Italian-born Gandhi’s journey from her student days in England, where she met her husband and Nehru-Gandhi dynasty scion Rajiv, to her integration into India’s most powerful political family that gave the country three prime ministers. Gandhi, 79, herself turned down the prime minister’s job in 2004, but her family continues to control the party that has ruled India for periods totalling nearly 55 years since independence from British colonial rule in 1947. “Sonia Gandhi could have titled her memoir ‘Whitewashing’, instead of ‘Belonging’ because the motive of this book appears to be sugar-coating the historical wrongs perpetrated by the Nehru-Gandhi family, and camouflaging the cardinal sins committed by the Congress party,” BJP spokesperson C.R. Kesavan said in a video statement. Kesavan cited the anti-Sikh riots of 1984 which erupted after then-prime minister and Sonia’s mother-in-law Indira Gandhi was assassinated by Sikh bodyguards. Nearly 3,000 people were killed. Rights activists accuse Congress of turning a blind eye and say that some party leaders helped orchestrate the carnage. Personal memoir In the book, Sonia describes the riots as a tragic and shameful chapter, accusing some officials of looking the other way or even fuelling the violence. She defends husband Rajiv who succeeded his mother as prime minister against allegations of complicity, while acknowledging failures of governance and delayed justice. Kesavan accused Sonia of trying to “normalise and justify” the notorious Emergency imposed by Indira. The 21-month state of Emergency in 1975 involved suspending civil liberties, censoring the press and jailing opposition leaders in one of the most controversial periods of India’s history. Sonia writes that Indira felt increasingly isolated and politically cornered before imposing the Emergency. She says Indira was never entirely comfortable with it but felt compelled to continue it amid the prevailing political turmoil. Although Sonia has herself not responded to the accusations, she said in the book’s prologue that it is a “personal memoir, one for which pol­itics is the background, a kind of framework”. US midterms put pro-Israel AIPAC on defensive

WASHINGTON: For years, the pro-Israel lobbying group AIPAC was a US political juggernaut, but deepening voter disillusion over US-Israeli ties and wins by openly Israel-sceptic candidates in last week’s midterm elections have left it reeling. Candidates of all stripes long courted the American Israel Public Affairs Committee’s support. But what was once an asset has become a liability in many races, as favourable opinions of Israel plummet over civilian deaths in Gaza and the costly Iran war. In the Michigan Senate race, AIPAC’s once irresistible force ran into an immovable object. Demo­crat Abdul El-Sayed — who tapped into anger over US funding for Israel’s military — faced AIPAC-supported candidates in both the primary and general election, but emerged victorious to become the first Muslim senator. The tens of millions of dollars spent by an AIPAC-affiliated group, the United Democracy Project, not only failed to beat El-Sayed — they helped him boost his own messaging. “To AIPAC… Thank you for giving me the opportunity to beat you twice,” the Michigan politician taunted in his victory speech. The tables flipped so completely that allies of El-Sayed’s Republican opponent, Mike Rogers, reportedly even asked the group to stop spending on him at one point. “They definitely have become a liability,” Adam Wood, a veteran Democratic campaign strategist with City & State Public Affairs, said, noting several campaigns he worked on in years past “coveted” AIPAC’s support. Another Democratic Senate success story, James Talarico, won in Texas with a similar message of ending US funding for Israeli offensive weaponry and keeping distance from AIPAC. “I’ve never taken money from AIPAC, and I’m not taking money from AIPAC in this campaign,” Talarico said early in his bid. Israel support plummets Officially, AIPAC is nonpartisan. It has traditionally supported candidates from both the left and the right. According to its website, it only accepts money from Americans and its board is composed exclusively of Americans. But the group has become a byword in US politics for Washington’s tight ties with Israeli governments, including the outgoing far-right coalition of Benjamin Netanyahu. The Chicago Council on Global Affairs has asked Americans to rate their favourability of Israel on a scale of 0 to 100 regularly since 1978 and this year marked a record low: an average score of just 44. That’s down from 58 in 2022, when the previous midterms were held. And for Democrats, the numbers are all the starker: a drop in favourability from 52 in 2022 to just 30 today. “Democrats started to really tank on Israel,” Dina Smeltz, a former State Department official who is now a managing director at the council, said. Leftist Democratic star, Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, has branded AIPAC as “a right-wing organization that undermines democracy.” But support also appears to be faltering on the right. On election day, President Donald Trump dialed into his favorite morning program, Fox & Friends, and made the surprising announcement that the US would be ending a major annual aid package to Israel — something El-Sayed and others on the left have repeatedly demanded. Vice President JD Vance said he’d been told by a trusted pollster that his party’s road to recovery after the midterms annihilation will require, in part, changing “our relationship to Israel.” And other right-wing voices, including broadcaster Tucker Carlson and libertarian Representative Thomas Massie, have openly criticized Israel in recent years in ways unimaginable in the 2000s and 2010s. Pro-Israel lobbyists are not going to go away of course. AIPAC already has competition from the left-leaning pro-Israel J Street as well as the recently formed pro-Palestine American Priorities super PAC. The aftermath of elections in Israel, with the possible political eclipse of Netanyahu, could also help AIPAC to find its feet again. “It has been around for a long time, it has a lot of money,” Smeltz said. History and leadership

HISTORY is never linear or monolithic. It may progress in leaps and bounds, or falter and regress. It may appear calm and seamless, but be restive and roiling beneath the surface. No wonder, a small trigger can lead to a cataclysm. Historians have long debated whether historical dialectics shape human destiny, or human agency lights up the darkest epochs of history. There’s no definite answer as yet. But there is a broad consensus among them on two requisites of a historical ‘development’ — ripe ‘objective’ conditions, and an intelligent leadership that can transform a conducive environment into, at the very least, meaningful change. Interestingly, the modern era manifests both trends. We see countries and societies long affected by broken governance, endemic corruption, political instability, economic meltdown, civil strife, and regional and global conflagrations. Significant parts of the Middle East, North Africa, and South and West Asia are a dismal reflection of failed or failing states ruled by autocrats or absolutist dynasties — largely without popular mandate and without accountability. Yet there are countries once dismissed as ‘basket-cases’ that have turned themselves around — such as Singapore, Malaysia, Vietnam, Turkiye, South Korea, and China. Over the years, they have taken tremendous strides in terms of political stability, socioeconomic progress, scientific and technological advancement, and human-resource development. They have carved out a large share for themselves in international supply chains and productivity. Much of the credit for their phenomenal achievements goes to their visionary, upright, and courageous political and institutional leadership. True, some of them did not begin as ideal democracies. Yet they built robust, efficient institutions, along with enabling infrastructure, to ensure continuity, stability, peace, and development, and to facilitate a gradual transition to democracy. In contrast, our state has been stuck in a time warp for decades. Every generation has suffered gross ‘elite capture’ characterised by authoritarianism, disenfranchisement, misgovernance, instability, inequality, impoverishment, and never-ending conflicts. In dialectical terms, although the objective conditions for radical sociopolitical change have long been ripe, the desired agency of change — the political and institutional leadership — has remained elusive. No wonder the state continues to exist and function in colonial and neocolonial moulds. Authoritarian governance, a compromised and complex legal order, an extractive and iniquitous social structure, the dominance of the security and administrative apparatuses, and a pro-elite neoliberal policy framework remain among its key features. Our state has been stuck in a time warp for decades. But has this state worked? Never. The founding ideal of a democratic republic itself has been upended. Instead, a security and administrative juggernaut has been forged on the pretext of guarding national security and ‘ideology’. In fact, the state has been virtually detached from its constitutional and democratic controls. It seeks to draw its power, legitimacy, and even sustenance not from the people’s mandate, but from its coercive powers and capacity to control. Hence, it does not need to worry about citizens’ rights or welfare. However, this mindset has drawn an ever-widening wedge between the state — controlled and exploited by a small club of security, bureaucratic, political, landed, industrial, and financial elites — and the people, more than half of whom are classified as ‘poor’ or ‘very poor’. Yet, instead of correcting this perilous course, already scarce resources are being further drained for security obligations — arguably, not so much at home as abroad. This raises a disturbing question: for how long can this behemoth, guided by colonial notions of governance and built around failed politico-legal machinery, sustain itself on the proverbial feet of clay — an ever-brittle economy compounded by worsening security and governance failure? This question has arisen in every post-colonial state where elites have captured power, stolen resources, disenfranchised people, and eventually pushed society into chaos and conflict. But in many instances, upright, visionary, and courageous leadership provided the answers — convincingly — by steering the country out of the woods. Do we have such leadership that can also provide convincing answers — by transforming public angst and alienation towards dysfunctional state structures into a force for building state and political institutions that are democratic, equitable, accountable, and, especially, peace-loving? Ironically, much of the mainstream political leadership — the PPP, PML-N, PTI, and others — has long forsaken its distinct agenda, and even its identity. Rhetoric aside, they have offered little in the way of ‘answers’ to the many problems afflicting the state and governance. Instead, over the decades, they have tended to outrun one another in their efforts to be more ‘compatible’ with the demands of an extractive state toeing the colonial model. The PPP-PML-N duo’s joining hands with non-political elements to weaken the judiciary, election commission, and media, and the PTI’s eagerness to mend fences with the powers that be instead of the release of its leader are some recent instances of crass political opportunism and historical compromises on the part of our ‘popular’ leaders. So, the causes of our recurrent sociopolitical tragedies and the decline of democracy should not be traced to history’s apathy in the face of ominous lengthening shadows. It is the frayed moral fabric of much of our leadership — political and institutional — that gives preference to personal, political, and institutional interests over democracy and the good of the people — the real masters. Under these hopeless circumstances, a new class of self-led movers of history — a sort of Gen-Z — is needed to change our collective destiny. External sector turning points

THE slight increase in workers’ remittances reported for October prompted a host of questions directed to me. Here are the responses. The rise is too small to matter. In fact, it falls far short of what it needs to be to serve its purpose: helping plug the trade deficit. There is no reason in the world for celebration based on one data outturn. What matters is the trend that a series of data points describe; most often, that trend is intelligible only when seen in its proper context. So what is the proper context for remittances? First, remittances are traditionally how Pakis­tan’s economy finances its trade deficits. The trend is cyclical and reveals itself when you take a 12-month average of the total trade balance plus remittances, then plot that on a month-to-month basis. You will notice something. A wavy line emerges. From a low point in 2015, it rises slowly until it briefly turns positive — around $40 million — in November 2015. Then it plunges back below the X-axis, before bottoming out in July 2018 at around negative $1.45 billion. This is trade balance plus remittances, mind you, so a negative value means remittances can’t plug the trade deficit, and the balance is being financed from reserves or borrowing. If you don’t see the reserves plunging by the same amount in that month, then you can be sure the balance is being financed by new borrowing, and that data will show up in the balance-of-payments whenever the next release comes along. From July 2018, the line rises sharply again until it hits a fresh peak in November 2020 at around $100m. It then plunges again until it hits its second trough in July 2022 at negative $1.14bn. This is when another attempt to stabilise Pakistan’s economy by choking its imports began — abortively, as it turned out. At least since June, the trade deficit’s growth has outstripped remittances. After that, the next peak — $780m — was in March 2025; it has stayed above the X-axis for the most part, barring one or two months. But if the trend so far is anything to go by, the line has been falling since March, reaching $480m by September. This is the 12-month average. For September alone, the figure is closer to $150m. The October trade data has not yet been released, so we don’t know where the figure will be for that month. But given the pace of increase in imports, and the relative stagnation of export growth, grounds for excess optimism are conspicuous by their absence. This is entirely in sync with the long-run trend of how Pakistan’s external sector behaves. It heaves and ebbs in cycles. Take the earliest low point of the trade deficit in some month and trace it forward. What you see is the trade deficit growing as imports outstrip exports each month, but remittances cover the gap. Hence, the overall current account in the balance of payments remains above zero. Then the trade deficit starts to grow beyond the ability of the remittances to cover. The current account falls into deficit. Borrowing increases during these months to cover the deficit. As the required pace of borrowing increases, the government is unable to fetch dollars in quantities sufficient to pay for the deficit, and the reserves start to fall. Once that fall begins, it becomes very difficult to contain the situation. Eventually, the reserves drop to a level that necessitates a return to the IMF for a bailout. That bailout requires taking steps to choke imports and kill the fiscal deficit as well, and eventually normalcy returns, but at the cost of choking economic activity in the country. Take four indicators and plot them from 2014 onwards (you can start earlier if you like). Plot exports, imports, remittances, and external payment obligations over a 12-month period from that year onward. For external obligations, use the total figure for “predetermined drains” given in the International Liquidity sheet released every month by the State Bank. What you’ll see is that exports are the slowest to grow over the years, while external obligations are the fastest. Here is what the numbers look like: exports have grown by 1.4x since 2014, imports by 1.6x, remittances by 2.7x, and external obligations by 4.6x. This is the real long-run trend that needs to break before any indicator can mean anything. This year alone, for example, the trade deficit has grown faster than remittances. There are a few different ways to measure this growth, and each gives you slightly different numbers. But all of them show that, at least since June, the trade deficit’s growth has outstripped remittances. If this trend continues, then it shows we have already passed the peak that historically defines the turning point from growth to bust. And there is little reason today to believe that the trend will turn around on its own. In past periods, governments had a story to tell at this point, even if the story held little water. In both past peaks, when the trade deficit began yawning and eating up all current inflows, both governments in power at each peak said their period had seen a large inflow of machinery that, once installed, would lead to export growth and reverse the trend. That story failed to pan out both times. But at least it was there. What is the story now? What is this government’s plan for containing the trade deficit, which is only a few months away from outstripping remittances’ ability to plug it? The only story they’re running with these days is some urgency to see dramatic improvements in the country’s credit rating by June 2027. What is that in preparation for? More borrowing? He warned that when courts became subservient to political or executive pressure, the law was replaced by the will of the powerful and applied selectively rather than impartially. Outgoing judge warns of anarchy if citizens lose faith in justice system Safeguarding judicial independence was therefore the collective responsibility of Parliament, the judiciary and the executive, he observed, adding that citizens would otherwise lose faith in the justice system. The reference was attended by Chief Justice of Pakistan (CJP) Yahya Afridi, Attorney General for Pakistan Mansoor Usman Awan, Pakistan Bar Council Vice Chairman Pir Muhammad Masood Chishti, Supreme Court Bar Association President Syed Amjad Shah, senior law officers and members of the legal fraternity. Justice Mandokhail said that in the absence of an independent judiciary, political dissenters and critics could be targeted, while systematic corruption, human rights abuses and illegal actions by state officials would go unchecked. In such circumstances, citizens might resort to their own means of seeking justice, potentially leading to anarchy, he cautioned. The outgoing judge emphasised that the Constitution was both a source of authority and a reminder of its limits. It prescribed what Parliament, the judiciary and the executive must and must not do, he said, adding that the judiciary’s constitutional role was to keep the other organs of the state within their limits. The judiciary’s authority lay in interpreting and applying the Constitution faithfully. Serving the Constitution meant recognising that power was entrusted, not owned, that constitutional rights were not favours and that the law must remain equally accessible to ordinary citizens and the powerful, he said. Justice Mandokhail also underscored that the judicial oath demanded impartiality, integrity and unwavering adherence to the Constitution and the law, without fear, favour or external influence. Paying tribute to Justice Mandokhail, Chief Justice Afridi lauded his distinguished judicial career and commitment to the administration of justice. Recalling his professional journey from the bar in Balochistan to the Supreme Court, he acknowledged his contributions to constitutional jurisprudence, judicial administration and institutional development.
