The decision, opposed by the industrial sector, is likely to have a negative impact on all electricity consumers, including those served by K-Electric and other distribution companies (Discos). Industrial consumers complained that the Rs25.30 per unit revised ICP — against the actual marginal cost of Rs31.83 or Rs29.65 per unit calculated by Nepra —resulted in higher monthly fuel cost adjustments (FCAs) and burdened all consumer categories across the country. This was despite the fact that the ICP announced in December last year on the prime minister’s instructions was based on the commitment that any additional costs arising from the package would remain ring-fenced, and that the difference between consumer-end tariff and ICP would not be shifted to any other consumer category except the ICP beneficiaries, the consumers said. Nepra members’ observations In the determination issued on the ICP last Friday, all four members of the regulator unanimously held the Power Division and its subordinate entities responsible for failing to fulfil their legal obligations within the prescribed timelines, pushing Nepra into an unprecedented deadlock and potentially increasing costs for electricity consumers across the country. The authority was divided 2-2 on the matter. They said that Nepra’s own institutional calculations put the marginal cost at Rs31.83 per unit. “The revised incremental rate of Rs25.30 per unit proposed by the Ministry of Energy (MoE), and allowed by the honourable chairman and Member (Law), is based on the generation mix apparently assumed at the design stage of the package, with updated fuel prices,” the two members observed. They called for the immediate suspension of the ICP until authentic data became available. The dissenting members argued that compliance with the federal cabinet’s decision and Nepra’s own directives required a reassessment of the existing rate of Rs22.98 per unit to determine whether it should be retained, revised or suspended. However, this exercise had not been carried out, resulting in the continuation of the package at a rate below the actual cost of supplying incremental electricity. “The resulting misalignment has had implications for consumers in two ways. First, to the extent incremental consumption increased the system’s average fuel cost, the resulting impact was recovered from all consumers through the FCA mechanism. Second, because incremental units are excluded from quarterly tariff adjustments, the exclusion of such units increases the quarterly adjustment borne by consumers not availing the package,” they said. They questioned how a package based on the principle of cost-reflectivity could continue without a validated cost-reflective rate, arguing that since the alternative rate had no credible link with the actual marginal cost, continuation of the package was no longer justified. They said other consumers should not be made to bear the cost of a review that the MoE was required to submit five months ago but had yet to provide. In his casting vote, Mukhtar observed that the dissenting members’ conclusions “need serious consideration”. However, he said suspending the package at this stage would not be appropriate and that the MoE should be given another opportunity to provide the information repeatedly sought by the regulator, including in its latest determination. Forex crowding out

THE State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) has successfully rebuilt foreign exchange reserves over the last three years. Much of this success, however, has been purchased by drawing liquidity directly out of the forex market. It attributes its purchases to improved market liquidity and describes their purpose as strengthening external buffers and the capacity to meet external obligations. These are legitimate considerations. Yet the extent to which dollars have been siphoned from the market to accumulate reserves raises concerns for the wider economy. When the central bank becomes a persistent and dominant buyer of dollars in the market, it effectively crowds out some private businesses, which must compete with an institution that enjoys an informational advantage. While Pakistan is familiar with the crowding out of the private sector through large and persistent government borrowing to finance budget deficits, crowding out in the foreign exchange market is unprecedented. If the forex market has sufficient depth, it can smoothly accommodate both the central bank’s purchases and the private sector’s requirements. But where the market is constrained, the adjustment has to take place somewhere. It may appear in the exchange rate, transaction spreads, delays in opening letters of credit (LCs), or restrictions on access to foreign currency. In effect, private sector buyers are either priced out of the market or pushed into waiting queues. There have been several media reports of import payment delays and dollar rationing by some banks. Concerns about these practices have also surfaced in official trade assessments. The US National Trade Estimate Report 2026, for instance, records stakeholder concerns about delays in banks’ issuance of LCs, reportedly resulting from informal SBP limitations on commercial banks’ borrowing of foreign currency through the interbank market. The report also highlights that corporations and foreign equity holders have encountered administrative delays, documentation hurdles, and informal queuing systems when seeking to repatriate their profits, dividends, and royalties in dollars. The SBP must reassess its market interventions. Such restrictions carry consequences. Central banking, in essence, is not an accounting exercise and balance sheet management. Every action has microeconomic and macroeconomic repercussions for resource allocation, business costs, investment decisions, and, ultimately, economic growth. Thus, a policy intended to strengthen the country’s external position can, paradoxically, undermine investors’ sentiments and the productive capacity to generate future foreign exchange. A further paradox concerns the exchange rate signal. Despite extensive purchases, the exchange rate is not responding. Conventional macroeconomic models offer limited help in explaining such interventions. Instead, an exchange rate microstructure approach, built on order flow, may offer a better explanation of exchange rate determination. Order flow refers to the buying and selling pressure generated by market participants. This approach recognises the importance of participants’ identities, information and trading behaviour in influencing exchange rate movements. A study by Evans and Lyons, published in the Journal of Political Economy in 2002, shows that order flow has substantial explanatory power for exchange rate changes. When a central bank, which also issues licences to the market participants, enters the market, its order flow may convey its preferences about price as well as quantity. Forex dealers, observing this institutional order flow, may adjust their quotes and inventory positi­ons accordingly. Consequently, the exchange rate may increasingly reflect the central bank’s institutional prefere­nces rather than the underlying balance of forex demand and supply. Granular level data can explain forex dynamics on the basis of the order flow model. The SBP has both the technical capacity and the high-frequency data required for such analysis. It could examine import payment delays, outstanding foreign exchange requests, transaction spreads, interbank turnover, and the daily pattern of its own purchases. A range of forex market data is already covered by IMF monitoring and reporting requirements on a daily basis. Greater transparency and wider dissemination of this data may also allow independent researchers to apply an order flow model to exchange rate formation in Pakistan. Reserve accumulation is a necessary but not sufficient condition for stability, which ultimately depends on the economy’s ability to generate foreign exchange through exports, investment, and productive activity. The SBP must, therefore, assess its market interventions within a robust analytical framework and develop an exit strategy for its dollar purchases for reserve accumulation.
