States of exception: what the Constitution, 3 court rulings and 50 years say about the KP emergency

‘Sovereign is he who decides on the exception,’ wrote the notorious German thinker Carl Schmitt in 1922. Or, put simply, whoever gets to decide when the rules stop applying is the one actually in charge. Gifted with a big brain and sharp turn of phrase, Mr Schmitt went on to become a noted critic of parliament, constitutionalism, and liberal democracy. For a time, the age was also his: in the ‘20s back then, much like the ‘20s now, such subjects had fallen out of fashion. But as a much-quoted critique of Mr Schmitt went, ‘His preferred cure turned out to be infinitely worse than the disease.’ Why did this happen? So too, the centre tells us, that the ministry of Sohail Afridi in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is the disease, and emergency the cure. As to what kind of disease, we can’t be sure. The centre claims militancy; terror bleeding back into the headlines. But if that is the main case to be made, everything that attaches to Sohail Afridi in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa also attaches to Sarfraz Bugti in Balochistan, a province nearly half the country’s landmass. The unity regime is unhappy with such comparisons; apples and bowling balls, they say: Bugti’s ministers are cooperating with the centre, whereas Afridi’s are Taliban sympathisers. That, too, is an odd distinction: as Maulana Fazl-ur-Rehman pointed out, Nawaz League had opposed Musharraf’s terror wars not too long ago, and no one had sent home the maulana’s coalition for putting up similar opposition in the same province after 2002. Then there’s the timing: militancy has been resurgent in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa for years. Yet the din of emergency only sounded when Afridi announced he would hit the streets over access to his jailed leader, Imran Khan. Bloody terror attacks, and there were several, were never the trigger point. If there was an explanation, it came — intentionally or otherwise — from prime minister Shehbaz Sharif the day emergency was declared. Sharif cited the PTI’s Long March protests at length: ‘Islamabad ki charhai…ka napak mansooba,’ was how he described it to his cabinet, before thanking Maulana Fazl for trying to keep the peace between the centre and the PTI (the maulana has since blamed the centre for backpedalling on its commitment). ‘It’s now or never,’ concluded Sharif, ‘…History will remember us for such decisions, in this way or that way.’ But history is already heading in that way, not this one: displacing a duly elected provincial government with a two-thirds majority is certainly memorable, but not for reasons the prime minister may suspect. After all, it’s a question of perspective: when Asif Zardari imposed governor’s rule in Punjab in February 2009, then-deposed chief minister Shehbaz Sharif had taken a much poorer view of the issue. The same president is now doing it on the same man’s advice in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa: the unity regime has imposed both emergency as well as governor’s rule. Breaking down emergency These two provisions of the Constitution — emergency under Article 232, and governor’s rule under Article 234 — have different purposes and different tests. Exceptions exist in the affairs of states, and they are a part of the law for a reason: Pervez Musharraf was sentenced to death not for the act of declaring emergency in 2007, but for expressly refusing to use the Constitution to do it. That said, there are rules to follow: emergency requires the threat of war or external aggression, or an internal disturbance beyond Peshawar’s power to control. Governor’s rule, by contrast, is the answer to a narrower problem: when a provincial government can no longer function under the Constitution. Both have trade-offs: while the provincial assembly retains some of its lawmaking powers under emergency, those powers can be handed off to Islamabad under governor’s rule. Yet whereas governor’s rule cannot be extended beyond six months, emergency — subject to parliamentary approval — does not contain any hard outer limit within the text. Crucially, emergency also lends the centre an umbrella to suspend fundamental rights under a third provision, Article 233. For reasons obvious, the unity regime has decided to go for broke, and push both buttons 232 and 234. By mixing two very distinct provisions in the same soup, it can try and claim the best of both worlds: emergency that can go on and on, that can swallow up fundamental rights, and that can, for the present, shutter the assembly via the governor. Of course, opposition protests, at least at the time of the proclamation, neither amount to external aggression nor to internal disturbance required for emergency. They also do not speak to the breakdown of provincial government required for governor’s rule. This is quite apart from the fact that going for a combo deal is a strange step: Article 232 assumes a provincial government overwhelmed by events, and leaves its assembly standing. Article 234 says the government itself cannot function constitutionally. It is hard to imagine both being true (and, in any event, neither is). Plus, the unity regime is diving over guardrails it once built itself: the Eighteenth Amendment requires a resolution of the province’s own assembly for an internal emergency. Failing that, each house of parliament must approve within 10 days. Zardari, who signed off on the original amendment once upon a time, has used the backdoor: at no point did the province consent. What becomes of the PTI government? Afridi’s ministry may have been displaced for now, but it cannot be ousted outright. That can only happen when he loses the legislature’s confidence: as a Supreme Court concurrence put it in Pir Sabir Shah v. Federation in 1994, ‘The president, therefore, cannot dismiss the chief minister and the provincial cabinet in exercise of his power under Article 234.’ Afridi thus returns the second the proclamation of governor’s rule is lifted, per Sabir Shah, along with his cabinet and assembly. As for ‘history remembering’, Sharif has his own party men to thank for securing such a verdict in 1994, that too against the Bhutto-Zardaris’ attempt at ouster. ‘History will remember’: what have the courts held? A year after Sabir Shah, when the same PPP thought to remove chief minister Manzoor Wattoo in Punjab, it again ended in tears: Shehbaz Sharif, then leader of the opposition, even raised the alleged plot in the provincial assembly. The Lahore High Court ruled against the PPP’s power grab: to impose governor’s rule under Article 234, a ‘breakdown of constitutional machinery’ was a must. No such breakdown was in the offing then in Punjab, nor now in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. As for emergency, the test is even starker. Again, the ruling regime would know: in May 1998, the prime minister’s brother Nawaz Sharif had imposed emergency under Article 232 and suspended fundamental rights under Article 233, right after Pakistan and India’s nuclear tests. But Nawaz’s emergency was challenged in the Supreme Court by former president Farooq Leghari and one Imran Khan, described (in a strange echo of network television’s ongoing ban) as ‘chairman of a political party Tehrik-e-Insaf’. While the Court ruled in favour of emergency, it did so explicitly on the basis of ‘imminent danger of external aggression’. In 1998, that meant mushroom clouds. In 2026, it means Sohail Afridi’s caravan meandering down from Peshawar. Not least, it felt the government’s claims of lawlessness in Sindh and sectarian killings in Punjab didn’t count as actionable internal disturbances, and that Islamabad had no good reason to suspend fundamental rights. The Court struck down the suspension. Compared to the present, context is also important. Under Article 232, Chief Justice Ajmal Mian held in Leghari, ‘the existence of the nation itself is at stake’. In 1998, that meant mushroom clouds. In 2026, it means Sohail Afridi’s caravan meandering down from Peshawar. Let the voter decide The unity regime’s latest disaster is not only self-inflicted, it is also self-defeating. Back when Zulfikar Ali Bhutto dismissed the National Awami Party’s ministry in Balochistan in 1973, it was for reasons that rhymed with today’s: terror, disloyalty, and the backing of hostile powers. Yet the only thing Bhutto managed to destroy was his opposition. After Quetta fell, the NAP’s coalition in the frontier (led, in yet another irony, by Maulana Fazl’s father Mufti Mahmood) resigned in protest. A four-year insurgency followed in one province, and mayhem and murder in the other. It turns out that displacing an elected government in a restive land has been tried before. The only way through, in both Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan, is through greater representation, not less. When Pakistan did prevail against insurgency in the previous round, it was because of a hard-won consensus between state and society. That consensus is fracturing. Emergency will be its accelerant.

Perilous misstep

THE federal government on Sunday made good on its threat to dismiss an elected government and impose governor’s rule and emergency in KP. The president obliged. The head of state, the only civilian elected twice to that office, has now imposed governor’s rule three times: Punjab in 2009, Balochistan in 2013, and now KP — though the ruling PML-N is the main driver behind the latest move. His party, the PPP, has done so six times in the short history of Pakistani democracy, the others being in 1973 (Balochistan), 1975 (the then NWFP), and 1994 (again, KP). There is, therefore, nothing novel about Sunday’s move. However, it is a measure that has never achieved what its authors intended, and has more often than not backfired. That, perhaps, is why even those unsympathetic to the PTI government in Peshawar had been warning Islamabad not to take this step. In going ahead with it, the centre assumes responsibility for any blowback. The imposition of governor’s rule in Balochistan in 1973 is instructive. In February of that year, the then prime minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto dismissed the Balochistan government led by Sardar Attaullah Mengal, and also removed the then governor, Mir Ghaus Bakhsh Bizenjo. The centre ruled directly through Nawab Akbar Khan Bugti and sent in the army, fuelling an already smouldering insurgency. Many elected Baloch leaders turned separatist. The province never really recovered. KP is not Balochistan, and the PTI is no separatist movement. But the parallel of an elected leadership being swept aside on the pretext of security is unmistakable. The experience of the 1973 decision should be seen as a warning that this is how estrangement from the federation begins. There is no political justification for governor’s rule. If the authorities felt terrorism was increasing in some tribal districts and adjoining areas, they could have limited themselves to imposing a targeted emergency under Article 232, as they have already done. To deprive an entire province of its elected government is not only undemocratic; it can also be severely counterproductive, as history shows. The stated reason for Sunday’s decision is terrorism. The real target is perceived to be the only provincial government the opposition held, and the agitation it was leading. The PTI government may have faltered on several fronts, but in a democratic society, those failings are supposed to be addressed through the electoral process, not through executive fiat. Under Article 234, the proclamation lapses within two months unless parliament approves it. That is where this ill-considered action should be challenged on principle: lawmakers would do well to scrutinise it, and the courts to test whether the constitutional threshold was actually met. Why stability is elusive

RECENT events are another reminder of how elusive political stability remains in the country. The confrontation between PTI and the government escalated after an effort at dialogue between them ended in mutual recriminations. PTI pressed ahead with its protest march that the federal government tried to stop by threatening to oust the KP government through imposition of governor’s rule. The usual wave of arrests of party activists preceded the opposition’s march. Now the Shehbaz Sharif government has imposed an emergency  and governor’s rule in KP in a move that is likely inflame sentiment in the province and exacerbate the situation. This is a rerun of a familiar script, except it is set against a backdrop of deepening authoritarianism. It also marks a dangerous phase in confrontational politics with the federal authorities and the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa government at loggerheads. Inflammatory rhetoric by federal ministers was matched by fiery speeches from KP’s Chief Minister Sohail Afridi. Other than the brief interlude of dialogue, the government responded to the opposition protest by its usual mix of coercion, threats, and incendiary statements. It took the consequential decision to impose an emergency in KP and bring the province under federal control. The cabinet has approved this while parliament has been convened to sanction the move. The most egregious use of coercion was the reported abductions and incommunicado detention of over eight PTI MPs, including three members of the National Assembly. Using coercive means to manage the opposition has only reinforced the government’s slide into authoritarianism, which has resulted in the country’s democratic decline. This has been evidenced in the past couple of years in actions taken against the opposition and other critics: banning public gatherings, breaking up protest demonstrations, arresting opposition supporters, raiding homes of political leaders, filing cases against them, policing the media, imposing internet restrictions and criminalising free speech. Another telling indicator of democratic regression is the undermining of judicial independence by controversial amendments and defiance of court orders. The erosion of institutions meant to check executive power has contributed to growing autocratisation. The shell of democracy remains, but it is increasingly hollowed out. The aim of these actions is to tighten the government’s grip on the political system and the country. But it does not make the government strong. It signals weakness, not confidence. Some argue there is nothing new in this phase of repression, that it continues a familiar tradition and borrows from a well-worn playbook. Previous civilian governments, too, were guilty of undemocratic conduct, intolerance of opposition, jailing rivals on trumped-up charges, marginalising parliament, curbing press freedom, and undermining judicial independence. All of this is true. But the scale and scope of the repressive measures today go beyond anything seen under a civilian government before. Using coercive measures and undermining institutions do not produce stability. Public space is being encroached upon in an unprecedented manner. Because there is more to control in the digital era, especially social media, the present government has gone much further than its civilian predecessors in imposing digital space curbs and censorship and micromanaging the electronic media. The rationale offered by spokesmen of the hybrid government, when pressed to justify such measures, is that they are necessary for political stability. But does the veneer of calm imposed by force bring stability? On the contrary, it is a recipe for instability. Coercion does not produce stability. It deepens polarisation, erodes the social contract, divides the country further, and fuels uncertainty. The opposition represents a large section of society, has a significant parliamentary presence, and runs the government in KP. To suppress and demonise it does not promote stability. It guarantees the continuation of political confrontation. The economic cost of relentless political tensions and government-opposition conflict is plain to see. Frequent shutdowns and blockade of cities hit businesses hard. It vitiates the business climate. This at a time when recovery has yet to take hold, economic growth remains anaemic and stabilisation is still fragile. Jittery investors hold back, deterred by the spectre of turmoil and unrest. Celebrating every tranche received from the IMF doesn’t obscure the reality that the economy is yet to embark on the path to sustainable growth. Meanwhile, all recent surveys — Gallup, Ipsos — show declining public confidence in economic conditions and pessimism about the country’s economic future. THE significance of Mir Geo­rge Ali Murad Khan Talpur II, the last ruler of the former princely state of Khairpur who died on Oct 5, lies in more than just having witnessed the disappearance of a world. In Khairpur’s final years, he presided over an experiment in representative government and social development that deserves to be remembered. Born in Brighton on June 29, 1933, the eighth Talpur ruler of Khairpur was named George after King George V, whom his father, Mir Faiz, admired. In 1944, his father suffered a breakdown and the state was then run by the British Resident with the aid of local ministers. The announcement of the end of the British Indian Empire under the June 3, 1947 plan meant that Khairpur, in treaty relations with the British since 1838, would regain its sovereign status on Aug 15, 1947, when British paramountcy laps­ed. Thus, in order to make the transition smoother, the British deposed the ill Mir Faiz, and on July 24, 1947 the 14-year-old George Ali Murad became the Mir of Khairpur, with his uncle Ghulam Hussain Talpur as Regent. Khairpur celebrated Aug 15 as its Independence Day, but its position on the railway linking Punjab and Sindh made it strategically vital to Pakistan. Negotiations followed, and on Oct 3, 1947 the regent signed the Instrument of Accession, making Khairpur the first contiguous princely state to join Pakistan. It ceded defence, foreign affairs and communications, but retained its sovereignty and the power to accept or reject a future constitution of Pakistan. Joining Pakistan brought new challenges and opportunities and Mir George Ali Murad rose to both. After a stint at Cambridge, he assumed full ruling powers in September 1951. Teaming up with Mumtaz Kizilbash, chief minister since 1948, he oversaw a series of reforms that changed the nature of the state and the life of its people. In a first, Khairpur held Pakistan’s first elections on the basis of universal adult franchise and its 15-member Assembly, elected in May 1950, was the country’s first fully democratic chamber. No wonder then, when Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan inaugurated it, he noted that Khairpur had “stolen a march on others”. The genius of the Mir was that he recognised and appreciated democratic government and worked with it for the uplift of his people. Soon under the Government of Khairpur Act 1953 the state became a province, with the Mir as its constitutional head, akin to a governor. Khairpur also led the way in development. Its income nearly tripled, from Rs5.7 million in 1948 to Rs16.9m in 1955, giving Khairpur a per capita budget income of Rs53 against Punjab’s Rs20. No doubt this was spurred on by the Khairpur Textile Mill, established in 1951, which employed over 2,000 people. Over 22 per cent of the budget went to education, the highest of any unit in Pakistan. Education was free up to Matric, with free books and hostels, and elementary education was compulsory for boys. As a result, its literacy rate nearly doubled in 10 years. But just as things were going well, all came to a halt. Faced with the centre’s plan to merge all of western Pakistan, the Assembly protested that Khairpur was “ahead of even the Governor’s provinces in Pakistan in several respects” and that its people “cannot be compelled against their will to merge…” But these protests fell on deaf ears. Under mounting pressure, the Assembly voted for merger on Nov 10, 1954 and the Mir signed on the dotted line, ceding to Pakistan “his sovereignty and all his rights, authority and powers as Ruler of the State”. A state founded in 1783 came to an end with the inauguration of One Unit on Oct 14, 1955. The merger of the state took its toll on the Mir. He withdrew from public life, but remained deeply attached to Khairpur’s heritage and landscape, turning the family’s old hunting ground at Mehrano into an exemplary wildlife sanctuary. Yet his most enduring attachment was to the people of Khairpur. In his foreword to my book A Princely Affair (2015), he lamented: “Sixty years after the ‘merger’ with Pakistan, the people of Khairpur are still waiting for Pakistan to provide even a fraction of the services and benefits provided by my state.” With his death, the last living link to Khairpur’s princely past is gone. However, the question of what that past achieved, and what its people still expect from Pakistan, remains. A theorist of crisis

BORN in Karachi in 1945, Anwar Shaikh, a giant of Marxian economics and professor at The New School in New York, passed away last week. His father served in the Foreign Service as a result of which his family moved around frequently, through Malaysia, Canada, Kuwait, and finally the US. It was in Kuwait, he would later say in a biographical note from 2016, that economics first claimed him: standing amid the gleaming infrastructure of an oil economy, he could not reconcile the scale of wealth with the poverty of those who produced it. The obvious question was why. He spent the next half-century building an answer to this and its related questions. I came to Shaikh’s work first through an undergraduate class: Aasim Sajjad Akhtar’s Political Economy of Development and Underdevelopment course, which featured Shaikh’s short four-page elaboration of Marx’s theory of exploitation published in a 1990 reader on Marxian economics compiled by Eatwell, Milgate, and Newman. It was only later on, after having joined academia myself, that I picked up a more sustained interest in his political economy contributions. Many of these were based on the workings of the advanced capitalist world, but to me it felt possible — via a universalist understanding of the capital-labour relation that characterises our shared modernity — that one could recover a broader insight and logic extendable to other contexts as well. His magnum opus, Capitalism: Competition, Conflict, Crises, published by Oxford in 2016 after nearly 20 years of writing, is not an easy book. It runs into nearly 1,000 pages and I have only been able to digest parts of it, a process aided greatly (and almost singularly) by his video lectures on YouTube. However, its central propositions are clear enough to state plainly — capitalism is driven by the pursuit of profit as a structural consideration. Firms invest, hire, fire, pollute, underpay, and cut corners not out of some moral imperative or ethical inadequacies, but because competition demands it. Competition, Shaikh insisted, is quite unlike the “decorous ballet” of textbook economics. It is a war, fought with prices, wages, lobbying, disinformation, and whatever else gives one firm an edge over another. In this universe, marked as it is by war, it’s a bit difficult to model markets as tending towards equilibrium (as orthodoxy usually claims). Shaikh, instead, argued that rather than any form of stasis, capitalism fluctuates, overshoots, crashes, recovers, and overshoots again. What is worth celebrating in Anwar Shaikh’s work is his insistence that economics is never neutral. Mainstream accounts tend to view these as ‘failures’ (of regulation or rationality). Someone like Shaikh, on the other hand, would see these as systemic features of a mode of production built on separating the producers of value from the means of production. His writings on so-called externalities generated by this systemic feature of competition are worth dwelling on, given the cost-of-living and ecological crises plaguing the planet. Orthodox economics treats pollution, low wages, and dangerous working conditions as costs that firms impose on society from outside the market, ie, as unfortunate side effects, correctable with the right regulations. Shaikh went as far as calling this view of externality a conceptual fraud. Rather than by accident, a firm pollutes because the alternative — investing in cleaner production — costs money that would otherwise be profit. The extraction and pollution are thus internal to how profit works. The same point has also been made at a more philosophical level by theorist Alysa Battistoni, who argues how nature is classified as a “free gift” under capitalism, ie, as something that does not carry with it the moral force of reciprocity or care (unlike a real gift). Beyond the intricate debates with the mainstream, what is worth celebrating in Shaikh’s work is his insistence that economics is never neutral. In his own words, he asserted that “there is no such thing as a value-free or socially neutral economics”. Every model encodes assumptions about how society works and who it works for. The amnesty schemes, the short-lived consumption-led booms, the stabilisation programmes that periodically arrive with the IMF, the growth targets announced at press conferences, the optimistic narratives about investor confidence and trickle-down are all consequences of choices made in a particular direction and for particular interests. Shaikh spent his career mapping those choices with a view to uncovering their systemic root in the unceasing motivation for profit. By implication, he ends up showing how reformist ‘tinkering’ that seeks to curtail externalities and fallouts is ultimately futile, and how a far more fundamental overhaul is required. Shaikh was educated partly at St Patrick’s in Karachi, before finishing his schooling in New York, his undergraduate at Princeton, his doctoral studies at Columbia, and a long career of teaching and research at the New School for Social Research in New York. During that time, he was always on the heterodox edge of the discipline, and concerned with abstract theory, which explains why his work never found the same recognition in a society that otherwise rushes to embrace high-achievers with even the most ephemeral of connections to Pakistan. However, that biography — from Karachi to a position of heterodox criticality in American academic life — carries a relevant resonance right now. In the face of geopolitical and economic reordering, the questions of whose economics we use, the viability of its long-held orthodoxies, and what they are actually deployed for, matter more urgently in Pakistan and the world at large than at any point in the recent past. It is then truly unfortunate that we have lost one of the clearest thinkers available on such questions. The writer teaches politics and sociology at Lums.
