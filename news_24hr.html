Discussions on terrorism

WHILE visiting the British parliament a few years ago, I was struck by the tradition —observed by prime ministers — of paying homage and expressing gratitude at the start of the weekly question time session to British armed forces personnel who had been killed or injured in action. It was the time of the Afghan war and British troops were stationed in Afghanistan. I thought it was a great tradition that reminded everyone of the sacrifices made by the soldiers and sent a powerful message to the troops that their countrymen had the highest regard for them and their sacrifices. Although Pakistan follows the British parliamentary system, almost all Pakistani prime ministers have avoided personally answering questions in parliament. As prime minister, Yousuf Raza Gilani used to answer many supplementary questions himself when he was in the House — but that was not a part of the set parliamentary procedure. Imran Khan had promised to follow this British tradition when he was prime minister and personally answer questions. However, he neither tried to change the parliamentary rules nor did he ever answer any questions in parliament. Both Houses and the provincial assemblies observe question hour but there is no parliamentary tradition of routinely acknowledging the sacrifices of the country’s shuhda and ghazis. The president, prime minister, ministers and senior civil and military officers do issue statements, and legislatures occasionally pass resolutions honouring those fighting a bloody war against terrorism; but the possibility of a weekly tribute did cross my mind. After all, hardly a day passes without hearing of security personnel — in the armed forces, paramilitaries, police and other security bodies — who have lost their lives in the line of duty. We need committees that are more active in debating national security. Almost all sources of information show that the number of violent terrorist incidents, casualties and fatalities in Pakistan have reached their highest level in a decade. In fact, 2025 was the bloodiest year since 2014. According to the South Asian Terrorism Portal, the number of violent terrorist incidents in 2025 was 1,077 with fatalities among the forces and civilians touching 1,229 and 655 respectively during the year. The data gathered for 2026 points to even higher figures when compared to the average monthly figures for 2025. For example, the number of terrorism incidents increased to a monthly average of 118, compared to 90 in 2025. Similarly, average monthly fatalities of civilian and security forces personnel in the first nine months of 2026 (up to Sept 21) have increased to 74 from 55 last year and to 114 from 102, respectively. These figures indicate a steep spike in attacks, particularly carried out by two major terrorist groups that the state aptly refers to as Fitna al-Khawarij and Fitna al-Hindustan. We do not see the level of concern and debate within democratic institutions that is warranted by the rise in acts of terrorism. We have four key standing committees relating to the issue in parliament — separate Senate and National Assembly standing committees on defence and two other committees of the Senate and National Assembly on the interior. In addition, each of the four provincial assemblies has a standing committee on home affairs. Whatever little record is available on these legislatures’ official websites does not indicate that the committees have been often and actively debating the rising incidence of terrorism in the country — especially in KP and Balochistan —in detail, which should have been done in view of the situation. The plenaries of the six national and provincial legislatures have debated the issue on and off, and passed resolutions too. I am sure that more frequent discussions in the legislative bodies would not only contribute to finding more effective ways in which the menace of terrorism can be combated at the people’s level, but the attention given by the legislators to this issue would further boost security personnel’s morale. In 2019, the Speaker of the National Assembly had constituted a joint (National Assembly and Senate) committee on national security under his own chairmanship and included the senior-most parliamentarians and ministers. But since the last election in 2024, the committee has apparently ceased to exist. It doesn’t necessarily mean that we need a parliamentary committee on national security while the committees on defence and interior already exist. However, what we need are committees that are more active in debating national security. The public should be confident that national security issues are being discussed and timely decisions being made at the appropriate forum. In this context, it is difficult to understand the relatively inactive state of the National Security Committee, which was created in 2013. The Committee, which brings together the top civil and military leadership under the chairmanship of the prime minister, has not met even once during the current calendar year. Given the prevailing circumstances and the mandate of the NSC, it should meet more regularly, preferably on a monthly basis. Just for comparison, Britain’s National Security Council meets every week under the prime minister although the UK has far fewer security issues than Pakistan. It is hoped that the National Counter Terrorism Authority — a very important body especially in the present context — will not only become more active under its new national coordinator Maj-Gen Faisal Naseer but will also attempt to raise its public profile by, for example, sharing at least a sanitised version of the progress on the implementation of the National Action Plan with the people, their representatives and the media. What Netanyahu’s ‘welcome’ tells us about young America

POLICE detain US actress Susan Sarandon and other pro-Palestinian demonstrators near UN headquarters in New York, ahead of the Israeli prime minister’s address to the UN General Assembly.—AFP WASHINGTON: The most striking feature of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s appearance in New York this week was not simply the hundreds of protesters outside the United Nations, or the diplomats who walked out when he began speaking; it was the people standing outside. They included Palestini­ans and other Muslims, but also Jews, Christians, secular Americans, professors, artists and young activists, who all said they could no longer remain silent about the destruction in Gaza. Something fundamental appears to be changing in America. Younger Americans increasingly view Israel and the Palestinians differently from their parents and grandparents. A Pew Research Center survey conducted in May found that only 18pc of Americans aged 18 to 29 had a favourable view of the Israeli government, compared with 47pc among those aged 65 and older. By contrast, 58pc of the youngest Americans had a favourable view of Palestinians, compared with 47pc among those 65 and older. Devastating images from Gaza are reshaping young people’s views of Israel However, only 13pc of young adults view Hamas favourably. That distinction is important because the polling shows that many younger Americans increasingly distinguish between Palestinians and Hamas, while becoming more critical of the Israeli government. Shibley Telhami, a University of Maryland political scientist, notes that the images confronting younger Americans are vastly different from those that shaped earlier generations. Older Americans came of age with memories of Israel’s creation after Holocaust and its early wars. Younger Americans have grown up watching Gaza on their phones — dead children, displaced families and destroyed neighbourhoods. For many, Gaza has become a formative political experience. The shift is also visible among American Jews. Pew found in 2026 that 83pc of Jewish Americans viewed the Israeli people favourably, while 47pc expr­essed a favourable view of the Israeli government. The relationship between Israel, its government and American Jewish identity is being debated more openly. That debate was visible on the streets of New York. Manijeh Moradian, a professor of Iranian and Jewish heritage, told reporters the destruction of Gaza was being carried out “in our name, with our tax dollars” and that people had a responsibility to speak out. Jewish organisations also held demonstrations near the UN. Groups including T’ruah, J Street, the New Israel Fund, the New York Jewish Agenda and Partners for Progressive Israel criticised Netanyahu’s government. At another protest organised by Jewish Voice for Peace, police arrested more than 100 people. Some protesters identify with liberal Zionism; others reject Zionism entirely. But they share opposition to Netanyahu’s government and the conflict in Gaza. Inside the UN, dozens of delegations walked out as Netanyahu began speaking. The prime minister defended Israel’s military campaign, rejected allegations of genocide and accused his critics of hypocrisy. He also turned his attention to New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani, whom he accused of antisemitism and spreading falsehoods. Mamdani responded after the speech, accusing Netanyahu of repeating “baseless lies meant to sanitise his genocide of Palestinians”. He added that Netanyahu remained the subject of an International Criminal Court arrest warrant alleging war crimes and crimes against humanity. Shortly before Netanyahu’s speech, Mamdani planted an olive tree at Gracie Mansion with Palestinian families from Gaza. “May peace be our future,” he wrote alongside a video of the ceremony. The gesture, occurring as Netanyahu addressed the UN and attacked him by name, offered a starkly different image of how the Gaza tragedy was being remembered in New York. The exchange illustrated a larger change. Israel’s relationship with America is no longer debated only in terms of its security or right to exist. Increasingly, especially among younger Americans, the question is what Israel is doing now. While the generational divide is not absolute and attitudes remain sharply divided by party and ideology, the age gap is clear. For a generation growing up with Gaza on its screens, Israel is being judged not primarily by the history that shaped their parents’ views, but by the images and events shaping their own lives. Rs16bn bank-funded remittance reward scheme unveiled

• Rs4bn to be distributed among 2,521 winners in each quarterly draw • Each raffle to award Rs100m top prize; twenty Rs25m, 100 Rs10m and 2,400 Rs1m prizes • Beneficiaries receiving at least $100 in each of three months qualify automatically • First draw on Jan 15 next year for remittances received from Oct 1 to Dec 31 • Cash-over-counter, RDA remittances excluded from scheme KARACHI: State Bank Governor Jameel Ahmad on Friday launched the Pasban Remittance Reward Scheme, under which the banking industry will offer Rs16 billion in annual cash prizes to recipients of home remittances sent through formal channels, with no cost to the national exchequer. The scheme, launched under the auspices of the Pakistan Banks Association (PBA) and developed under the patronage of the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP), is aimed at encouraging overseas Pakistanis to send money through formal banking channels. It will distribute Rs4bn in prizes every quarter to 2,521 winners, or more than 10,000 winners annually. Each quarterly draw will offer one top prize of Rs100 million, 20 prizes of Rs25m each, 100 prizes of Rs10m and 2,400 prizes of Rs1m. The scheme rewards the person receiving the remittance in Pakistan instead of the sender abroad. Partici­pation is automatic and free, with no application, registration, ticket purchase or minimum-balance requirement. To qualify, a beneficiary must receive home remittances equivalent to at least $100 into an eligible bank account or wallet in each of the three months of a quarter. The number of entries increases with the value of each qualifying remittance. For example, a $260 remittance earns three entries, while $1,000 earns 10. There is no upper limit on the number of entries that an eligible account can earn. However, cash collected over the counter will not qualify, as the remittance must be credited directly to an eligible account or wallet. Roshan Digital Account inflows and remittances credited to foreign-currency accounts are also excluded. The first qualifying period will run from Oct 1 to Dec 31, 2026, and the first draw is scheduled for Jan 15, 2027. Prizes other than the top award will be distributed through regional pools, with 50 per cent allocated to remittances originating from the Gulf Cooperation Council countries, 15pc each to the United Kingdom and Europe, and 10pc each to North America and other countries. The Rs100m first prize will be open to eligible beneficiaries regardless of the country from which the remittance was sent. Speaking at the launch ceremony, the SBP governor said workers’ remittances were the backbone of millions of families, helping meet household expenses and supporting education, healthcare and investment. Mr Ahmad said Pakistan’s external account position had improved substantially and SBP’s foreign exchange rese­rves have jumped to $21.4bn from below $3bn in February 2023. He said the strengthening of foreign exchange buffers had been driven primarily by purchases from the market rather than accumulation of external debt. Meanwhile, workers’ remittances reached a record $41.6bn in FY26 compared to $21.7bn in FY19, nearly doubling over the period. Mr Ahmad said the government and SBP had over the years introduced various Home Remittance Incentive Schemes to encourage the use of formal channels. As those programmes expanded and their cost increased, the authorities began moving towards “a more sustainable, market-oriented model” funded in coordination with the banking industry. He said Pasban represented another step in that transition and would help maintain the attractiveness of formal remittance channels while recognising the contribution of overseas Pakistanis. The scheme’s terms and conditions state that Pasban builds on systems previously used for the Sohni Dharti Remittance Programme, which has been discontinued. Pasban is a separate scheme and points or rewards accumulated under Sohni Dharti will not be transferred to it. PBA Chief Executive and Secretary General Muneer Kamal said Pasban built on a remitter incentive funded by banks since July 2026, taking the banking industry’s annual commitment towards remittance-related support to close to Rs100bn. The draw process will be operated by 1LINK through an algorithm-based random selection system without manual intervention, while the entry-generation and draw systems will be independently audited. Prize money will be placed in escrow at the National Bank of Pakistan before each draw. Winners will be contacted by their banks and results will also be announced publicly without disclosing personal details. FBR sets up faceless tax audit, assessment centre in Islamabad

ISLAMABAD: The Federal Board of Revenue (FBR) has established the National Faceless Centre in Islamabad, under which audits and assessments will be handled by “faceless” wings to reduce official discretion and direct contact between tax officials and taxpayers. The initiative has been taken under Pakistan’s New Tax Operating Model and was approved by the government in principle in June. The centralised and faceless tax model is similar to systems used in the UK, Australia, the Netherlands, Singapore and India. It is designed to eliminate physical contact between tax authorities and taxpayers to prevent corruption. The decision to establish the National Faceless Centre was taken the Board in Council — a body headed by the FBR chairperson — in its meeting on Friday, an FBR statement said. It described NFC as a major reform that “changes how tax audits and assessments are carried out in Pakistan”. Until now, a taxpayer whose return was picked for audit had to deal with a particular officer in a particular office, often in person. Under the new system, that contact ends. Cases will be selected by a computerised, risk-based system rather than by any officer. Each case will then be assigned automatically to an officer who may be sitting anywhere in the country. The taxpayer will not know who the officer is, and the officer will have no say in which case comes to them. Every case will pass through three separate hands. One officer will conduct the audit, a second will make the assessment, and a third will review the work for quality before any order is issued. No single officer will control a taxpayer’s case from start to finish. All notices, replies and hearings will take place electronically through FBR’s IRIS system, and where the law requires a physical verification or recovery, it will be carried out by a separate field team, the statement detailed.
