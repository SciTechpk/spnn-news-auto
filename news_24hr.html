Cashless divide

IMAGINE a woman in Islamabad arriving at a government office to pay a fee. She has the money, but no bank account and no smartphone of her own. The counter no longer accepts cash. The official points her towards a QR code. She asks someone nearby to make the payment for her. The payment goes through, but she has had to depend on a stranger to make it. This is a hypothetical situation, but it captures a problem beneath the government’s plan to make Islamabad cashless by the end of December. Do people have the means to participate? And what happens to those who do not? There are good reasons to encourage digital payments. They can make transactions easier to trace, reduce delays and help bring economic activity into the formal system. Raast and other payment infrastructure can make this possible at a scale that cash cannot. But encouraging digital payments is different from withdrawing cash before people have a workable alternative. That distinction matters. The Pakistan Bureau of Statistics’ Household Integrated Economic Survey 2024-25 puts the gap in perspective. Internet use among individuals stands at 57 per cent, falling to 49pc in rural areas, compared with 69pc in urban areas. Only half the population owns a mobile phone. Ownership is 69pc among men and 39pc among women. These figures do not tell us how many people can use a banking application, but they do show how uneven access to the basic tools of a cashless economy remains. People should not be turned away from a government office because they cannot make a digital payment. The gap is even wider when we look at access to financial services. According to the World Bank’s Global Findex 2025, based on 2024 data, only 12pc of women in Pakistan have an account, compared with 42pc of men. Digital payment use stands at 13pc for women and 40pc for men. Among the poo­r­­est 40pc of adults, only 9pc made digital paym­e­nts, compared with 33pc among the wealthiest 60pc. For someone with a bank account, a smartphone and reliable internet, cashless payments may simply be more convenient. Someone paying in cash, living on an irregular income, sharing a phone or unfamiliar with digital interfaces may experience the same change as an additional expense. They may have to travel to an agent, pay a fee or ask someone else to make the transaction. They may also be unable to pay when a system is unavailable. The fact that everyone is being asked to use the same system does not mean everyone is equally able to use it. This becomes a more serious concern when the state makes digital payment the only option. From Dec 1, Islamabad’s ICT administration and CDA departments are expected to stop accepting cash. What will happen to someone who has no bank account or cannot use a digital application? Will there be assisted payment facilities? What happens during a network outage, a failed transfer or a disputed transaction? People should not be turned away from a government office because they cannot make a digital payment. Access is only part of the issue. A cashless economy also changes the information generated by ordinary transactions. A digital payment can create a record of who paid whom, how much and when. Depending on the system, it may also be linked to a location or other identifying information. Over time, these records can reveal patterns of work, movement, consumption and association. Of course, this does not mean government departments can simply access everyone’s financial records. That depends on the legal powers they have and the arrangements governing access to the information. But as more everyday activity moves onto digital payment systems, these questions become harder to ignore. Pakistan’s wider digital transformation makes this concern more immediate. Government departments are developing systems to exchange data, while digital identity and AI-assisted services are becoming part of policy discussions. Much of the promise of these systems lies in bringing information from different sources together. But that also creates the possibility of using information beyond the reason it was originally collected. Data generated to complete a payment should not become available for unrelated profiling or decision-making simply because it is technically possible to combine datasets. This raises some basic questions. Who can access transaction data, and for what purpose? How long is it kept? What safeguards apply when banks, payment providers and public bodies are involved? Can individuals find out if their information has been misused and seek a remedy? Pakistan has sectoral financial regulations and legal protections, but it still lacks an enacted, comprehensive personal data protection law. As the amount of financial information being generated grows, the absence of clear and enforceable rules becomes harder to justify. The government should measure Cashless Islamabad by more than the number of merchants displaying QR codes. It needs to establish who is being left out and provide assisted, low-cost ways to pay. Cash or another accessible alternative should remain available where digital access cannot reasonably be assumed, particularly for essential public services. A policy deadline cannot substitute for evidence that these arrangements actually work. There should also be clear procedures for failed transactions, refunds and complaints. Access to payment data must be limited by law and purpose, with proper audit trails and independent oversight. Moving towards digital payments may help document economic activity and make transactions easier. But before making cash unavailable, the government needs to ask a more basic question: can people actually use the alternatives being offered to them? The state is changing more than the way citizens pay. It is changing the conditions on which they participate in public and economic life. That change should make participation easier, not leave people behind because of their income, gender, location or access to technology. Whose life next?

THE killing of television anchor Rabia Ahsan in Rawalpindi this month revives a question Pakistan has yet to answer: how can we better protect women from those who threaten them? In August, digital media executive Hina Javed was found murdered. Last year, the killing of teenager Sana Yousaf in Islamabad and the shooting of a couple in Balochistan for choosing each other provoked public outrage. Though circumstances differ, the deaths show women may pay with their lives for asserting their independence or defying the expectations of others. As the world observes the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women today, Pakistan must also remember those whose suffering never makes headlines. According to the Human Rights Commission of Pakistan, at least 470 women were killed in the name of so-called honour in 2025. Rights group Sahil recorded 3,172 cases of gender-based violence in the first half of 2026. These figures do not capture the abuse hidden by fear, financial dependence and pressure to protect family reputations. For many women, violence is not a lone incident but a prolonged pattern of intimidation, control and isolation, endured behind closed doors with little outside help. Each high-profile killing brings grief, condemnation and demands for justice. But outrage fades while the failures that allow violence to flourish remain. Hina Javed’s case illustrates this: nearly three months after her death, her trial has yet to begin, though police have submitted the investigation report. For women facing threats from husbands, relatives or rejected suitors, delays send a frightening message. Many hesitate to seek help, fearing that complaints will be dismissed as family disputes or that reporting abuse will provoke further retaliation. This year’s UN campaign calls for justice, protection for survivors and an end to impunity — goals that will remain elusive in Pakistan unless laws against violence are effectively enforced. Complaints must be taken seriously; repeated threats and stalking must trigger prompt police intervention; and investigations must proceed without political or family pressure. Officers must be trained to recognise escalating danger, particularly where earlier assaults, death threats or access to weapons are involved, and to connect women at risk with emergency protection rather than send them home. Women need accessible shelters, legal aid and financial support that make leaving an abusive situation a realistic option. Schools, families and community leaders must challenge the notion that women’s choices can sully someone’s ‘honour’. What happens when a bank ducks rules in Pakistan? For Deutsche Bank, perhaps nothing

In 2018, the world turned its microscope on Pakistan’s financial system. Placed on the Financial Action Task Force’s (FATF) “gray list”, the country was thrust into a frantic race to prove it could secure its financial system against the flow of illicit capital. It was a moment of national urgency, defined by rigorous audits and a desperate need to reclaim international legitimacy in a landscape shadowed by allegations of money laundering and terror financing. The decision sent shockwaves through the country’s financial system and the resulting restrictions battered the economy. Yet, even as policymakers were scrambling to bolster the defences of the nation’s financial system, vulnerabilities in the internal systems of Deutsche Bank’s Pakistan operations were complicating the fight against illicit money flows. The global banking giant’s own assessment had classified Pakistan as a “restricted” high-risk country, subjecting businesses connected to it to additional checks and limitations. An internal report cited Pakistan’s “terrorism and Taliban links” and proximity to Iran as key reasons for the classification. Bank documents, however, show a history of overlooked alerts and bypassed regulations, and in some instances, an inability to identify who was truly moving the money in this “restricted country”. These internal records are part of over 15 million internal Deutsche Bank files leaked to the journalists Josy Joseph and Khadija Sharife. The records span many of the bank’s most sensitive departments: financial crime, sanctions monitoring, internal auditing, and the units responsible for deciding whether risky clients stay or go. This report is part of Inside Deutsche Bank, a months-long investigation of the leak involving over 200 journalists from more than 50 countries. The records reviewed by Dawn do not necessarily establish that suspicious or illicit transactions passed through the bank. What they reveal, however, is that while Pakistan was under intense international scrutiny over its financial controls, one of the world’s largest banks, while operating within the territory, struggled to distinguish customers from non-customers, monitor financial transactions and implement safeguards against financial crime. The Pakistan operation Deutsche Bank has held a commercial banking license in Pakistan since 1962. It processes hundreds of millions of dollars’ worth of transactions into, out of and within the country every year. It currently employs close to 100 staff in its Karachi and Lahore branches, offering Pakistan-based clients a “wide range of corporate and investment bank products and services, including foreign exchange, fixed income sales and trading, cash management, trade finance and treasury solutions for corporates”. Over the years, the bank’s internal controls against financial crime have been found wanting in multiple jurisdictions across the world. It has paid billions of dollars in fines and settlements over interest-rate manipulation, failures to prevent money laundering and other misconduct. In Pakistan, however, its small yet significant operation has maintained an image of respectability, not once being named in any scandal or accused of involvement in financial irregularities. According to internal documents, Pakistan has been one of the most active countries in Deutsche Bank’s global trade finance business. In 2016, it ranked third by the number of letters of credit handled by the bank, behind only China and India. That year, Deutsche Bank processed an estimated €872 million in incoming funds and €255 million in outgoing funds for Pakistan. Across its global network, the bank exchanged around 187,000 incoming and 174,000 outgoing cross-border payment instructions involving Pakistan in the same period. About six in 10 were in US dollars. Locally, the bank’s operations were relatively small and largely hands-on. An internal Deutsche Bank Pakistan spreadsheet dated December 2019 shows its local teams dealt with an average of about 4,560 items a day across cash counters, cheque clearing, payment orders and electronic payments. None of these was fully automated, according to the document. The spreadsheet also recorded more than 14,000 daily reviews of transactions flagged by sanctions screening, illustrating the substantial compliance workload the bank faced alongside its routine operations. Local compliance challenges In October 2019, the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) introduced key requirements under its Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML) framework and ordered banks to implement them with immediate effect. However, after feedback from some banks on implementation challenges, the regulator extended the deadline to September 30, 2020. The framework, seen by Dawn, came on the heels of pressure from the FATF and aimed to strengthen “trade-related Anti-Money Laundering/Combating Financing of Terrorism (AML/CFT) regime and restrict possible misuse of banking channels”. It was part of a broader overhaul of processes relating to financial institutions. Yet, internal records show Deutsche Bank Pakistan failed to implement two essential safeguards months after the September 2020 deadline: a price due diligence mechanism to identify artificially inflated or undervalued trade transactions; and an automated Management Information System (MIS) to monitor suspicious activity across its vast transaction volume. “DB Pakistan Trade Finance has not fulfilled the SBP’s Trade Based Money Laundering (TBML) Framework requirements on implementation of a price due diligence mechanism and an automated MIS tool,” Khurram Amanullah, the bank’s principal auditor for Pakistan, wrote in an email on July 30, 2021, almost 10 months after the SBP’s revised deadline.
