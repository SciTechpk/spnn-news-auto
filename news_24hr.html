Pakistan becomes chair of G-24 bloc of developing nations, reaffirms pledge to fairer global financial system

Pakistan assumed the chairmanship of the Group of 24 (G-24) on Sunday, with Finance Minister Muhammad Aurangzeb expressing the country’s resolve to strengthen the collective voice of developing economies and “advancing a more responsive, fair and development-oriented international financial system”. The intergovernmental group is a coalition of developing countries established in 1971 to coordinate their positions on international monetary and financial issues, according to its website. Aurangzeb described the chairmanship as a “profound responsibility”, a handout issued by the Ministry of Finance stated, adding that he also called for “renewed international cooperation, stronger multilateral institutions and reinforced global commitments to address climate shocks, development financing gaps and global economic fragmentation”. “The future of developing economies is not peripheral to the future of the global economy. It is central to it,” the statement quoted the finance minister as saying. He further highlighted the “immense potential of emerging and developing nations as drivers of investment, innovation, entrepreneurship and global growth”. Aurangzeb outlined three strategic priorities for Pakistan under the chairmanship: placing development at the centre of global economic policymaking, strengthening multilateral frameworks, and advancing a development-oriented international financial architecture. For the first priority, the finance minister stressed the importance of macroeconomic stability, saying it must underpin investment, productivity, employment, and shared prosperity. He added that such an approach would enable “developing and low-income economies to move beyond crisis management towards sustainable growth”. Secondly, he added that under its chairmanship, Pakistan would advocate for “more equitable and fair governance, meaningful participation in decision-making and greater responsiveness of the Bretton Woods institutions to the evolving needs and realities of their members”. Thirdly, the finance minister said Pakistan would prioritise affordable and timely development financing, sustainable debt solutions, stronger global financial safety nets, climate resilience, and greater mobilisation of public and private capital. “Our task is to ensure that the immense potential of developing economies is not constrained by an international financial architecture that has yet to fully adapt to a changing world,” Aurangzeb was quoted as saying. The finance minister emphasised aligning financial stability with long-term development objectives, drawing on Pakistan’s experience with economic stabilisation, reform and resilience, the statement said. He added that this would ensure that reforming economies have the necessary financing and policy space to pursue sustainable growth. Aurangzeb further stated that Pakistan’s tenure as the chair would be guided by “G-24’s longstanding principles of consultation, consensus and collective advocacy, working closely with members across Africa, Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean, alongside the International Monetary Fund (IMF), World Bank and other international partners, to translate shared priorities into practical outcomes.” According to the statement, the finance czar acknowledged outgoing chair Nigeria’s leadership and pledged to build on the group’s progress. He further emphasised that the “effectiveness of collective leadership must be judged by tangible development outcomes”. “The true measure of our collective leadership will not simply be the strength of our voice, but the difference that voice makes,” the ministry quoted him as saying. According to the statement, Aurangzeb reaffirmed Pakistan’s commitment to advancing G-24’s mission “from collective voice to collective influence, and from collective influence to meaningful progress”. The development comes as Pakistan was named the incoming chair of the Boards of Governors of the IMF and the World Bank Group (WBG) for next year, for the first time since 1953, last month. PM hopes chairmanship will help Pakistan advocate for global financial reforms Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif welcomed the development, calling it a “significant diplomatic milestone” and went on to express hope that the chairmanship will provide Pakistan “an opportunity to play an influential role in advocating for necessary reforms within the global financial system, securing equitable financial support to address climate change, and promoting sustainable development opportunities for emerging economies”, a statement issued by his office said. He also lauded the finance minister and his team for their “tireless efforts, effective diplomacy, and professional competence”. The premier further commended the team for ensuring “impactful” participation at the recently held annual meetings of the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund (IMF), which he said helped Pakistan secure a “prominent position in the economic leadership of developing nations”.

In Spectrum: Gymkhana: colonial origins and politics of selective outrage

The British did not establish clubs in colonial India merely for cricket, golf and other recreations. They formed part of the social architecture of colonial rule, providing civil and military officials with spaces to cultivate networks, consolidate solidarity and preserve racial distinctions. The exclusion of Indians from establishments reserved for Europeans reflected the belief that political domination conferred social superiority. This history deserves unequivocal condemnation. Yet condemning an institution’s colonial origins does not automatically invalidate its subsequent existence. Institutions, like societies, can evolve, and their ability to transcend the circumstances of their creation is an important measure of historical adaptation. Lahore Gymkhana emerged from this world of colonial exclusivity and acquired its present name on March 23, 1906. Its history, however, cannot be frozen at the moment of its inception. Partition in 1947 dismantled the political and social order that had sustained colonial institutions, yet Lahore Gymkhana survived and gradually became part of the civilian life of ‘independent’ Pakistan. An institution originally designed for a privileged foreign establishment adapted to a different national and social environment, drawing members from professional, business and salaried circles. While it remains selective and cannot be described as wholly egalitarian, its contemporary identity is no longer synonymous with the racial exclusivity of its founders. It should be borne in mind that salaried individuals are among those whose incomes are taxed at source, and the revenue generated through taxation helps meet government expenditures without relying solely on loans from international institutions. Those crying foul over Gymkhana are privileged individuals who, in many cases, do not bear their fair share of the tax burden. Anyhow reverting to the transformation deserves recognition, particularly in a country where many inherited institutions changed their personnel after independence without fundamentally altering their hierarchical structures. Lahore Gymkhana’s evolution illustrates the possibility of preserving institutional continuity while accommodating a changed society. Its achievement lies not in perpetuating colonial privilege but in acquiring a different social meaning. Its history should, therefore, be assessed in the context of what it has become, rather than reduced to the circumstances of its birth. Its internal electoral system is another important dimension of this evolution. The election of office-bearers by members provides a mechanism of representation and accountability, distinguishing member-based civilian governance from systems dependent entirely on external authority or official appointment. The transparency of these elections must be judged by the electoral rules, eligibility criteria, fairness of the process and publication of results. Elections alone do not guarantee flawless governance, but neither should an established electoral system be dismissed without examining its actual functioning. Internal democracy does not make a club universally accessible, just as selective membership does not automatically make it unaccountable. These are distinct questions and should be treated as such. This combination of historical continuity, social adaptation and member-based governance makes Lahore Gymkhana a distinctive institution in Pakistan’s civic landscape. Its survival is not remarkable merely because it has endured; its significance lies in its ability to acquire a place in a society radically different from the one that produced it. This transformation should neither be romanticised nor dismissed. It deserves recognition alongside an honest examination of the institution’s remaining privileges and obligations. It is against this background that the current criticism of the club must be judged. Its centrally located land is undeniably valuable, and questions concerning its lease, construction approvals, taxation and financial obligations are legitimate. No institution should be exempt from the law, and public property must be administered transparently. But accountability is only as credible as the evidence supporting it. The manner in which a member of the provincial assembly reportedly questioned the club’s chairman raises concerns if inaccurate examples or questionable comparisons are presented as established facts. Parliamentary oversight is a serious responsibility, not political theatre. An elected representative’s authority should be reflected in the accuracy of his information and the fairness of his questions, not merely in the audacity of his confrontation. The selectivity of this indignation is equally troubling. If public land must be administered equitably, the principle must apply to all institutions, irrespective of their influence or proximity to the state. What comparable scrutiny is directed at garrison clubs, golf courses and other establishments associated with powerful constituencies? What standards govern their leases, concessions and use of public resources? These questions do not absolve Lahore Gymkhana of accountability; they expose the inconsistency of targeting a civilian institution while comparable arrangements elsewhere may escape equivalent examination. If low rents, unauthorised structures or tax irregularities are objectionable, the same standards must apply across the board. Otherwise, equality before the law becomes a slogan invoked against the accessible while the powerful remain undisturbed. The value of the club’s land inevitably raises another concern: whether scrutiny is directed towards a genuine public benefit or could create an opportunity to transfer a valuable asset from one privileged constituency to another. The land’s commercial potential makes vigilance necessary, although it does not, by itself, prove that any particular critic is motivated by land-grabbing interests. If the lease is legally defective or financially indefensible, it should be reviewed through lawful procedures. But reclaiming valuable property is not automatically an act of public service. The authorities must explain what would replace the existing arrangement, who would benefit and whether the city would gain genuinely accessible recreational space or merely another commercially valuable development. Reform must serve the public, not simply change the beneficiaries of privilege. Lahore Gymkhana should, therefore, be judged on two distinct but related grounds: the legality of its present arrangements and the significance of its institutional transformation. Its colonial origins cannot be condoned, but neither should they become a permanent indictment of an institution whose social context and governance have changed. Its lease must withstand legal and financial scrutiny; its elections should be assessed for transparency; and its evolution after Partition should be recognised without exaggerating its egalitarian character. Acknowledging institutional progress does not mean granting immunity from accountability. The real test of accountability is consistency, just as the test of institutional progress is the capacity to change. Pakistan must do more than condemn the exclusions of the Raj: it must subject contemporary privilege to equal scrutiny, regardless of who enjoys it. To single out a civilian club while leaving comparable or greater concentrations of privilege insufficiently examined is not a triumph of democratic accountability but a contradiction of its central principle. An independent society demonstrates its maturity by judging institutions impartially, recognising genuine adaptation, demanding transparency in the use of public resources and refusing to let political theatre replace evidence. Lahore Gymkhana’s colonial past should remind us of the injustice of exclusion; its subsequent evolution should remind us that institutions can change. India’s water blackmail

SINCE April 2025, when India unilaterally suspended the Indus Waters Treaty (IWT), most scholarship has treated it as a political decision by the BJP-led NDA government. While it is true that the BJP has been developing a narrative that Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi was ‘rectifying’ the ‘mistakes’ of the Nehruvian era, the decisions and developments during the past 18 months suggest that New Delhi is developing a long-term strategy to strangle Pakistan economically. India’s primary target in this strategic campaign is the Chenab river, which it is systematically converting into a long-term instrument of leverage to dictate Pakistan’s foreign policy, weaken its stance on Kashmir, and dominate bilateral relations. In the 18 months since it decided to keep the IWT in abeyance, India has aggressively leveraged this treaty vacuum to build a massive network of storage, regulation, and diversion infrastructure on the Chenab. New and accelerated hydroelectric power projects, primarily concentrated in the Chenab basin, have ballooned from a baseline capacity of 3,263 MW to 7,620.5 MW, supported by a massive financial outlay of approximately Indian rupees 414.04 billion. No single dam or diversion canal can permanently dry up a river. However, a highly synchronised, connected network of mega structures, coupled with diversion canals, can fundamentally determine who controls the timing and volume of river flow. India is trying to apply a two-pronged strategy, ie, increase storage capacity and enhance the ability to divert the Chenab’s water. India’s strategic roadmap involves five megaprojects alongside extensive local diversions. This includes Sawalkot (1,856 MW), Kirthai I & II (1,320 MW), Pakal Dul (1,000 MW), Ratle (850 MW), and Bursar Storage (800 MW). India’s infrastructure push began in May 2025, with the announcement of the tender for the Uri-II project (240 MW), followed quickly in June 2025 by the tender announcement for the Dulhasti-II project (260 MW). Activity accelerated in July 2025 with two major announcements — first, a tender for the removal of deposited sediments from the Salal Dam Reservoir, and second, the Sawalkot project (1,856 MW) tender under international competitive bidding. In March 2026, civil contracts were officially awarded for both the Uri-I stage II and Dulhasti stage-II projects, alongside the official tender announcement for the highly controversial Chenab-Beas Link Tunnel Project (Link-3). These aforementioned projects command a combined potential diversion and storage capacity of roughly 7.287 MAF. For years, New Delhi has strategically used international platforms and institutional levers to weaken Pakistan’s economic foundations. The Chenab-Beas Link Project is particularly consequential. With a reported budget of Indian rupees 23.52bn, it proposes to transfer water from the Chandra river, a major Chenab tributary, through an 8.7-kilometre tunnel into the Beas basin and onward through a 113km canal network to eastern Indian states. The proposed diversion of 1.9 MAF annually from the Chenab basin raises serious questions about compliance with Article III of the IWT. The threat of manipulation, or the Chenab’s vulnerabilities, poses a direct risk to Pakistan’s economic survival, social stability, and national security. The Chenab directly irrigates agricultural lands across more than 11 districts of central Punjab, the country’s undisputed food basket. The report, based on data from Nasa’s Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS), collected through VIIRS instruments aboard the Suomi NPP, NOAA-20 and NOAA-21 satellites, found that 92 per cent of the detected fire spots in Pakistan’s Punjab were located on farmland and were likely associated with crop burning.
