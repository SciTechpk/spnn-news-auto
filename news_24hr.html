Islam forecast to soon exceed Christianity as world's biggest religion by middle of next decade

Islam is expected to surpass Christianity as the world’s biggest religion by the middle of the next decade, the Pew Research Centre projected on Wednesday. While Christianity has ranked as humanity’s largest religion for centuries, the global Muslim population has grown much faster and is forecast to reach 2.57 billion by 2035, compared with a projected tally of 2.52bn Christians in that year, the Pew study found. If recent trends hold, Muslims will outnumber Christians 3.6bn to 3bn worldwide by 2070, and Islam will also then account for the largest portion of the global population — 37 per cent, up from 26pc in 2020, Pew said. Christianity, meanwhile, is expected to hold steady at around 29pc, ranking second in global proportion. The Pew study also concluded that Pakistan’s Muslim population was expected to double from 227 million in 2020 to 461m in 2070. “The country will rise from having the second-largest Muslim population to the world’s largest,” it said. The study attributes the projected rise in the world’s Muslim population mostly to higher fertility rates in Muslim countries, averaging 3.1 children for each Muslim woman, compared with 2.5 children per Christian woman during the period 2020-2025. In all other religious categories combined, the birth rate in that period averaged 1.6 children per woman, below the minimum rate of 2.1 considered necessary for a generation to replace itself. While natural population growth — births exceeding deaths — remains the biggest factor in the changing face of Muslim and Christian demographics, religious switching will also play a role, said Pew, a non-partisan, Washington-based think tank. Switching faiths Based on past trends, according to Pew, both Islam and Christianity will lose more adherents from switching than they gain, but Muslims are expected to lose far fewer — 8m between 2020 and 2070, compared with 105m for Christians. The world’s overall population is projected to increase 29pc between 2020 and 2070, with Islam and Christianity the only two religious groups forecast to keep pace with or exceed that growth rate, expanding by 80pc and 30pc, respectively. Hindus are growing in absolute numbers but are expected to shrink slightly as a share of the world’s population (13.6pc in 2070), with Jews projected to follow a similar pattern (0.17pc in 2070). Buddhists, meanwhile, are forecast to decline in numbers and as a percentage of global population (2.5pc in 2070). All other faiths combined — including Baha’is, Daoists, Jains, Sikhs and followers of various folk religions — are expected to see a modest rise in numbers, while collectively slipping below 2pc of all humanity by 2070, according to Pew. Moreover, people with no religious affiliation, a category that includes those identifying as atheist or agnostic, are expected to decline sharply as a proportion of the global whole, from 24pc in 2020 to 17pc in 2070, the study found. The geographic distribution among religions is also shifting dramatically. By 2070, for instance, Christianity will rank as the largest religion in the United States but not its majority religion, and the Democratic Republic of Congo will surpass the US as the country with the greatest concentration of Christians, Pew projected. The numbers of religiously unaffiliated people are expected to grow in most countries in the coming decades, but those increases will be entirely outweighed by the dwindling population forecast for China, where most of the world’s non-believers currently reside.

M-tag, you're it: How to register your cashless payment mechanism for travelling on the highways

Picture this: you are travelling on the highway and come across a toll plaza. But instead of having to scrounge around in your pockets for cash and waste precious moments arguing about change, you can just ease through the gates and the toll is deducted automatically. That is the purpose of an M-tag. A radio frequency identification (RFID)-based solution, an M-tag is a sticker that goes on the windscreen of your vehicle. The system automatically deducts the toll fee from a prepaid account when it passes through the designated lanes. The cashless toll payment mechanism has made the process of travelling on the highways seamless. While M-tags are accepted on all designated smart motorways across the country, including the M-9 from Karachi to Hyderabad, they are currently not mandatory. Additionally, in Islamabad authorities are also using it as a security measure, with all vehicles entering the federal capital required to have an M-tag. The M-tag allows RFID-based Safe City cameras to detect the details of the vehicles and their owners as they enter and move within Islamabad. Here is a guide that breaks down how to get an M-tag for your vehicle. What do you need? There are three main things that you need to get an M-tag for your vehicle. An original and valid Computerised National Identity Card (CNIC) The original vehicle registration book to show proof of ownership A valid contact number, with the SIM registered in the name of the CNIC holder Visit a designated centre The process itself is not a complicated endeavour. Registration centres are located across major cities and motorway entry points. You can call the M-tag helpline 1313 or check the One Network app to find the nearest active centre to your location. According to the official website, you can visit centres between 9am and 5pm throughout the week, apart from Sunday. At the centre, once you hand over all your necessary documentation, officials will check the details you have provided. If everything checks out, they will issue you the M-tag sticker. According to the available information, a new M-tag sticker should cost you Rs300, while a replacement sticker should cost you Rs500. What to do with the sticker? Once you get the sticker, all you need to do is attach it to the windscreen of your vehicle. As stated earlier, the process is not overly complicated. Then you can download and open the M-tag One Network mobile application, which will allow you to add cash to your account. You can recharge your account using your debit or credit card, or even using digital wallets such as EasyPaise and JazzCash. The following video gives you a quick rundown on how to recharge your account using the mobile application; it is available on both Android and iOS platforms, ensuring accessibility for all users. With the app, you can recharge your account, check toll rates, view your travel history, and receive important updates. It is worth mentioning that an M-tag sticker expires after five years, although the account remains active. The owner of the vehicle can check the amount of money in their account by accessing the mobile app. In addition, any queries can also be answered by calling the 1313 helpline. Once the set-up is completed, you can use the M-tag lanes at toll plazas on the motorways and highways. The benefit is that commuters no longer have to interrupt their travelling at toll plazas. For commercial vehicle owners, it also has the added benefit of directly loading money onto the account for toll taxes instead of handing over physical cash to those driving their buses/trucks. Currently, there is no difference in the toll charges of vehicles that have an M-tag and those that do not. The only exceptions are the Lahore-Sialkot motorway, where the charges are 50pc higher for non-sticker vehicles, and the Swat-Chakdara motorway, where they are 75pc higher. Can you transfer an M-tag? An M-tag cannot be transferred as it is vehicle-specific; but those purchasing used vehicles can change the details to reflect their own. For that, the vehicle in question has to have crossed any toll plaza so that the account is active through the RFID reader cameras. After the activity is registered in the system, the new owner can do one of three things: call the 1313 helpline and request an ownership update; use the app to delink the M-tag from the previous owner; or visit a registration centre.

India’s move on Indus Waters Treaty is 'outlaw behaviour', Pakistan tells UN

UNITED NATIONS: Pakistan has described India’s unilateral decision to place the Indus Waters Treaty (IWT) in abeyance as “outlaw behaviour”, warning that the move violates international law, threatens the water security of more than 240 million people and risks escalating tensions between the two nuclear-armed neighbours. Speaking at a UN Security Council meeting on natural resources and conflict prevention, Pakistan’s representative said India was seeking unilateral authority to decide when the treaty applied and what conditions should govern its implementation. “That is not treaty law; it is outlaw behaviour,” First Secretary Ansar Shah said in Pakistan’s right of reply to remarks by the Indian representative. He said weaponising the waters on which more than 240 million Pakistanis depended was not a “reassessment” of the treaty but an act of coercion that created conditions conducive to conflict. Pakistan’s position was initially presented by Ambassador Usman Jadoon, Deputy Permanent Representative of Pakistan, at an Arria-formula meeting convened by Liberia on November 18 under the title “Natural Resources and Conflict: Strengthening Cooperation for Prevention and Peace”. Jadoon said natural resources should serve sustainable development, shared prosperity and peace rather than become sources of exploitation and conflict. He warned that external interference, commercial interests, historical grievances, ethnic tensions, political exclusion and weak institutions could combine to turn resource wealth into a source of instability. Expressing solidarity with African countries seeking sovereign control over their natural resources, he cited the Democratic Republic of Congo, where immense mineral wealth had sustained conflict instead of bringing prosperity to the population. He outlined three priorities for preventing resource-related conflicts: addressing underlying political and governance problems; disrupting illicit financial flows through international cooperation; and ensuring that resource-rich developing countries benefited from investment, technology transfer, local processing and skills development. Developing countries, he argued, should become equal partners in global supply chains rather than remain suppliers of raw materials. Jadoon stressed that responsibility for disrupting illicit resource flows could not rest solely with producing countries. Transit and consuming states, multinational corporations and financial institutions also had to contribute through stronger traceability mechanisms, responsible supply chains and information-sharing. Turning to shared natural resources, particularly water, he said cooperation among neighbouring states and peaceful dispute settlement were essential for regional stability. Citing UN Security Council Resolution 2788, he underlined the importance of peaceful means of resolving disputes envisaged under Article 33 of the UN Charter. These principles, he said, were particularly relevant to transboundary water resources, where respect for international law and mutually agreed arrangements was indispensable. Jadoon accused India of seeking to weaponise water against Pakistan by unilaterally placing the IWT in abeyance and threatening to stop river flows into the country. He cited the August 31, award of the Court of Arbitration in the Indus Waters dispute, which found that the treaty remained fully in force and that India continued to be bound by its obligations. The court also rejected the grounds advanced by India to justify suspending or terminating the agreement. He said no country should be allowed to use shared natural resources as instruments of coercion or political pressure in violation of international law and binding treaty obligations. India defends its position Responding to Pakistan’s intervention, India’s Permanent Representative, Ambassador Harish Parvathaneni, defended the use of existing international mechanisms for managing resource-related disputes, citing the Kimberley Process as an example of cooperation that had helped curb the trade in conflict diamonds while protecting legitimate commerce. Pakistan rejects Indian allegations Exercising his right of reply, Shah accused the Indian representative of resorting to deflection and denial rather than addressing the treaty dispute. He said the 1960 IWT contained no provision allowing either party to place it unilaterally in abeyance. Responding to India’s argument that the world had changed since the treaty was signed, Shah pointed out that the UN Charter was even older. “Does India consider that outdated as well?” he asked, accusing New Delhi of disregarding Security Council resolutions on Jammu and Kashmir. Shah alleged that Indian forces had killed more than 100,000 Kashmiris since 1989 and continued to suppress their fundamental rights and deny them self-determination. Countering India’s accusations, he accused the ruling Hindutva establishment of promoting discrimination against minorities, suppressing free speech and inciting religious hatred. Shah urged India to abandon its “hubris” and comply with international law and its obligations under treaties and Security Council resolutions. The diplomatic exchange highlighted the widening disagreement between the two neighbours over the future of the treaty, which governs the sharing of the Indus river system. Under the agreement, the eastern rivers — Ravi, Beas and Sutlej — were allocated primarily to India, while Pakistan received rights over the western rivers — Indus, Jhelum and Chenab — subject to specified uses by India.

All manner of articles have been written about whether the exchange rate needs to be devalued to keep exports competitive. Those advocating this are pointing to the so-called Real Effective Exchange Rate, or REER, which, they say, is overvalued, making Pakistani products expensive in export markets by comparison to our main export competitors. The REER is a tool used mainly by central bankers and academics to measure how a given country’s exchange rate is aligned with the inflation-adjusted exchange rates of its main trading partners. The way it works is simple. When the REER is 100 there is, in theory anyway, alignment between Pakistan and its trading partners in terms of how well the exchange rate reflects the costs of doing business in Pakistan versus its competitor countries. But if the REER rises above 100 it is considered overvalued, meaning the exchange rate no longer adequately reflects the rising price level within the country by comparison to its competitors, and keeping it at present-day levels will render exports non-competitive. The more the REER is above 100, the more overvalued the rupee is considered to be. And if it is below 100, then the exchange rate can be considered undervalued, meaning exporters are enjoying a premium in international markets by comparison to the cost of doing business that faces their competitors in other countries. Conditions that have historically called for an exchange rate devaluation are simply not there today. Now here is the strange thing. The REER has been above 100 for a number of months now, and is now far enough above its equilibrium level to potentially merit a devaluation. But none among the exporter community is demanding this. Not publicly anyway. All exporter associations, and other chambers around the country, are very vocal about the direction of interest rates, about the taxation burden, about electricity and gas pricing. But not a word about the exchange rate. So what to make of a situation where economists are saying the currency needs to depreciate to make exports competitive, but exporters themselves are making no such demand? So far their objections have not prevailed over the cabinet, though they have found sympathetic ears within government. What is striking is the silence of the key stakeholders — the auto companies themselves. Why are those whose business is directly in line to be impacted by this measure not saying anything about it, at least publicly? The answer is a little obvious: the proposed measure does not adversely impact their interests. The exporters are not asking for a devaluation and auto makers are not asking for the return of the protections behind which they grew up, because their business is not going to be as adversely or favourably impacted by the proposed measures as theory might tell us. The auto part manufacturers are a different story. They cannot sell their parts to anybody else other than the big auto makers for whom they have been vendors for many years now. Changing that would require a deep process of retooling their plant, and figuring out where auto volumes are sufficiently mature to merit retooling the plant and producing parts at industrial scale. Given that the auto industry is in the middle of shifting to electric vehicles anyway, such a process of obsolescence and adaptation to a changing market reality is perhaps coming anyway, regardless of how much protection the government extends to legacy manufacturers. Regarding the exchange rate, policymakers in Pakistan have never paid much mind to the REER in deciding the level they want for the rate. The level is usually decided more with rupee cost of external debt servicing requirements in mind, first and foremost. Adjustments to the exchange rate always come when their hand is forced by market reality, which means devaluations come when the State Bank’s liquid reserves fall below the level required to keep rampant dollarisation in check. Never have they based exchange rate decisions on export competitiveness and there is no reason to suppose they are about to change now. Conditions that have historically called for an exchange rate devaluation are simply not there today. Under these conditions, the REER is about as relevant as the daily weather forecast. There will be no devaluation in the near future, not until their hand is forced by market reality. ICJ report sees 'judicial capture' in Pakistan after 26th, 27th Constitutional Amendments

A report released by the International Commission of Jurists (ICJ) on Wednesday said that the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments led to a “systemic judicial capture” in Pakistan. The 26th Amendment, passed in October 2024, carved constitutional benches out of the Supreme Court, with their members chosen by a reconstituted Judicial Commission of Pakistan (JCP) in which judges no longer held a majority. Barely a year later, the 27th Amendment turned those benches into an entirely separate court, the Federal Constitutional Court (FCC). It also made significant changes to the judicial system, the military’s chain of command and granted immunities to government officials. Two of the SC’s senior-most judges resigned in protest the day it became law. On Wednesday, the ICJ released a report — titled ‘Judicial Capture: Independence of the Judiciary in Pakistan after the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments’. It said that after analysing the institutional framework governing higher courts following the two amendments, the judiciary in Pakistan no longer enjoyed institutional guarantees of independence required under international law and standards. “The 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments were a decisive break from the model of judicial independence that Pakistan had built after the restoration of the judiciary in 2009,” the report noted. However, it observed that the “erosion of judicial independence” was done through constitutional amendments, rather than the overt removal of judges. The report said that while the government argued that amendments were made to “check judicial overreach, reduce the backlog of cases, end the perceived politicisation of the SC’s original jurisdiction and give Parliament a role in judicial appointments comparable to that of legislatures in other democracies”, the claims did not justify “changing the judicial structure in a manner that is glaringly inconsistent with Pakistan’s obligations under international law”. It also acknowledged that the Supreme Court had “encroached into roles of Parliament and other institutions”. However, it also noted that with the 26th and 27th amendments, the Parliament failed to ensure these “deficiencies” were fixed in line with judicial independence. “Instead, the 26th and 27th Amendments have led to systemic judicial capture by other branches of the state and the dismantling of the rule of law in the country,” the ICJ stated. It was found that the ruling coalition had “exploited” the public’s grievances over the functioning of courts in a “self-serving manner” to control the judiciary. “As a result, even the highest courts of the land in Pakistan no longer serve as a check on the executive or the Parliament and their function as avenues for redress of victims of human rights violations is seriously impaired,” the report stated. Call for comprehensive review In its recommendations, the report appealed to the government to begin a comprehensive review of the amendments and their implementation in “light of international law and standards”, particularly under the framework of Pakistan’s obligations under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and by holding “transparent and broad” public consultation. It suggested that the government engage in international cooperation, including seeking technical assistance from the UN special rapporteur on the independence of judges and lawyers and inviting them for a country visit. In its recommendations to the JCP, the ICJ called on it to revise the criteria for judicial appointments in accordance with international law, standards and best practices. It further suggested: “Ensure the published criteria are the sole basis for nomination of judges to the FCC, the SC, the high courts and constitutional benches of the high courts.” It also called on the commission to establish a transparent procedure for appointments and publish reasons for each appointment that “demonstrate the published criteria are being implemented in practice”. It also called on the JCP to refrain from transferring a high court judge without their consent. In its recommendations to the Supreme Judicial Council, the ICJ said: “Define ‘inefficiency’ in a manner that is consistent with international standards to only mean serious incompetence that renders judges unfit to discharge their duties.” For the Special Parliamentary Committee, which has the power to nominate the chief justice of Pakistan, the report called for it to adopt “clear and transparent criteria and procedures” for the appointment of the SC and FCC top judges and to publish the reasons for each selection. Lastly, the report urged the FCC, the SC and the high courts to “interpret and apply the provisions of the 26th and 27th Amendments, to the greatest extent possible, in a manner consistent with the independence of the judiciary as guaranteed by Article 14 of the ICCPR”.
