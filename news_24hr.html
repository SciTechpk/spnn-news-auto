National question

IT has been 79 years since Pakistan was brought into being. Much water has passed under the proverbial bridge, but basic contradictions around national identity, resources and representation, and relations with neighbouring countries are as acute as they were in 1947. At the heart of it is the ahistorical notion that the subcontinent’s Muslims are a timeless nation with social mores and interests distinct from Hindus. East Pakistan’s secession in 1971 could have spurred a fundamental reformulation, but the state chose to reinforce this narrative. The rise of Hindutva in recent years, and the events of May 2025 in particular, have given state ideologues a new lease on life. Yet the treatment of Pakistan’s peripheries continues to provide fuel for competing national claims amongst politicised youth across numerous geographies. These voices remain at best unheard and at worst suppressed. And so, Pakistan remains beset by a fundamental paradox — a state that insists on projecting itself as a Muslim power at home and abroad even as a significant cross-section of ethnic-linguistic groups and non-Muslims assert entirely different ideological claims. This, of course, isn’t just a matter of identity. It’s also about power and resources. The modern nation-state is a constructed entity everywhere, not just in Pakistan. But in many other cases, diverse ethnic-linguistic, religious and other communities have made compromises over national identity because they have devised more or less acceptable power-sharing arrangements. Take Malaysia, for instance. Big non-Muslim Chinese and Indian minority populations that enjoy significant economic privilege have acquiesced to a political order which enshrines the supremacy of the Malay majority. Mandarin and Tamil are not national languages, but enjoy constitutional protection. The Malaysian system has many faults, including a significant Malay underclass in rural peripheries and a penchant for authoritarianism, but as messy postcolonial systems of power go, it certainly functions better than what exists in Pakistan. Indeed, Pakistan has virtually no functioning representative institutions; its unelected apparatuses of state gobble up resources in peripheral regions and dispossess the wretched of the earth with reckless abandon. Regime ideologues spew rhetoric about sardars and nawabs who deprive their people, while devising hare-brained schemes to replace existing provinces with dozens of administrative units themselves. Pakistan faces a fundamental paradox. In a nutshell, a militarised ruling class refuses to share power with even the elites of the historical peripheries, yet it struggles to keep every pliant regime intact for a few years before unceremoniously removing the incumbent bourgeois motley crew and replacing it with another. All of this carries on almost 80 years after it started on the back of geography. There remains a conviction within the establishment that palace intrigues and dumbing down of the masses are viable strategies because the US, China, Gulf kingdoms and other benefactors are invested in keeping the security-centric state afloat. In fact, the ordinary Pakistani has become weighed down by suffocating debt and a completely unproductive formal economy. Dependence on geostrategic rents coexists with the framing of our immediate neighbours as adversaries. Of course, rapacious, hate-mongering ruling regimes in India and Afghanistan do the cause of regional peace no favour. Indeed, on Aug 15, the Indian establishment will do its hyper-nationalist jig too, pretending that it is free of internal dissidents from occupied Kashmir to Nagaland in the form of cockroa­c­h­­es or whatever else. In the same vein, the state has imposed a rinse and repeat project upon long-suffering peripheries and working people in this country. Oppression and exploitation are rationalised under the guise of stamping out foreign conspiracy — almost inevitably hatched in India or Afghanistan. The ‘enemies’ within, however, have multiplied over time; this, above all, is an indictment of a decrepit system kept afloat by small-minded rulers who only desire power and wealth. All while an exceedingly young population is deprived of basic human needs and liberties. What, then, are the prospects for resolution of Pakistan’s tortured national question? Not high, but there are still many in our midst who continue the struggle for genuine decolonisation. It will be a tall order to create the conditions for it, but we need a plurinational social contract that treats all of Pakistan’s constituent peoples as equals. Resources bequeathed to us by nature must be used to meet the needs of all, including future generations. And there must be peace with our neighbours. It’s only then that the self-proclaimed guardians will be subject to the popular will. The writer teaches at Quaid-i-Azam University, Islamabad. Published in Dawn, August 14th, 2026

A nation between adversaries

PAKISTAN is currently in the global spotlight for mediating between the United States and Iran. Islamabad’s rigorous mediation efforts helped bring about a much-needed ceasefire between the US and Iran in April, which culminated in the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding in June 2026. Analysts see the current mediatory position as a new shift in Pakistan’s foreign policy priorities, with Islamabad attempting to assert itself as a middle power and a relevant strategic player in Middle Eastern geopolitics. However, a comprehensive historical analysis reveals that mediation has remained one of Pakistan’s most essential foreign policy imperatives. Pakistan has long adopted an active strategy of mediation to prevent, manage, and settle disputes in the Middle East and within its immediate western neighbourhood. Far from being a passive regional actor, Islamabad has often positioned itself as a mediator in conflicts ranging from great-power rivalries to intra-Muslim problems. This approach is based on a deliberate foreign policy calculus determined by geography, strategic alliances, and the need to protect national interests while expanding diplomatic influence beyond South Asia. The logic behind Pakistan’s mediating role The primary strategic motive behind Pakistan’s mediation diplomacy is the need to keep regional disputes from spilling over onto its own borders. Unlike great powers, which may approach mediation largely for geopolitical or ideological reasons, Pakistan’s security is immediately influenced by conflicts in its near neighbourhood. Regional conflicts have historically been accompanied by refugee flows, cross-border terrorism, interruption of trade routes, arms trafficking, narcotics smuggling, and humanitarian crises, all of which have imposed significant economic and security costs on Pakistan. For Islamabad, mediation, therefore, constitutes a preventive tactic meant to contain problems before they evolve into greater challenges to Pakistan itself. Afghanistan is a glaring example of this security rationale. For over four decades, Afghanistan’s instability has had far-reaching implications for Pakistan, including inflow of millions of refugees, increased terrorism and border insecurity, and enhanced economic burden. As a result, Pakistan expended enormous diplomatic capital in promoting the intra-Afghan peace process, as well as talks between the Taliban and the US. Islamabad’s coordination and facilitation resulted in the 2020 Doha Agreement. These efforts were primarily motivated by Islamabad’s desire to construct a relatively stable Afghanistan capable of denying space to transnational terrorist organisations and minimising fears of long-term instability along the Pakistan-Afghan border. Thus, mediation is a strategic investment in Pakistan’s domestic security. A comprehensive historical analysis reveals that mediation has remained one of Pakistan’s most essential foreign policy imperatives. A similar rationale supports Pakistan’s efforts to defuse tensions in the Middle East, particularly efforts to reduce tensions between Iran and Saudi Arabia. Pakistan shares a lengthy border with Iran while also maintaining strong strategic ties with Saudi Arabia and the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states. The signing of the Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement in 2025 has further increased Pakistan’s geopolitical stakes in the Middle Eastern security environment, as has the recent mutual defence pact with Turkiye and Saudi Arabia. Any escalation between Tehran and Riyadh could place Pakistan in a perilous strategic position. These confrontations have often exposed Islamabad to opposing geopolitical realities, as well as exacerbating local sectarian tensions. The 1990s era of sectarian violence has had a significant impact on Pakistan’s strategic thinking. Islamabad doesn’t want to allow space for another wave of sectarian violence influenced by the Middle Eastern security environment. This is why Pakistan has continuously pursued a policy of passive or active mediation between Saudi Arabia and Iran. For instance in 2016, when tensions between Saudi Arabia and Iran escalated due to the execution of a famous Saudi Shia cleric, Sheikh Nimr al-Nimr, Pakistan hurried to utilise shuttle diplomacy to defuse tensions. Pakistan’s then-prime minister Nawaz Sharif and chief of army staff Raheel Sharif advocated holding direct discussions between Saudi Arabia and Iran in Islamabad and recommended that both countries establish focal persons to keep communication lines open for dialogue. These activities were widely considered as an effective tool for reducing tensions between Saudi Arabia and Iran. Similarly, in June 2017, a Saudi-led quartet (also including the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain, and Egypt) broke diplomatic relations and implemented a rigorous land, air, and sea blockade against Qatar. Saudi Arabia put enormous pressure on Pakistan to take Riyadh’s side. Rather than supporting the blockade, Pakistan maintained its neutral posture and initiated a joint diplomatic push with Kuwait and Turkiye to ease tensions between Qatar and the Saudi-led quad. Although the attempts did not yield significant results, they did reduce pressure on Pakistan’s foreign policy. An Iranian journalist reports from a media facilitation centre during the April 2026 talks held between the US and Iran in Islamabad.—Dawn Library Similarly, following strikes on Saudi oil sites in 2019, the then-prime minister Imran Khan conducted shuttle diplomacy between Tehran and Riyadh in an effort to ease tensions and foster dialogue. Although Pakistan was not the primary architect of the ultimate Saudi-Iranian rapprochement, its diplomatic efforts highlighted Islamabad’s preference for regional de-escalation over bloc politics. More recently, Pakistan’s co-mediation with Qatar in supporting US-Iran negotiations under the Islamabad MoU demonstrated its broader goal of preventing another potentially destabilising conflict in its western neighbourhood. Secondly, Pakistan’s mediation diplomacy strengthens its strategic relevance within an increasingly competitive international system. As global politics grows more polarised, middle powers seek influence through diplomatic rather than coercive means. Pakistan positions itself as a responsible regional partner or net security stabiliser, capable of maintaining communication lines in situations where direct dialogue has become politically challenging. Islamabad believes that successful mediation in regional conflicts can boost Pakistan’s diplomatic profile by presenting it as a proactive rather than reactive actor in regional disputes. This is especially relevant in the context of global preferences to look at Pakistan through the security and counterterrorism lenses. Mediation allows Islamabad to change global perceptions by demonstrating diplomatic ability and a commitment to peaceful conflict settlement. Pakistan also wants to demonstrate its strategic value to major powers through mediation. High-profile facilitation operations, such as organising Henry Kissinger’s secret 1971 visit to Beijing, highlighted Pakistan’s ability to bridge the gap between Washington and Beijing. As of today, Beijing recognises Pakistan’s facilitation efforts, which have improved its status in Washington and Beijing. Islamabad has been consistently seeking strategic goodwill in the international community. States that successfully facilitate negotiations frequently collect diplomatic capital, which can later be used to gain political support, strengthen bilateral relations, or increase influence within multilateral institutions. Pakistan’s active participation in facilitating dialogue in Afghanistan, and encouraging regional de-escalation in the Middle East, allows it to strengthen ties with a wide range of international actors, including the US, China, Gulf states, Central Asian republics, and Muslim-majority countries. The diplomatic goodwill broadens Pakistan’s foreign policy options while reducing the likelihood of strategic isolation. Islamabad believes that an active mediatory role in regional conflicts can protect and further secure Pakistan’s long-term economic interests. Regional instability discourages foreign direct investment, interrupts trade corridors, increases international trade insurance costs, and causes large infrastructure projects to be delayed. As Pakistan strives to transform itself into a regional connectivity hub through projects like the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), peace in neighbouring regions has become an economic necessity rather than a diplomatic aspiration. Stability in Afghanistan is critical for developing overland trade with Central Asia, while lower tensions between the Gulf states and Iran can improve the security of Pakistan’s energy imports, and enhance the utility of Gwadar port. As a result, mediation indirectly helps to create an external environment that promotes economic growth and regional integration for Islamabad. Furthermore, effective diplomatic engagement can boost investors’ confidence by portraying Pakistan as a stabilising rather than destabilising regional actor. International investors often prefer regions with political certainty and amicable interstate relations. Finally, Pakistan’s mediation efforts are motivated by its overall diplomatic identity. Since independence, succeeding governments have continuously prioritised peaceful conflict resolution, negotiation, and negotiated agreements in their official foreign policy rhetoric. Individual mediation initiatives are definitely shaped by geopolitical goals, but Pakistan has often depicted itself as a country eager to provide good offices whenever conflicts endanger regional peace and international security. Whether facilitating dialogue in Afghanistan, encouraging Saudi-Iran engagement, or launching a diplomatic initiative to defuse tensions between the US and Iran, Islamabad has sought to position itself as a bridge between competing actors rather than a participant in regional rivalries. The historical record Pakistan’s diplomatic role as a successful mediator or back-channel facilitator started in late 1969 when Islamabad facilitated the US-China rapprochement. Pakistan facilitated a secret historic visit of Henry Kissinger to Beijing. The visit paved the way for US-China diplomatic and economic engagement, which dramatically reshaped Cold War geopolitics. This episode remains a typical successful example of Pakistan using its contacts to achieve a significant diplomatic outcome. In Afghanistan’s context, Pakistan was a key player in the 1988 Geneva Accords, alongside Afghanistan, the Soviet Union, and the United States. The accords ultimately resulted in the evacuation of Soviet forces from Afghanistan’s territory. This outcome is widely recognised as Pakistan’s most significant multilateral diplomatic achievement. More recent initiatives have produced mixed outcomes. Pakistan facilitated the 2015 Murree peace talks, which were the first publicly acknowledged direct engagement between the Afghan government and the Afghan Taliban. However the process eventually halted. Throughout the protracted Afghan peace process (2009-2020), Pakistan continuously promoted negotiations and backed the Doha Agreement. Islamabad’s initial coordination and efforts to bring the Afghan Taliban and the US to the negotiation table eventually resulted in a US-Taliban pact. However, Pakistan’s efforts did not result in a comprehensive intra-Afghan settlement. Limits of Pakistan’s mediation Despite Pakistan’s long history of promoting dialogue and providing good offices in regional and international conflicts, its overall success rate remains low. According to a qualitative assessment of Pakistan’s mediation record, only about one-third of its initiatives have resulted in concrete diplomatic outcomes, with the majority focusing on confidence-building, maintaining communication channels, or preventing further deterioration of warring parties’ relationships rather than achieving comprehensive conflict resolution. This lower success rate does not reflect lack of diplomatic expertise, but rather the institutional and structural limits Pakistan faces. One of the main issues is that Pakistan frequently mediates highly complex disputes. Conflicts such as the intra-Afghan conflict, external invasions in Afghanistan, Saudi-Iran tensions, Middle Eastern conflicts, and US-Iran tensions involve various stakeholders with competing strategic goals that cannot be resolved only by external mediation. Mediators can facilitate discourse, but they cannot compel opposing parties to compromise when the political costs of concessions are excessively high. As a result, Pakistan’s participation has frequently been restricted to facilitating negotiations, with ultimate decisions determined primarily by warring parties on their own political and strategic calculations. Another important restraint is Pakistan’s low diplomatic leverage in comparison to other regional and extra-regional countries. Successful mediation frequently necessitates the capacity to offer economic incentives, security guarantees, reconstruction assistance, or political assurances that motivate opposing parties to reach difficult concessions. Countries with significantly more financial resources, political power, and diplomatic networks to sustain long-term peace initiatives include the US, China, Norway, Qatar, the UAE, and Saudi Arabia. In contrast, Pakistan typically focuses on persuasion, trusted relationships, and geographical familiarity rather than economic incentives. While these assets are valuable, they are largely insufficient to resolve longstanding disputes. Pakistan’s internal security environment further complicates mediation efforts. Persistent tensions with India, ongoing instability along the Pak-Afghan border, menace of terrorism, and periodic economic crises all divert diplomatic focus and reduce the resources available for long-term international mediation. Effective mediation is a long-term process that requires consistent diplomatic engagement, technical expertise, and institutional capacity. Pakistan’s diplomatic machinery usually operates with competing internal and regional environments. This makes it difficult to maintain long-term involvement in various peace processes. Perceptions of neutrality also challenge Pakistan’s mediatory efforts in Middle Eastern conflicts. Theoretically, for mediation efforts to be successful, all parties must believe that the mediator is impartial and trustworthy. However, Pakistan’s deep strategic ties with Saudi Arabia and other Arab Gulf states often cause concern among opposing parties. For example, Pakistan’s longstanding defence and security partnership with Saudi Arabia has historically hampered its ability to be perceived as completely neutral by Iran, despite Islamabad’s consistent efforts to maintain cordial relations with both nations. Similarly, Pakistan’s extensive engagement with the Afghan Taliban has occasionally prompted segments of the former Afghan political elite and some Western policymakers to question Islamabad’s neutrality, even though Pakistan actively encouraged negotiations and political settlements in Afghanistan prior to the fall of Kabul in 2021. These perceptions, whether accurate or not, frequently undermine the warring parties’ willingness to fully embrace Pakistani mediation. The geopolitical climate further limits Pakistan’s effectiveness as a mediator. Many recent conflicts are increasingly impacted by great power competition, and leave little diplomatic space for middle powers to independently secure comprehensive settlements. The Afghan peace process, for example, brought together the strategic interests of the US, China, Russia, Iran, India, Qatar, and numerous other Gulf states. Similarly, the ongoing conflict between Iran and the US is driven by larger issues such as nuclear proliferation, sanctions, Israel’s security, insecurity of GCC states and regional proxy conflict. In such cases, Pakistan can encourage dialogue and propose confidence-building measures, but it lacks the ability to address the underlying strategic calculations that drive the conflict. Another rationale for Pakistan’s relatively low success rate is the nature of the mediation role Islamabad normally adopts. Despite Islamabad’s structural limitations, its geographic proximity, long-standing regional relationships, understanding of local political dynamics of Iran and Afghanistan, and ability to maintain engagement with mutually hostile actors such as Saudi Arabia and Iran are valuable diplomatic assets. By affirming the collective defense principle that an armed attack against one state will be treated as an attack against all three, the pact has introduced a new bout of debate on the future of the regional order in the Middle East. Early commentary has interpreted the agreement as a departure from the U.S.-led security order and a formal arrival of a “Muslim NATO,” driven by the integration of the Muslim world’s most politically influential and militarily capable states. Rather than signaling a complete geopolitical rupture, however, the agreement represents a pragmatic attempt by regional and regionally adjacent states to hedge against growing strategic uncertainty. From dependence towards regional self-help For now, the U.S. remains central to the regional security order across the Middle East. Its forward military bases, integrated air defense networks, real-time intelligence capabilities, and logistical depth cannot be replaced by any other great power. Even the three signatories to the Mecca Agreement maintain vital security ties with Washington: Saudi Arabia remains dependent on the American defense technology and seeks a civil nuclear deal with the U.S.; Turkey is a NATO member hosting U.S. nuclear weapons on its territory; and Pakistan retains long-standing military cooperation with the U.S., while coordinating closely with the U.S. Central Command across Middle East and Central Asia. The persistence of American military power has not, however, addressed growing regional uncertainty regarding the predictability of U.S. security guarantees. This uncertainty stems from the dual risks of abandonment and entrapment. Regional partners worry that Washington may fail to respond when local territory and critical infrastructure come under asymmetric attack, and they fear being dragged into broader regional conflicts driven by American strategic priorities. Besides, China, the other great power, is neither prepared nor willing to construct an alternate regional order through forward deployments, extended deterrence and maritime patrolling. Even though Beijing has expanded its diplomatic ties and trade across the Middle East, its foreign policy remains focused more on East Asia and the Western Pacific, with a hands-off approach to Middle Eastern regional security. These new political and defensive arrangements are intended to strengthen their military capacity and complicate the calculations of potential adversaries. Iranian military planners considering strikes against Saudi energy assets or urban centers must now account for the political, intelligence, and defense-industrial costs of provoking regional powers situated directly along Iran’s own frontiers. Besides, pre-pact consultations involving Egypt demonstrate that this emerging regional architecture is not confined to a single agreement or select countries, but it is a flexible arrangement of political and defensive coordination among regional states. Extending maritime security A crucial dimension of the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement is its focus on maritime security across the Western Indian Ocean, spanning from the Arabian Sea and the Strait of Hormuz to the Bab-el-Mandeb Strait and the Red Sea. Historically, Pakistan’s naval posture remained centered on the South Asian theatre, particularly coastal defence along Pakistan’s Arabian sea shoreline. However, the protection of Sea Lines of Communication (SLOCs) passing through critical Middle Eastern chokepoints has become an existential economic imperative for Islamabad, given its near-total reliance on Gulf energy shipments. By coordinating maritime domain awareness, joint patrols, and naval exercises with Saudi and Turkish naval forces, Pakistan can actively secure the maritime trade routes that support both South Asian and Gulf economies. Similarly, for Turkey, maritime security in the Red Sea and the Horn of Africa represents a critical strategic vector connecting Eastern Mediterranean commerce to broader Indo-Pacific trade. Ankara’s expanding commercial investments, its naval presence in Somalia, and its export-oriented defense sector make maritime security in the Red Sea a direct national interest. The Mecca Agreement synthesizes the complementary naval capabilities of the three signatories: Pakistani naval manpower and operational experience in the Arabian Sea, Turkish naval technology and warship manufacturing, and Saudi Arabian coastal infrastructure and logistical depth. By injecting ambiguity into the agreement, the signatories project collective solidarity while preserving the operational freedom of each government to determine its specific response. This enables Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan to mitigate the risk of abandonment without exposing themselves to entrapment in one another’s peripheral conflicts. An adversary contemplating aggression against one signatory state must now assess whether the action will provoke wider political and military responses, ranging from diplomatic pressure and intelligence sharing to joint maritime operations and direct military deployments. This political association fundamentally alters regional calculations, transforming what was previously treated as a bilateral confrontation into a multi-state risk for Iran, Israel, and regional non-state actors. However, defensive intentions do not guarantee that others will interpret the pact as defensive. Iran may view the pact as an attempt at containment. Israel may view the combination of Saudi resources, Turkish military technology and Pakistani military prowess with growing concern. India will inevitably assess the agreement through the lens of its rivalry with Pakistan. Ultimately, the success of the agreement will depend on whether it can increase the costs of aggression and stabilize the regional order without triggering counter-alignments or an intensified regional arms competition. Pakistan Army major general appointed new force commander of UN mission in Western Sahara

Pakistan Army Major General Jawwad Ahmed Qazi has been appointed the new force commander of the United Nations Mission for the Referendum in Western Sahara (Minurso), the Permanent Mission of Pakistan to the UN announced on Thursday. In a social media post on X, Pakistan’s mission said, “UN Secretary General António Guterres has appointed Major Gen Qazi of Pakistan as the new force commander of Minurso.” “With over 35 years of leadership and operational experience in the Pakistan Army and UN peacekeeping, Major Gen Qazi brings extensive command, field and international experience to the mission,” the mission said. According to Pakistan’s mission, Major Gen Qazi has previously served in senior leadership roles, including command, staff and instructional positions, both in Pakistan and abroad. His previous appointments in Pakistan include general officer commanding of an infantry division, force commander in the Northern Areas and director general at the Ministry of Defence, it added. Earlier in his career, he also served with the UN Transitional Administration for Eastern Slavonia, Baranja and Western Sirmium (UNTAES) in Croatia. “Pakistan takes pride in this appointment and remains committed to supporting UN peacekeeping and international efforts for peace and stability through deployment of mission-ready contingents and leaders who have vast peacekeeping experience,” it stated. Western Sahara, a former Spanish colony largely controlled by Morocco, is the only territory on the African continent whose post-colonial status has not been settled. Morocco has long been in conflict with the Algerian-backed Polisario Front, which seeks independence for the Sahrawi people. It is claimed by the Polisario Front, which insists on the indigenous Sahrawi people’s right to a referendum on self-determination monitored by the UN. Polisario is seeking independence and says Morocco should adhere to an agreement it accepted in 1991 that provides for a UN-supervised referendum on self-determination.

Rabiul Awwal moon not sighted, Eid Miladun Nabi to fall on Aug 26

The announcement was made following a meeting of the committee in Islamabad, while a notification issued by the Ministry of Religious Affairs stated that the first of Rabiul Awwal, 1448 AH, would commence on August 15 (Saturday). Representatives from the Pakistan Meteorological Department, the Ministry of Science and Technology and other relevant institutions provided technical support. Muslims around the world celebrate the birth of Prophet Muhammad (Peace Be Upon Him), known in Arabic as the Mawlid al-Nabawi holiday, on the 12th of Rabiul Awwal — the third month of the Islamic lunar calendar. On Saturday, Chief Minister Syed Murad Ali Shah directed authorities to ensure foolproof security and uninterrupted civic facilities across the province for processions and religious gatherings on the day. Chairing a meeting with scholars from various schools of thought and senior officials, he said that it was the collective responsibility of the government to facilitate devotees in observing the sacred occasion in a peaceful, secure and clean environment. The chief minister directed the police, Rangers and district administrations to implement a coordinated security plan, ensure continuous monitoring and make effective deployments for all processions and Milad gatherings. The CM also instructed K-Electric, SSGC, the Karachi Water & Sewerage Corporation and other utility providers to ensure uninterrupted supplies of electricity, gas and water in areas around mosques, imambargahs and along procession routes.
