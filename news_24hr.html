The elephant in the room will be the inability by both countries to seal their long-awaited trade deal, experts say. Several factors, including US President Donald Trump’s tariff blitz, friction over energy imports and Washington’s warmer ties with India’s arch-rival Pakistan have made it harder for negotiators to clinch an agreement. Rubio will start his six-day overseas trip on Saturday with a visit first to Uzbekistan before arriving in India, according to the US State Department. Here are some of the challenges the trade pact has faced and the road ahead. Why has the deal been delayed? India and the United States agreed in February on the framework for an interim deal under which Washington would lower reciprocal tariffs from 25 per cent to 18pc and India would reduce duties on a range of industrial and agricultural goods. Despite months of negotiations, the agreement has not yet been finalised and signed. One reason is the legal uncertainty surrounding Trump’s tariff plans after court challenges undermined the reciprocal tariffs that formed their basis. “The February framework had offered to reduce India’s reciprocal tariff… in return for substantial Indian concessions. Once that tariff regime was struck down, the value of the US offer became unclear,” trade expert Ajay Srivastava said in a note. India is also wary of new trade measures by Washington that could impact it, including an investigation into alleged unfair practices in several countries and a new law that allows for up to 100pc tariffs on nations that purchase Russian oil. Trump on Friday signed an executive order that waived those threatened sanctions. Where do negotiations stand? Progress appears to have hit several hurdles. US ambassador to India Sergio Gor in June said negotiators were working on the “last one per cent” of the pact. More recently, the tone has turned markedly cautious. India’s Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman said last week that talks had hit a “plateau”, with little room for further concessions. Her remarks came days after US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said an announcement was not “imminent”. What have been the main sticking points? Agriculture remains a sensitive area for India, which is reluctant to expose farmers to greater foreign competition. Washington, meanwhile, wants India to buy more US energy supplies. New Delhi has also insisted that its final tariff rate should be competitive with those received by other regional manufacturing rivals such as Vietnam. Indian trade minister Piyush Goyal said this week that the first tranche of the deal was in its final stages, but added that it would not be operational until India secured an edge over its competitors. “It’s going through the final contours, the legal drafting, and we hope to be able to finalise that soon and operationalise that once we get competitive advantage over our peer countries,” he said. Japan to launch historic round-trip mission to Mars moon

Japanese scientists are preparing a mission to Mars’s moons to retrieve samples that could help unlock the origins of the solar system and life on Earth, in what aims to be the first round trip to the Martian region. The unmanned Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) probe, a collaboration with US and European space agencies as well as numerous universities and tech companies, could also help lay the groundwork for eventual human exploration. The flagship H3 rocket carrying the MMX is set to blast off from the Tanegashima Space Centre on Tuesday at 4:41 am (12:41am PKT), according to the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA). The spacecraft will arrive near Mars one year later, where it will orbit its moon Phobos for up to three years before landing and collecting at least 10 grams (0.35 ounces) of material from the surface. A rover dubbed IDEFIX — the French name for the comic hero Asterix’s dog — will first conduct surface observations aimed at ensuring a safe landing. “The sampling (from) Phobos is of course the world’s first, but it means… this is the first round trip to the Martian system,” MMX’s project manager Yasuhiro Kawakatsu told AFP. JAXA said the mission will also explore the possibility of using Phobos as a “natural space station” by establishing the requirements for a round trip to Mars — something “essential for crewed Mars exploration”. It will also observe the Red Planet’s moon Deimos, although it will not land on it, before returning to Earth. Impact or capture JAXA hopes the mission will reveal how Mars and its two moons formed, a key piece of knowledge in working out how the Solar System was born. One theory for the origin of the Martian moons — the “giant impact” hypothesis — is that they formed after a celestial body collided with Mars. The “capture” hypothesis, meanwhile, postulates that the moons are asteroids captured by Mars’s gravity. Determining which is right will help reveal “how a habitable environment for life might have arisen on the terrestrial planets”, JAXA said, referring to the four rocky worlds closest to the Sun — Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars. “MMX will shed light on the transport mechanisms that delivered water, volatiles, and organic compounds from the outer solar system to the inner rocky planet region — processes that may have played a key role in making these worlds habitable,” said Patrick Michel, a French astrophysicist working on IDEFIX. “If Phobos and Deimos are indeed remnants of these early delivery systems, they may hold clues to the origins of life itself, or, to be more correct, to the transport of the materials that were key to the emergence of life on rocky planets,” he told AFP. Deep in the ‘gravity well’ MMX is a collaboration with France’s National Centre for Space Studies (CNES), the German Aerospace Centre (DLR), Nasa, the European Space Agency (ESA), and about 20 universities and tech companies. The development of the spacecraft’s landing legs was particularly difficult, as gravity on Phobos is extremely weak, JAXA scientists say. Japan’s unmanned Smart Lander for Investigating Moon (SLIM) — the “Moon Sniper” — tipped over after touching down on Earth’s Moon in 2024, but still transmitted data and survived several two-week lunar nights. Japan also has experience in bringing back samples from space, including JAXA’s unmanned Hayabusa-2, which returned 5.4 grams of rock and dust collected from an asteroid several hundred million kilometres from Earth in 2020. One of the differences between JAXA’s past asteroid probes and the MMX mission is that this time “we have to travel very close to Mars and return”, which means escaping the planet’s heavy gravity, Kawakatsu told reporters. Phobos orbits about 6,000 kilometres above the Martian surface. “Going deep into what we call the ‘gravity well’ is difficult,” Kawakatsu said. China’s mission US and Chinese space agencies have been racing to be the first to return rock samples from Mars’s surface back to Earth. Nasa’s Perseverance rover, which has been roaming the Red Planet since 2021, has already collected samples. The mission, however, was effectively cancelled by the administration of President Donald Trump following a vote by the US Congress in January. China, meanwhile, has announced its Tianwen-3 spacecraft is scheduled for launch around 2028 and hopes to return Mars samples to Earth as early as 2031 — potentially before the MMX’s return. JAXA scientists say sand and rocks from Phobos may offer a wider variety of samples than those on the planet itself, especially if it was formed by a collision between a celestial body and Mars.

Experts term findings of first national mental health survey ‘most reliable’ Nearly 18,000 adults from all four provinces participated in the 2018-2022 study, providing the first nationally representative picture of mental health in the country. Researchers also identified significant social inequalities in mental health. There are political scientists, legal scholars and economists who have long observed that structural emergencies frequently act as catalysts for institutional growth. By fracturing rigid bureaucracies, overriding legislative inertia, regulating politicised security institutions and mobilising state capacity, there is a history of emergency conditions that have spurred economic advancement and curbed security threats. In Singapore, early post-independence security emergencies led to the establishment of streamlined administrative and judicial frameworks that laid the foundation for one of the world’s most stable, low-crime and commercially reliable legal environments. According to Austin, in India, periods of national emergency eventually led the judiciary to refine constitutional protections, such as establishing the “basic structure doctrine” to balance executive emergency authority with civil liberties.
