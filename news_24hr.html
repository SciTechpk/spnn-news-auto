Opposition walks out as Punjab Assembly passes controversial Anti-Terrorism (Amendment) Bill 2026

LAHORE: The Punjab Assembly on Monday passed the controversial Punjab Anti-Terrorism (Amendment) Bill, 2026, despite objections and a full walkout by opposition lawmakers. The session saw heated exchanges between government members and the opposition, centred on a debate between Punjab Assembly Speaker Malik Muhammad Ahmad Khan and Opposition Leader Moin Riaz Qureshi over the legal and constitutional implications of the new legislation. Addressing the house, Speaker Khan issued a ruling confirming that the bill could legally be placed on the agenda, citing Article 142 of the Constitution concerning criminal-law jurisdiction. Defending the move, the speaker referred to his personal experiences from the 1990s, recalling how terrorism claimed more than 100,000 lives in Pakistan, including those of high-profile figures such as Col (retd) Shuja Khanzada and Captain Mubeen, and led to attacks on places of worship such as Data Darbar. He argued that, in the past, terrorists often walked free due to a lack of witness protection and physical security. He noted that even democratic nations such as the US had established “faceless” or anonymous courts to deal with extreme terror threats, urging the opposition to propose constructive amendments rather than block the bill. However, Qureshi fiercely condemned the law, labelling it “draconian” and calling it a “bill to crush the citizens of Punjab”. He accused the ruling government of introducing the amendment to target political rivals, particularly PTI leaders and activists, much like previous defamation and National Accountability Bureau laws. He raised concerns over a provision under which a Grade-20 designated official would have the authority to advise the chief justice of the Lahore High Court on whether cases should be classified as “special security cases”. Joining the debate, opposition lawmaker Ahmar Rashid Bhatti read out on the floor of the House an opinion column he had written for a daily newspaper, comparing the proposed law to a 1909 British colonial-era act that was opposed by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, the founder of Pakistan. He warned that allowing the identities and voices of judges, prosecutors, defence lawyers and witnesses to be concealed or altered would violate Article 10-A of the Constitution, which guarantees the fundamental right to a fair and transparent trial. Following the intense debate, opposition members chanted slogans against the bill and walked out of the hall. As they exited, lawmaker Waqas Mann pointed out a lack of quorum, but the treasury benches successfully rallied enough members to maintain the quorum. With the opposition absent, their proposed amendments were rejected, and the House voted to approve the Anti-Terrorism (Amendment) Bill, 2026. The Anti-Terrorism (Punjab Amendment) Bill 2026 The bill seeks to insert a new Section 21AAA into the Anti-Terrorism Act, 1997, creating a “special security case” mechanism for cases in which participants are considered to require extraordinary protection. Under the proposed law, a designated authority — an officer of at least BS-20 whose identity would itself remain confidential — could declare a case or a class of cases a special security case. At the authority’s request, the chief justice of the Lahore High Court would assign the case to an Anti-Terrorism Court judge. The bill also proposes that the identities of judges, public prosecutors, defence counsel, police officers, witnesses and other persons involved in such proceedings “shall not be disclosed”. Judicial orders would carry official titles instead of the judges’ names, while witnesses would be identified through codes issued by the designated authority. The proceedings could be held at secure premises or conducted through video links, including from prisons. The proposed provision would also allow the government to adopt other security measures it considers appropriate. An accused person would not be able to challenge the constitution of the tribunal, seek the recusal of a judge or effectively question the judge’s independence if the judge’s identity remained concealed. Under the existing anti-terrorism act, the trial record is required to be transmitted to the high court, while the accused is entitled to a copy of the judgment. However, keeping the record sealed with both the chief justice and the designated authority could make appellate scrutiny difficult. The proposed provision also gives the designated authority a role in selecting prosecutors. The prosecutor general would provide a panel of five public prosecutors, from which the designated authority would assign one to the case. Another contentious provision allows the regime to be applied to a “class of cases”, rather than requiring a case-by-case judicial determination. The bill’s “notwithstanding” clause, which gives the proposed provision effect over the Anti-Terrorism Act and the Code of Criminal Procedure, has also been flagged as a potential source of conflict with federal legislation and constitutional provisions.

Hague court orders India to uphold Indus Waters Treaty, suspend work on its hydropower project

Water and the IWT remain a contentious issue between India and Pakistan, following New Delhi’s unilateral abeyance of the accord in April last year — a move that was followed by a brief military conflict between the two sides in May 2025. Following India’s move, Pakistan termed any attempt to suspend its water share an “act of war”, noting the IWT had no provision for unilateral suspension. It later argued that Delhi’s action was a violation of the 1969 Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties. In its order, the PCA backed Pakistan’s stance, declaring that the IWT between the two countries remained fully in force, as India had no justification for ending or suspending the agreement. The Hague-based court issued an award on the status of the IWT “in respect of India’s decision on abeyance” and an order on Pakistan’s application dated March 4 regarding “interim measures” concerning the Ratle hydro-electric plant. The IWT allocates the eastern rivers — Ravi, Beas and Sutlej — to India, while the western rivers — Indus, Jhelum and Chenab — are largely allocated to Pakistan. The 1960 water-sharing agreement has also established mechanisms for data-sharing and dispute resolution. During the proceedings, Pakistan requested the court to address the “design of certain components of the run-of-river hydro-electric projects that India is permitted by the IWT to construct on the Indus, Jhelum, and Chenab rivers and their tributaries (“western rivers”), before those rivers flow into Pakistan”, the PCA statement said. As part of the enforcement operation in Blue Area, the CDA sealed Shaheed-i-Millat Building, State Life Building, Trade Development Authority of Pakistan Building and Beverly Centre. It added that the chairman had emphasised that no building, institution or organisation could be permitted to compromise people’s lives and safety by ignoring essential fire prevention and emergency response requirements. According to the CDA, its Fire & Emergency Services Directorate had been conducting inspections of buildings to identify deficiencies relating to fire prevention, emergency exits, firefighting arrangements, evacuation preparedness and other essential life safety measures. The Building Control Section of the CDA had been advised to rectify identified deficiencies within the prescribed timeframe, the press release said. The CDA chairman also directed relevant departments to maintain close monitoring of fire safety compliance and ensure that safety observations were followed by effective corrective measures. Central Development Working Party clears five projects worth over Rs60 billion

• Plans include Gwadar water scheme, Karachi neighbourhood project, Gilgit medical college, NDU campus and Orakzai road • Study on wastewater treatment in Gwadar ordered to prevent sewage from polluting sea ISLAMABAD: The Central Development Working Party (CDWP) has cleared five development projects involving an estimated cost of over Rs60 billion, including a multi-billion-rupee water supply project to provide water from Mirani Dam to Gwadar through a 125-kilometre pipeline. The CDWP meeting, presided over by Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal, approved three development projects with a combined estimated cost of Rs11.7bn. It recommended two other projects worth Rs48.545bn for approval by the Executive Com­mittee of the National Economic Council (Ecnec), including the procedural clearance of the Rs19.55bn Karachi Neighbourhood Impro­vement Project (KNIP), which is already nearing completion with a World Bank loan. Under the current financial rules approved by the National Economic Council (NEC), the CDWP is empowered to approve projects costing up to Rs7.5bn, while projects with higher estimated costs are approved by Ecnec on the recommendations of the CDWP after technical clearance. Based on a technical evaluation by the Planning Commission, the CDWP recommended reducing the estimated cost of the “Construction of Water Supply Scheme to Gwadar Town from Mirani Dam under Balochistan Socio-Economic Deve­lopment Projects Phase-III” by around 20 per cent to Rs23.82bn, against the Rs29bn proposed by the Balochistan government. The reduction would result in an estimated saving of Rs5.235bn without any change in the design or scope of the project. The Planning Commission rationalised the project cost by 20 per cent, exclusively under non-scheduled items, which account for approximately Rs23.4bn, or 86.7pc, of the base cost, by curtailing contractor overheads. It has, however, asked Ecnec to approve the project subject to satisfactory resolution of outstanding technical observations, particularly regarding demand figures, hydrology, confirmation of the water source, procurement strategy, land acquisition status, a self-sustainable operation and maintenance (O&M) strategy, and a realistic implementation plan. While reviewing the project, Minister Ahsan Iqbal directed the provincial government to initiate a study on wastewater treatment in Gwadar, observing that the city should not be allowed to become another Karachi, with sewage polluting the sea. He also allowed the project to proceed to the bidding stage to save time, but directed that the contract should not be awarded until the project receives approval from Ecnec. The CDWP also cleared the “Karachi Neighbourhood Impro­vement Project”, with a revised cost of Rs19.55bn, for approval by Ecnec. The project is proposed to be financed through foreign funding, including an $85 million (Rs16.7bn) World Bank loan and Rs2.8bn contributions from the Sindh government. While discussing the Karachi project, the CDWP constituted a committee comprising the Planning Commission’s member (Population), member (Infrastructure) and a representative of the Economic Affairs Division (EAD) to develop a comprehensive framework for effective monitoring, transparency and accountability of foreign-funded development projects. Of the three smaller projects, with a combined estimated cost of Rs11.714bn, the CDWP approved the establishment of a Medical and Nursing College at Gilgit at an estimated cost of Rs4.924bn. The CDWP also approved the establishment of a National Defence University (NDU) FCS Campus at Defence Complex Islamabad (DCI) at an estimated cost of Rs2.75bn. It also approved the “Construction of Road from Zera to Daboori, District Orakzai (53.4 km)” at a cost of Rs4.037bn. Lamenting that permanent employees were extremely powerful and embroiled their departments in litigation if they were ever dismissed, Mr Kamal touted his idea as the only ‘permanent solution’ to the prevailing rot in the country’s healthcare system. “Whoever performs and works should remain, everyone else should be let got. A CEO should be brought in [from the private sector] who should have full hiring-and-firing authority. They should get their annual budget approved from us and then be responsible for managing their affairs and ensuring accountability,” he said. Mr Kamal also noted that the inquiry report into the deadly fire in the gynae ward had found that only 120 seconds, or two minutes, elapsed between the first spark and the fire engulfing the entire Mother and Child Health Centre. When asked about a fire that had broken out in a Pims nursing hostel around a month ago, the health minister said he had only learnt of the incident after last week’s tragedy. He explained that at the time, a bulb in the waiting room of the nurse’s hostel had short-circuited and fell onto a sofa, which caught fire. He maintained that this incident was not reported to the ministry at any level, nor was it a major incident of the kind that would have been reported by the media. However, he maintained that the previous executive director (ED) had internally formed a committee to conduct an inquiry and provide a way forward. The report of that committee, he said, was only submitted to the ED just a day prior to last week’s tragedy. Referring to reports that he had already offered his resignation to PM Shehbaz, but was asked to hold off until the investigation into the incident concluded, Mr Kamal said the ministerial slot did not hold any meaning for him. He recalled how he had taken responsibility on the floor of the National Assembly. “When I am offering myself up for accountability, then no one should be spared… even though I am not at fault in any of this, but I do take responsibility as the minister,” he said. When asked about criticism against him and calls for his resignation – especially from the PPP – Mr Kamal regretted how the party was trying to link this to the issue of new administrative units, at a time when such a tragedy had taken place. However, Mr Kamal insisted that what transpired actually supports his viewpoint on the issue of new provinces. “If Islamabad were a separate administrative unit, with elected officials and a mayor who was directly elected by the people, they should be the ones tasked with running this hospital,” he said.
