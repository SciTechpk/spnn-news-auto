SMOKERS’ CORNER: POLITICS OF THE BIG TENT

In Europe and the United States, the post-1945 era marked a shift from ‘mass parties’ to broad ‘big-tent’ parties. Mass parties had operated on strict class lines. Democratic socialist outfits appealed to industrial workers, while conservative parties represented business elites and religious traditionalists. Big-tent politics had already taken root in North America due to its vast geography, rapid industrialisation, and diverse immigrant and Black populations. To build winning national majorities, the Democratic and Republican parties developed broad platforms, accommodating competing internal factions under a shared umbrella and a loose ideological core. This was the beginning of big-tent parties. The Democratic Party in the US, especially from the early 1930s onwards, has housed democratic socialists, progressives, social liberals and pro-business centrists, alongside its conservative Southern wing, which existed within the party till the early 1960s. The latter was mainly to bag votes from the ‘Bible belt’ states. The modern Republican Party functions as a broad umbrella encompassing social conservatives, Christian nationalists, fiscal centrists and right-wing populists. A similar evolution occurred in western Europe after World War II. According to the German jurist Otto Kirchheimer, as the post-war economic growth expanded the middle-class, most voters began to favour pragmatic moderation over class antagonism. Left-wing parties, such as the UK Labour Party and Germany’s Social Democratic Party, shifted toward a progressive centre-left stance, while centre-right parties, such as the British Conservatives and Germany’s Christian Democrats, softened their hard social moralism and elitism to capture votes from across the classes. Political scientists mostly view big-tent parties as important democratic stabilisers. By absorbing diverse economic, regional and social groups, a big-tent outfit facilitates them to negotiate and form a consensus under a single umbrella. This prevents extreme polarisation and avoids the chaotic parliamentary fragmentation typical of unstable multiparty systems that lack big-tent parties. From the Democrats and Republicans in the United States to the PPP, PML-N and PTI in Pakistan, the very diversity that makes big-tent parties electorally powerful can also become their greatest vulnerability Big-tent outfits combine diverse demands and elements, transforming societal rivalries into organised, workable governing units. The success of a big-tent party depends on how they design mechanisms to manage internal dissent. Rather than enforcing monolithic obedience, party structures use primary elections, regional quotas and factional appointments to give diverse interest groups a seat at tables that matter. This internal diplomacy allows the overarching party to remain flexible. A well-managed big-tent party can position its platform across different political cycles, moving slightly leftward or rightward to keep pace with  changing electorate moods, without suffering an electoral decline. Following the 1947 Partition, India saw a classic big-tent system emerge under the Indian National Congress (INC). Operating on a secular, centre-left platform, Congress absorbed diverse socialist, moderate and regional groups to maintain a 30-year political monopoly. As the nation’s founding party, it offered a shared national brand that co-opted multiple local political elites and bridged vast linguistic and religious divides. INC’s dominance was broken in 1977 by the Janata Party (JP). JP was a broad anti-INC coalition of secular, regional, Hindutva and left-wing groups who couldn’t find a place under INC’s tent. However, JP illustrated a common pitfall related to big-tent politics: unmanaged factional infighting. Driven by intense ideological friction and personal rivalries among its leadership, JP collapsed from within after just three years in power. When internal fractures widen in big-tent parties, the parties tend to lose their electoral appeal or fall prey to radical internal takeovers. This occurred within the US Republican Party, where the right-wing populist Maga (Make America Great Again) movement led by Donald Trump weaponised primary elections to displace the Republican Party’s dominant free market and moderate factions. The populist wing of the party then reoriented the outfit into becoming a nationalist-populist vehicle. But this transformation has introduced severe vulnerabilities, driven by infighting within the party’s populist wing, foreign policy controversies and an increasing backlash in the US against Israeli lobby groups such as the American Israel Public Affairs Committee (Aipac). Trump’s approval ratings have plummeted. The Democrats are facing their own internal tensions. Key primary victories by progressive candidates of the party have alarmed its centrist factions, who fear left-leaning Democrats could alienate moderate voters in upcoming national contests. In South Asia, apart from INC and then JP, the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) began to function as a big-tent party. Historically a Hindu nationalist party with an upper-caste base, the BJP began to widen its umbrella. It started to accommodate distinct groups: Hindutva hardliners, big business groups, populists and pragmatists absorbed from opposition parties. Hindutva remains the BJP’s ideological anchor, though, even as it increasingly applies big-tent electoral strategies. Pakistan developed a big-tent model led by the Pakistan Peoples Party (PPP) and the Pakistan Muslim League-Nawaz (PML-N). Before these, Pakistan’s founding party, the Muslim League, had successfully managed to combine a wide range of left to right groups to win enough seats in the 1946 elections in British India, which allowed it to push for a separate Muslim-majority country. But after the creation of Pakistan, friction between different groups in the League became so intense that the party fractured. The PPP, formed in 1967, shaped a centre-left core with diverse leftist, progressive, liberal and centrist groups, while the PML-N, formed in 1992, united urban pro-business cohorts, landed elites, centrists and social conservatives. In 2011, facilitated by the then military establishment, the Pakistan Tehreek-i-Insaf (PTI) expanded into a third big-tent party by absorbing traditional ‘electables’ from the two existing big-tent outfits. PTI operated as an archetypal contemporary populist party, weaving a flexible and performative core of right- and left-wing tropes around a central anti-corruption narrative and personal loyalty to its leader. This vague ideological architecture allowed urban middle-class youth, conservatives, landed elites and mainstream Islamists to coexist under one banner. However, like India’s Janata Party, PTI struggled to manage its internal frictions and rapidly unravelled once its top leader was removed from power and imprisoned. Big-tent parties remain double-edged constructs. While their capacity to combine diverse interests and build broad platforms shields democracies against fragmentation, their reliance on a conflicted ideological core and mismanaged internal conflicts leaves them vulnerable. This requires continuous maintenance. Without disciplined leadership, big-tent parties risk devolving into factional warfare, opening the door to radical takeovers or collapse when internal consensus breaks down. Maintaining a workable equilibrium remains a constant challenge. In Pakistan, big-tent parties such as the PML-N and the PPP have done remarkably well to avoid disintegration despite attempts by previous military establishments to dismantle them by exploiting their internal frictions. Pakistan’s streets witnessed similar enthusiasm just over a decade ago, when the country’s power elites sold the nation the dream of the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC). Though the Makkah Agreement is different from CPEC in essence, as the latter project was conceived as a plan for economic connectivity, the recent trilateral accord is a geopolitical initiative involving the region’s middle powers, and ordinary people have attached their hopes to it. Just as they expected CPEC to bring relief to their lives, create employment and generate economic activity, the perception now being nurtured is that the accord will bring foreign investment into the country, Saudi money, oil on deferred payments and employment for more Pakistanis in the Gulf. Waiting for a miracle to happen is an old practice of Pakistan’s power elite. Every few years, the ruling establishment constructs a new narrative of hope: that a breakthrough is just around the corner, prosperity is waiting beyond the horizon, and Pakistan need only remain patient for the miracle to unfold. This habit of looking abroad for transformation dates back to the country’s inception, when its leadership placed its hopes in the US, joined Seato and Cento, and became a Western defence and security partner alongside Iran. Pakistan’s dreams of geopolitical influence and economic gains through its defence partnership with the US have been shattered repeatedly. Yet, its power elites have repeatedly restored their optimism and embraced new partnerships, expecting external allies to increase the country’s strategic weight and generate financial inflows. The same pattern has extended from Pakistan’s relationship with the US and its Western alliances to its strategic partnership with China, the CPEC, the anticipated opening of Central Asia, and now, the emerging security architecture involving Saudi Arabia and Türkiye. One may recall that when Nawaz Sharif began his third term as prime minister in 2013, and CPEC was formally launched in 2015, it was described as a ‘game changer’. Islamabad’s streets were covered with Pakistani and Chinese flags, creating the impression that an extraordinary partnership was about to transform Pakistan’s economic destiny. China and Pakistan were and remain close strategic partners, and CPEC has delivered important infrastructure and energy projects. But more than a decade after its launch, the promised transformation remains incomplete. Pakistan continues to struggle with debt, inadequate exports, weak productivity and recurring balance-of-payments crises. It still depends on IMF programmes, financial deposits, loan rollovers and other forms of support from friendly countries, including China and Saudi Arabia. CPEC therefore demonstrates the limits of relying on external partnerships as substitutes for the difficult domestic reforms required for sustained economic transformation. He argued that the new order in Afghanistan would eliminate corruption and inspire a more just system in Pakistan. He carefully avoided using the word ‘Sharia’, perhaps because of his professional background, but his meaning was difficult to miss. It was another utopian moment. The reality moved in the opposite direction. Afghanistan’s economic and humanitarian crisis produced new displacement, while the banned TTP intensified its attacks. Relations between Islamabad and Kabul deteriorated sharply. Instead of facilitating regional connectivity, insecurity across the border became one of Pakistan’s gravest internal threats. Its significance should therefore be assessed realistically rather than burdened with promises it may never have been designed to fulfil. Partners and projects change, but the underlying psychology remains remarkably constant. Pakistanis have been conditioned to look outward for a change in their fate. Building political consensus is less dramatic than receiving a foreign dignitary. Improving governance does not produce the spectacle of flags lining Islamabad’s avenues. Makkah Agreement

THE Makkah Joint Defence Agreement, signed by Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye on Aug 7, stands out for one clause: an attack on any of the three countries is an attack on all. Coming in the wake of the recent tensions in the Middle East, this agreement has the potential to reshape the strategic layout of the region. Pakistan’s foreign ministry has described the agreement as a reflection of a “shared commitment” to further strengthen the “collective security” of the region. Understandably, the Makkah Agreement does not give the specifics of the commitments that each side has made. Such matters lie in the realm of operational details that are generally kept confidential. What is, however, clear is that it is not a military bloc. Nor is it aimed at any specific country. Since these three countries have different threat perceptions, the agreement seems to be an attempt to enhance their collective deterrence and defence capabilities to deal with any aggression. In the midst of the US-Iran tensions, Saudi Arabia came to the conclusion that the American security umbrella was not working. It started exploring multilayered security arrangements, including a strategic mutual defence agreement with Pakistan and a civil nuclear agreement with the US. In some ways, the Makkah Agreement is an upgrade on the existing Pak-Saudi defence agreement. Anchored in Turkiye’s defence manufacturing capabilities, Saudi Arabia’s financial heft and Pakistan’s trained forces and capabilities, as demonstrated in the war with India in May 2025, the agreement marks an important strategic landmark in the evolution of a new security architecture of West Asia. Beyond the three countries’ deterrence and defence needs, there are regional and global aspects to discuss. Detractors are projecting it as an anti-Iran, Sunni group. The Turkish foreign minister has stated clearly that neither Iran nor anyone else was the target of this agreement. Iran’s spokesperson also indicated that Iran has no reason to fear the agreement, as regional countries, rather than outside powers, were working on regional security. Importantly, the Makkah Agreement ensures that there would be no single regional hegemon should America decide to retreat from the area. Given that the agreement is predicated on the idea of collective security, Iran and other countries in the neighbourhood could possibly join the agreement in due course so that a durable regional security architecture is established. The accord shouldn’t be seen as a military bloc. The Makkah Agreement is also a strong signal to Israel that its ambition of establishing Greater Israel and dominating the region is over. The US strategy to use the Abraham Accords to integrate Israel into Gulf security is also no longer plausible. The three signatories have consistently called upon Israel to stop its genocide of Palestinians in Gaza and respect international law and UN resolutions calling for an independent state of Palestine based on the pre-1967 borders, with East Jerusalem as its capital. The accord reduces the US burden of protecting the Gulf states and places the region on the path of self-reliance through collective security. America has two vested interests in the region. One is Israel’s security. The second is the continuity of petro-dollars. The US-Iran war has shown the limits of Israeli regional dominance, while America will do its utmost to ensure that the pricing of oil remains in US dollars. The petro-dollar is increasingly likely to be challenged by several countries desiring to buy Iranian and other oil in non-dollar currencies. Yet, it is important for the three signatories to take the US on boa­rd on the scope of this agreement. As for China, it will be pleased with the ag­­reement as it helps expand its concept of collective security encapsulated in the Global Security Initiative. As it is, any step that seems to weaken America’s footprint in the region is a welcome development in Chinese and Russian eyes. Pakistan’s leadership has done well by consistently using its peace diplomacy to not only bring stability to the region but also shape an entirely new image of the country. At the same time, Pakistan must continue its constructive engagement with Iran and major powers. While these strategic alignments serve the interests of the three signatories and the region, it would be useful to explore a corresponding initiative of concluding a trilateral economic agreement to pool together resources for the shared interests of the three states. The ultimate message of the Makkah Agreement is that the region is secure only when every country located in it is safe and when a culture of cooperation replaces one of confrontation. Muhammad Ahmed Bhardey filed an application before the additional district and sessions judge (East) through his counsel, Khalid Mumtaz, seeking pre-arrest bail in the alleged murder case. On Thursday, the court granted interim bail to Ahmed against a surety of Rs100,000 and issued notices to the investigating officer and the prosecution for Aug 15. On Saturday, Bhardey’s lawyer contended that his client was being harassed by police and feared being arrested despite cooperating with the investigation. “My client was the business partner of the victim. He ran one shop while Raza ran the other,” the lawyer submitted, adding that the police had also included his client in the investigation. The lawyer said Bhardey had been summoned to the crime scene rather than to the police station and alleged that he had been harassed by the previous investigating officer. Jibran Nasir, the lawyer representing the deceased’s family, rejected the allegation, saying the police had merely summoned Bhardey during the investigation process. He said around 35 witnesses and other individuals had been called and questioned for several hours as part of the investigation. The court questioned why Bhardey had sought bail when he had not initially been nominated as an accused in the case. “Think about it. Do you want to pursue this bail or withdraw it?” the court asked the applicant’s counsel. The court also made it clear that Ahmed would have to join the investigation regardless of whether he remained on bail. Ahmed told the court that he appeared at the police station whenever the police called him. The court directed him not to leave the city while the investigation was under way and extended his interim bail till Aug 24. Separately, a hearing was also conducted in the main case presided over by the judicial magistrate (East). During the hearing, the newly appointed investigating officer (IO) appeared before the court and said the victim’s SIM was required to take the investigation forward. When asked how the SIM would be obtained, the officer said the police would try to secure it using the biometric verification and Computerised National Identity Cards of the victim’s mother. The investigating officer also sought more time to complete the medical and investigation reports. The court granted the IO time to submit the required documents and directed that a progress report and medical examination report be produced at the next hearing on Aug 24.
