Cricket's saliva ban to stay despite Indian skipper Shubman Gill’s call to overturn rule

The International Cricket Council has no plans to reverse the ban on applying saliva to shine the ball, despite India skipper Shubman Gill’s call to allow the practice again. Gill on Monday said the ban should be revoked to help bowlers get reverse swing, after India’s high-scoring opening Test in Wellington against New Zealand ended in a draw. However, AFP understands that the ICC has no immediate plans to relax the rule, which is treated in the same way as any other unfair method of changing the ball’s condition. Saliva can materially alter the ball when mixed with sweets, chewing gum and mints and the practice is incredibly hard to monitor and regulate. The ICC temporarily barred the practice of applying saliva as a precautionary measure during the pandemic in 2020 and two years later made it permanent. The use of saliva to shine one side of the old ball and keep the other rough helps fast bowlers get the leather ball to swing late. “I don’t understand why we haven’t been allowed to shine the ball yet – we are way past Covid times,” Gill said after the draw. “There was a rule that you can’t put saliva on the ball to make it shine. It’s been seven-eight years and I still don’t understand why they are not allowing it. “I think this is also a big factor (in bowlers struggling to get wickets).”  The tourists had declared their first innings on a mammoth 720-9 midway through the fourth day, but New Zealand survived 120 overs to finish on 239-5 at the close. The missing link

RULERS who don’t bring power closer to people via strong local bodies want us to believe that they will now bring power closer to people via more provinces. So, the false idea of making many provinces as a cure for our many ills is back with a bang. Months after it first emerged, the bang is still more noise than a clarion call of reason. I have seen three key reports against it, but none in its favour. Support — strong on rhetoric but weak on evidence — comes mainly from ministers and friendly analysts. One minister cites Afghanistan’s 34 units. That’s odd as Afghanistan helps us avoid the blushes of being last in South Asia on key governance metrics. It’s a unitary state whose units lack legislative powers and elected bodies. We have 150-plus stronger units (districts) which have elected bodies, at least as per the law. Some cite unitary France’s 18 units. They too lack legislative powers. Counting them is easier than proving they cause its progress. It has democracy and civilian sway, unlike us. These key parts of the causal chain are not cited in our French politics lessons. Rulers also ignore the proven merits of local bodies. These offer no instant cure. But with higher democracy and civilian sway present, they bring power closer to people globally. And when the two aren’t in place, nothing usually does that. But they remain our missing link between power and the powerless. Most of us don’t have elected local bodies, they being absent in Gilgit-Baltistan, Quetta, Punjab and Islamabad and now KP due to emergency rule. Even when and where they exist, they are too weak to fix problems. Provinces often delay their elections for long but dissolve them early. Elections are often held on non-party basis; local officials often lack a public mandate, as in Punjab, and seats for weak groups are often hogged by elites. Citizens have little say in what they do, little recourse when they fail and little voice in how they spend money. Uneven council sizes and missing tiers weaken them further. Local tasks are split among local bodies and many higher-level agencies. Local heads often lack effective control as vague supervisory powers allow interference from above. Weak coordination and capacity gaps further hamper them. Such split and bureaucratic control is high in Islamabad, Sindh and Punjab. The setups closest to the people have the least powers. Provinces first make voters wait for local bodies. They later make local bodies wait for cash. They only spend about five per cent of our total state funds, much lower than in many similar states. They depend heavily on provincial funds and can’t legally and practically collect much cash locally. For example, over 95pc of their funds come from such transfers in Sindh. It is often hard to see how provinces divide money across districts, but easy to see that favoured districts get more cash per head. So, the setups closest to the people have the least powers. The aim behind dividing provinces may be to make them powerless like local bodies and actually move power away from the people. What provinces have done to local bodies, the centre may now do to the provinces. So, the masses will have one more government level between them and real power.
