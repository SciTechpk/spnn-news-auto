SMOKERS’ CORNER: Serving the State Rather Than the Timeline

The history of political communication demonstrates that tools designed for campaign mobilisation often make poor instruments for governance. A strong case study in this regard is the tenure of the Pakistan Tehreek-i-Insaf (PTI) as a ruling party from 2018 till its removal in April 2022. Having risen to power through a powerful digital campaign that redefined political participation in Pakistan, the PTI government revealed a stark example of how the mechanisms that drive social media engagement are rather opposed to the requirements of effective governance. Other parties aiming to govern effectively must extract a crucial lesson from PTI’s administrative struggles as it tried to enact governance and durable policymaking. Therefore, even in the ‘digital age’, the current government must make a deliberate break from social media platforms, or limit its presence there. As was the case during the PTI regime, an active presence on digital platforms undermines governance. To understand this, one must examine the dynamics of ‘digital populism.’ A noted research on digital media and political polarisation by the American academic and media expert Silvio Waisbord highlights how platforms such as X (previously Twitter) and Facebook “reward anger, immediate emotional resonance and simplified binaries.” As Pakistan continues to navigate an intensely polarised digital political environment, the government must establish a deliberate separation between the demands of social media algorithms and the slow work of governing On the other hand, effective governance and policymaking rely on thoughtful deliberation, bureaucratic routine and consensual compromises. But in contrast, during its time in government, PTI remained trapped in what British political scientist Pippa Norris terms the “permanent campaign mode”, or what Pakistani political commentator Najam Sethi rephrased as “permanent movement mode.” Rather than transitioning from protest/electoral mobilisation to institutional policymaking, the party’s decision-makers remained fixated with 24/7 digital metrics. Policy proposals were frequently drafted and then suddenly modified and, on multiple occasions, abandoned altogether, based on trending hashtags. The party turned governance into a reactive exercise dictated by digital feedback loops. When a government places its feedback mechanisms on these platforms, it drags government policy towards obvious demonstrations of bias. For example, the PTI government’s so-called ‘anti-corruption drive’ turned accountability into a digital spectacle, using social media metrics and online performance to showcase high-profile arrests. Due to the fact that these platforms prioritise emotional outrage, the PTI government tailored its actions to instant feedback loops, pursuing retribution against political rivals to satisfy online supporters. The platform algorithm favours simplistic positioning over complex foundational reform. Actual economic stabilisation and administrative overhaul are inherently slow, unglamorous processes that do not translate well into short-form content. By prioritising immediate validation on digital platforms, PTI frequently used symbolic communications, such as digital anti-corruption and moralistic drives, in the name of reform. According to the German political theorist Jürgen Habermas, the public sphere is a space where rational discourse leads to democratic consensus. But contemporary digital spaces represent a distorted version of this sphere, dominated by echo chambers and abject polarisation. When a ruling party fails to remove itself from such an environment, political communication spirals into performative antagonism. Cabinet ministers and policymakers become content creators tasked with defending daily party narratives rather than managing their respective ministries. This results in unstable and inconsistent policy outcomes. Also, long-term planning is continually disrupted by short-term controversies generated online. The mechanics of digital populism have not faded with PTI’s removal from power in 2022. Instead, they have evolved into a permanent feature of the political arena. Since its departure from government and the subsequent legal proceedings involving its top leader, PTI has continued to deploy digital platforms to maintain a vocal base. Rather than moving toward conventional parliamentary oversight, the party operates in a state of continuous friction. Social media is leveraged not merely to question policy but to construct alternative narratives, challenge state institutions and sustain highly emotional engagement. By treating every political development as a serious crisis, it maintains pressure on traditional media and public discourse, effectively forcing the state to respond on digital terms. To govern effectively while facing a loud, digitalised opposition, the sitting government must establish a clear policy framework that insulates administrative strategy from public relations skirmishes. Governance requires a separation between economic planning and the daily outrage cycles of online platforms. The government must rely on official briefings and documented policy measures rather than permitting individual cabinet members to engage in digital disputes. The rulers’ attention must remain grounded in larger economic, political and social goals, ensuring that public administration is driven by long-term outcomes rather than algorithmic metrics. The task of managing a digital opposition demands that state institutions cultivate structured resilience against digital provocations. When opposition messaging is shaped around high-velocity digital campaigns, sitting governments are frequently baited into deploying state machinery or reactive media campaigns to control the narrative. This is an error. Attempting to match an opposition’s digital tempo dilutes the government’s focus and swings its resources toward public relations. A prudent government policy must establish clear boundaries, prohibiting civil servants and key ministries from altering policy implementation in response to social media rhetoric. Government credibility is preserved through quiet operational continuity rather than digital retaliation. A well-thought-out strategy must be designed to make sure that a communication framework is clearly differentiated from anarchic public feedback and algorithmically amplified rage. Modern digital spaces are heavily manipulated by coordinated networks and an outrage architecture that are formed to create false impressions of broad public sentiment. They are mostly manufactured. By institutionalising offline platforms for public consultation, the state can bypass algorithmic distortions. This approach deprives digital agitators of their primary leverage point: the claim that online trends represent the unvarnished voice of the populace. They often don’t. The hazard for any administration facing a hyper-mobilised digital opposition remains the temptation to mimic its tactics. Entering an algorithmic arms race leads directly to policy degradation, as government responses become short-term, erratic and reactive. The classic ‘politics-administration dichotomy’ was articulated by the early 20th century German sociologist Max Weber and he emphasised that “sustainable public administration relies on institutional durability, procedure and insulation from transient political conflicts.” Engaging in continuous digital combat ultimately validates the opposition’s preferred arena, shifting public focus away from foundational governance towards perpetual political friction. Stable governance inherently requires measured deliberations, all of which are eroded when executive focus is diverted by online trends. Long-term statecraft depends on the sitting administration’s discipline to maintain a firm boundary between state policy and online discourse, ensuring that the government serves the state rather than the timeline. ‘Disproportionate use of force deepened polarisation in AJK’

LAHORE: The Human Rights Commission of Pakistan (HRCP) has attributed the recent unrest in Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK) to longstanding political and socioeconomic grievances, institutional distrust and governance deficits, saying an increasingly confrontational state response, including the disproportionate use of force, further deepened polarisation and eroded public trust. The HRCP fact-finding report, Civic Spaces under Strain in Azad Jammu and Kashmir, (a copy available with Dawn), stated that the dispute over implementation of a 2025 agreement with the Jammu Kashmir Joint Awami Action Committee (JAAC), coupled with controversy over 12 seats reserved for Kashmiri refugees in Pakistan, had become central to the political confrontation. The report said negotiations between the government and JAAC failed on May 30, after which the government proscribed the organisation on June 5 under the AJK Anti-Terrorism Act 2014 and imposed restrictions on movement and communications, including a prolonged suspension of mobile internet services. The ensuing unrest coincided with the AJK Legislative Assembly elections, which were eventually held in phases because of security concerns. The HRCP mission, which conducted fact-finding and election observation from Aug 8 to 10 after a stakeholder consultation in Islamabad on July 31, said it received sharply conflicting accounts about the scale of violence, JAAC’s methods and the conduct of security forces. However, it found broad agreement among political and community stakeholders, including people critical of JAAC, that the underlying dispute should have been resolved through political dialogue rather than coercion. HRCP fact-finding report calls for independent investigation into deaths, injuries during protests The commission expressed particular concern at the allegations of lethal and excessive use of force against protesters. It said the AJK police chief had reported around 38 security personnel killed and 742 police personnel injured since June but the HRCP mission also received multiple accounts from families, alleging that protesters had been killed or seriously injured by the security forces. The HRCP cautioned that it could not independently establish the full scale or circumstances of civilian casualties, particularly because it was unable to access Rawalakot, one of the main centres of violence. It therefore called for an independent and impartial investigation into all deaths and serious injuries since June, including whether the use of force was lawful, necessary and proportionate. The report also raised concerns at the government’s use of anti-terrorism and preventive-detention provisions against political activists. It said at least 147 JAAC activists were initially placed on the Fourth Schedule, while a series of notifications issued between June and August reportedly brought the total number of political workers, journalists, residents and activists listed under anti-terrorism legislation to at least 157. The HRCP urged authorities to ensure due process for everyone arrested in connection with the protests, including their production before courts, access to lawyers and information about the grounds for arrest. It called for independent review of cases against protesters, activists, journalists and political workers, with cases withdrawn where individuals had been detained or prosecuted solely for peaceful political activity or lawful assembly. It also criticised restrictions on journalism and access to information, saying journalists had faced arrests, legal action, restrictions on access to affected areas and special security requirements while the prolonged communications shutdown severely constrained independent reporting and verification of deaths, injuries, arrests and alleged electoral irregularities. The report noted that journalists had reported pressure from both state authorities and JAAC supporters, creating a difficult environment for independent reporting during the unrest and elections. The communications shutdown, according to the mission, went far beyond political activity, disrupting banking, education, commerce, transport, public administration and healthcare. On the elections, the HRCP made a distinction between political boycott and physical obstruction of voters. At polling stations it visited in Bagh, it found limited evidence of systematic physical coercion by JAAC supporters. In some areas, extremely low turnout appeared to reflect voluntary boycott, political disillusionment, fear and the wider security and communications environment rather than voters being physically prevented from casting ballots. At the same time, the mission said the heavy deployment of security personnel, prolonged communications restrictions, boycott by major political actors, phased polling and postponement of seven constituencies raised wider questions about the credibility of the electoral process. 25 Pakistanis return home after release from Indian jails via Wagah border

Twentyfive Pakistani fishermen returned home on Saturday after being released from Indian jails, crossing into Pakistan through the Wagah-Attari border in Lahore, the Pakistan High Commission in New Delhi said. The prisoners crossed the border in the afternoon after completing immigration formalities and were handed over to Pakistani authorities. Pakistani mission officials facilitated the repatriation process and were present at the border. “The Government of Pakistan will continue its efforts for the release and repatriation of all Pakistani citizens detained in Indian jails,” the high commission said. Following their repatriation, the Fishermen’s Cooperative Society issued a statement congratulating the community on the release of the 25 fishermen, who it said hailed from Karachi and Thatta. “The Fishermen’s Cooperative Society (FCS) extends heartfelt congratulations to the fishing community on the release of 25 Pakistani fishermen who had been imprisoned in Indian jails,” the statement said. The FCS further expressed hope that “the released fishermen would return to their homes and families and resume their normal lives”. FCS spokesperson Ghulam Rasool Sheikh told Dawn that the 25 fishermen had been handed over to the Punjab Rangers after their repatriation. Earlier this month, in a letter addressed to the prime ministers of Pakistan and India, the Pakistan-India Peoples’ Forum for Peace and Democracy (PIPFPD), the National Fishworkers Forum (NFF-India), the Pakistan Fisherfolk Forum (PFF) and the families of imprisoned fishermen urged both governments to immediately release and repatriate all imprisoned prisoners who have completed their sentences and their nationality had been verified by their respective governments. The letter urged both governments to use the occasion of the Independence Day celebrations to demonstrate a shared commitment to humanity, justice, and the dignity of ordinary people by releasing the prisoners. On July 1, Pakistan urged India to release and repatriate 97 Pakistani prisoners who had completed their sentences. The prisoners included 33 fishermen, Foreign Office said at the time. The statement came as part of an exchange of lists between the two countries in pursuance of the Agreement on Consular Access, 2008. The agreement requires both sides to share the lists of prisoners in each other’s custody on January 1 and July 1, every year. According to the latest available figures before Saturday’s repatriation, 198 Indian fishermen remained imprisoned in Pakistan, while 53 Pakistani fishermen were in Indian prisons. Many among them had continued to languish in prison years after completing their sentences. The sentences of 10 Indian fishermen ended on July 16, 2021; 115 on March 16, 2022; 21 on November 17, 2022; 10 on January 31, 2023; and 22 on July 18, 2024.

Govt apologises for ‘temporary’ night-time loadshedding due to unavailability of RLNG

The government on Saturday apologised to consumers for electricity loadshedding during the night, attributing it to the unavailability of regasified liquefied natural gas (RLNG), and assured them that load management would be reduced “as soon as” the delayed RLNG cargoes arrived. In a statement, the Power Division spokesperson said temporary load management of 1.5 to three hours had been carried out during last night’s peak hours due to a 3,600MW shortfall resulting from the unavailability of RLNG after a cargo failed to arrive on time. The situation was compounded by a 195MW decline in generation from Mangla Dam. Furnace oil-based power plants were also brought into operation during peak hours so that the electricity demand in the country could be met during the night hours, the statement added. “This temporary load management will be reduced as soon as the RLNG cargoes arrive,” it stated. The Power Division stressed that consumers needed to “moderate their electricity usage during peak hours at night so that load management can be kept to a minimum”. “There is no load management during the day. However, in areas where load management is carried out due to losses, electricity is being supplied as per the schedule,” it added. The statement concluded with an apology for the “temporary load management during night hours due to the unavailability of RLNG”. The government had also apologised to power consumers in April for loadshedding exceeding the promised 2.25 hours, arising out of lower water availability for power generation. The US-Iran war, which began on February 28, has disrupted the supply of RLNG cargoes from Qatar via the Strait of Hormuz. This resulted in the government purchasing five expensive cargoes from the spot market in July, with the sale price of RLNG witnessing the biggest-ever increase in the commodity’s decade-long history. Consequently, the cost of RLNG-based power generation surged by a record 242 per cent to Rs47.4 per unit in July, from less than Rs14 in April. Chiefly because of the LNG factor, the power companies have sought a Rs2.52 per unit increase in fuel cost adjustment (FCA) to consumers across the country in September bills despite 73pc generation in July from cheaper domestic, predominantly zero-cost fuel sources. LNG contributed about 11pc of the total grid supply.
