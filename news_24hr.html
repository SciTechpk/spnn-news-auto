Like a 'vertical roller coaster': Simulating flydubai flight FZ1073's sudden plunge

Flydubai’s flight FZ1073, which plunged 16,000 feet in about 30 seconds before being levelled out and landing safely, would have felt like a “vertical roller coaster”, according to an aviation instructor who recreated the descent in a flight simulator for Reuters. The steep fall, after an attack on the captain by the co-pilot during a flight on Wednesday, would have put huge strain on the Boeing 737 MAX 8 aircraft — as well as the passengers and crew, said Alberto Paduanelli, owner of ALPA Simulation in Sherfield on Loddon, southern England. The co-pilot of the flydubai jet, which was bound for Israel from Dubai, attacked the pilot with an emergency crash axe, the United Arab Emirates prosecutor general said on Saturday, calling the incident a “terrorist attack”. The co-pilot was identified as Hammam al-Hammami, an Omani national, two sources with knowledge of the investigation told Reuters. UAE authorities did not immediately respond to a request for confirmation of his identity. Reuters could not independently reach al-Hammami for comment. Flydubai says the circumstances surrounding the incident are still under investigation and it cannot comment further. Oman has yet to comment on the incident. “A plunge of 16,000 feet (4,877 metres) over 30 seconds will feel like you are over on a very, very vertical roller coaster,” Paduanelli said, sitting at the controls of the simulator cockpit. “The difference is that on the roller coaster, you know what’s happening,” he said, adding that with a sudden drop “you feel the negative g-force on your body”. For passengers who are not strapped in, “Your body will literally lift from the seat”, he said. Using a simulator to recreate the incident, Paduanelli said the aircraft’s autopilot disconnected before the plunge began, causing the jet to accelerate rapidly. United States President Donald Trump has praised the Boeing jet for withstanding the severe forces put on it during the incident. The plane was levelled out after passengers overpowered the co-pilot, before a reserve crew of pilots onboard landed the plane. “It was an amazing show of precise and powerful structural planning and design,” Trump posted on Truth Social, adding that the 737 MAX “was able to withstand G Forces and Strain far beyond what it was designed and expected to do”. Boeing did not respond to a request for comment. Aircraft are designed and tested to withstand substantial aerodynamic loads, but Paduanelli said a prolonged high-speed dive can place extreme strain on an airframe. “This was nothing but a miracle that nothing was hurt apart from the captain, unfortunately, and that two pilots were onboard,” Paduanelli said. Details about al-Hammami’s past have trickled out. Royal Air Maroc told Reuters he did a three-month training with the airline in 2025 but his application to work there afterwards was rejected. Buckinghamshire New University in Britain said he did a distance-learning course in aviation management with the institution, but no pilot training. An Australian Federal Police spokesperson said on Sunday that authorities there were investigating al-Hammami’s “links to Australia”. A source briefed on the investigation said the co-pilot studied at a university in Melbourne.

The apparent contradiction lies in the difference between what inflation measures and what consumers experience when they enter the market. Headline inflation measures how quickly the overall price basket is changing. It does not show how far the general price level has already risen. The official measure is based on prices collected from different markets and cities and combined into urban, rural and national indices. Therefore, a national average may not correspond to the retail price paid by an individual consumer in a particular market. Lower inflation does not mean prices have returned to previous levels. It simply means that they are rising more slowly on average. Consumers continue to bear the cumulative impact of price increases recorded in previous years. This helps explain why easing inflation has yet to translate into a comparable reduction in the cost of living. A comparison of Pakistan Bureau of Statistics prices between October 2024 and October 2026 illustrates the extent of that accumulated burden. Several essential food and energy items remained substantially more expensive than they were two years earlier, even though headline inflation later moderated. Among the sharpest increases were those in onions, electricity, diesel and petrol. Slower inflation masks cumulative rise in essential prices over past two years These individual price movements do not, of course, represent overall inflation, which is calculated from a weighted basket of goods and services. But they help explain the disconnect between official inflation readings and what consumers encounter in retail markets. Wheat flour provides one of the clearest examples. The national average price of a 20kg flour bag increased from Rs1,835 in October 2024 to Rs2,697 in October 2026 — an increase of 47 per cent. During the 1970s and 1980s, a dramatic political shift permanently altered the geopolitical topography of the Middle East and the broader Muslim world. Islamism gained momentum as secular nationalism declined. It was a collapse accelerated by the defeat of Egypt, Syria and Jordan in the 1967 Arab-Israeli War. At the time, Western powers viewed Islamism, a right-wing ideology opposed to socialism and secular nationalism, as a useful counterweight to Soviet-backed nationalist regimes in Egypt, Syria, Iraq and, later, Libya. One of the most consequential applications of this strategy unfolded in the Israeli-occupied West Bank and Gaza. Anti-Israel Palestinian resistance was largely dominated by secular and left-leaning groups operating primarily within the Israeli-occupied territories of Palestine and neighbouring nationalist Arab states. The French scholar Jean-Pierre Filiu wrote that, in order to neutralise Palestinian resistance groups, Israel quietly nurtured the growth of local Islamist movements, specifically the Palestinian branch of the Muslim Brotherhood. This was a deliberate strategy, designed to fragment and undermine the primary face of Palestinian nationalism: Yasser Arafat’s left-leaning and secular Palestine Liberation Organisation (PLO). For Israeli strategists, the PLO’s anti-colonial rhetoric posed a serious political threat. To curb the PLO’s influence, Israeli military intelligence began to cultivate a counterweight in the shape of Sheikh Ahmed Yassin. A charismatic Palestinian cleric, Yassin had begun building a social and religious network under the Muslim Brotherhood umbrella in Gaza, culminating in the founding of al-Mujama al-Islami in 1973. The PLO viewed Yassin’s followers as “quietist fundamentalists” preoccupied with social welfare and moral purification. The PLO did not exhibit concern even when Israeli authorities permitted al-Mujama to operate unhindered. In 1979, the Israeli military administration formally recognised the al-Mujama charity, granting it permission to establish mosques, schools and medical clinics across Gaza. Israel’s relentless efforts, across decades, to weaken secular Palestinian resistance to its occupation of Palestine by backing a ‘counterweight’ ultimately backfired. Writing in The Wall Street Journal in 2009, journalist Andrew Higgins documented how Israeli forces raided Yassin’s home in 1984 following a tip-off, discovering a concealed weapons cache. Yassin was arrested and sentenced to 12 years in prison. The turning point occurred in December 1987 with the outbreak of the first Intifada, a spontaneous grassroots uprising against Israeli occupation. Fearing that the PLO would bounce back to take command of the uprising, Yassin and his inner circle transformed al-Mujama al-Islami into a militant resistance organisation. In early 1988, the group published its founding covenant under a new name: Hamas. Hamas was thus the direct byproduct of a charitable and proselytising network shaped in the 1970s by the Gaza chapter of the Muslim Brotherhood and facilitated by Israeli policy. Its original objective of drawing Palestinians toward religious piety and discrediting secular groups, evolved during the Intifada into an armed struggle. Hamas’ 1988 charter combined militant anti-Zionism with unyielding Islamism, calling for an Islamic state in Palestine and rejecting the PLO’s gradual move toward a diplomatic, two-state compromise. Even during the initial months of the Intifada, Israeli authorities hesitated to suppress Hamas with the severity applied to the PLO, hoping the group would fracture the uprising. However, as Higgins observed, when Hamas initiated armed attacks, kidnappings and suicide operations against Israeli personnel, Tel Aviv recognised the scale of its strategic error. By the time Israel outlawed Hamas and rearrested Yassin in 1989, the network that Israel helped nurture was deeply entrenched. This manoeuvre yielded a fierce adversary. A quietist religious charity group transformed into an entrenched militant movement. This policy relied on tactical coordination, work-permit allocations and economic incentives to manage Hamas, while derailing two-state diplomatic efforts.
