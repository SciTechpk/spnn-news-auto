Exchange rate & other protections

THERE is always something a little off when economists and other experts say that a given course of action is beneficial for industrial growth in the country, but those industries directly connected with that course of action or policy direction are silent on the matter. Normally, industry is very vocal about preserving its interests and either takes the lead in advocating its preferences, or at least responds to suggestions coming from other quarters. These days we are seeing this happen in two areas. One is the conversation around the exchange rate. All manner of articles have been written about whether the exchange rate needs to be devalued to keep exports competitive. Those advocating this are pointing to the so-called Real Effective Exchange Rate, or REER, which, they say, is overvalued, making Pakistani products expensive in export markets by comparison to our main export competitors. The REER is a tool used mainly by central bankers and academics to measure how a given country’s exchange rate is aligned with the inflation-adjusted exchange rates of its main trading partners. The way it works is simple. When the REER is 100 there is, in theory anyway, alignment between Pakistan and its trading partners in terms of how well the exchange rate reflects the costs of doing business in Pakistan versus its competitor countries. But if the REER rises above 100 it is considered overvalued, meaning the exchange rate no longer adequately reflects the rising price level within the country by comparison to its competitors, and keeping it at present-day levels will render exports non-competitive. The more the REER is above 100, the more overvalued the rupee is considered to be. And if it is below 100, then the exchange rate can be considered undervalued, meaning exporters are enjoying a premium in international markets by comparison to the cost of doing business that faces their competitors in other countries. Conditions that have historically called for an exchange rate devaluation are simply not there today. Now here is the strange thing. The REER has been above 100 for a number of months now, and is now far enough above its equilibrium level to potentially merit a devaluation. But none among the exporter community is demanding this. Not publicly anyway. All exporter associations, and other chambers around the country, are very vocal about the direction of interest rates, about the taxation burden, about electricity and gas pricing. But not a word about the exchange rate. So what to make of a situation where economists are saying the currency needs to depreciate to make exports competitive, but exporters themselves are making no such demand? Another example is with the auto policy that the government has been working on for many months now. A couple of years ago they brought customs duties down on imported vehicles, and are now moving to cut import tariffs further on other manufactured goods. Despite a few years of applying this trade policy, and now moving to codify the gradual elimination of protections in a new auto policy, the auto companies themselves are silent. Sharp objections are being raised by manufacturers of auto parts, organised under an umbrella grouping called Pakistan Auto and Auto Parts Manufacturers Association (PAAPAM), who rightly argue that large investments made in the manufacture of auto parts like vehicle glass, headlights, doors, bonnets, other body parts and lots more are at risk with this new policy. So far their objections have not prevailed over the cabinet, though they have found sympathetic ears within government. What is striking is the silence of the key stakeholders — the auto companies themselves. Why are those whose business is directly in line to be impacted by this measure not saying anything about it, at least publicly? The answer is a little obvious: the proposed measure does not adversely impact their interests. The exporters are not asking for a devaluation and auto makers are not asking for the return of the protections behind which they grew up, because their business is not going to be as adversely or favourably impacted by the proposed measures as theory might tell us. The auto part manufacturers are a different story. They cannot sell their parts to anybody else other than the big auto makers for whom they have been vendors for many years now. Changing that would require a deep process of retooling their plant, and figuring out where auto volumes are sufficiently mature to merit retooling the plant and producing parts at industrial scale. Given that the auto industry is in the middle of shifting to electric vehicles anyway, such a process of obsolescence and adaptation to a changing market reality is perhaps coming anyway, regardless of how much protection the government extends to legacy manufacturers. Regarding the exchange rate, policymakers in Pakistan have never paid much mind to the REER in deciding the level they want for the rate. The level is usually decided more with rupee cost of external debt servicing requirements in mind, first and foremost. Adjustments to the exchange rate always come when their hand is forced by market reality, which means devaluations come when the State Bank’s liquid reserves fall below the level required to keep rampant dollarisation in check. Never have they based exchange rate decisions on export competitiveness and there is no reason to suppose they are about to change now. Conditions that have historically called for an exchange rate devaluation are simply not there today. First, there is an abundant supply of dollars by comparison to the supply of rupees in the market, and the position is only improving. Second, there is no flight into the dollar. Third, the interbank market is virtually shut, with banks told (through silent channels) to finance their clients’ import requirements from their own inflows, while the State Bank aggressively buys these inflows at a steadily appreciating rate, to fuel its own thirst for dollars. Under these conditions, the REER is about as relevant as the daily weather forecast. There will be no devaluation in the near future, not until their hand is forced by market reality. Leveraging the solar revolution

AT COP31, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif told a high-level dialogue on electrification that “an extraordinary transition has already taken place” in Pakistan: nearly 22 gigawatts of rooftop solar in two years, “a quiet revolution by ordinary citizens seeking affordable power and a cleaner, more predictable future”. More than one in 10 motorcycles sold this year, he added, was electric. He was right to showcase it. Few countries can match that speed. Fewer can accept that their citizens paid for it. Nobody planned it: capacity payments rose whether plants ran or not, the rupee fell, and the 2023 IMF programme stopped the state hiding these costs in subsidies. Base tariffs doubled between FY22 and FY25, just as Chinese panels became cheap and loadshedding persisted. The IMF did not create the high cost of power. It made it visible, and achieved the revolution by accident, what policy had not. The dividend is large. By the estimates of Ember, CREA and Renewables First, distributed solar avoided over $12 billion in oil and gas imports by February 2026, with $6.3bn more expected this year, and about 35 million tonnes of CO2 in FY25. Counting grid losses, it supplies about a third of the country’s electricity. On farms, solar runs 52pc of 1.83m irrigation pumps, almost entirely without state help. Banks financed less than 5pc of the $14bn invested. The official response has been to contain it, with a sales tax on panels and cuts to net metering. A showcase needs a strategy. The leveraging should run on two tracks. Phase one put panels on roofs; phase two must make that power go further. The first is phase two. Phase one put panels on roofs; phase two must make that power go further. Rooftops have already displaced most of the daytime thermal power they can reach, so more panels alone bring falling returns. Three areas will win the game. Storage, with a tariff that spreads the grid’s fixed costs fairly, so surplus daytime power serves the evening peak. Transport, where petrol imports are still growing: electric motorcycles reached 13pc of sales by August, already ahead of the New Energy Vehicles Policy’s schedule. The 30pc target should move from 2030 to 2028 for automobiles, two-wheelers, rickshaws and delivery fleets first, with battery-swap and charging corridors for bikes and freight planned along the motorways now. And solar mini-grids for the 40m people still without electricity. The prize is worth the effort. Adding 3GW of distributed solar a year would lift avoided emissions to about 50m tonnes by 2030, as per Renew­ables First estimates, more than the power sector now emits. At this year’s rate, fuel import savings by then would approach $25bn. Electric bikes save less, but their savings will help poorer hou­seholds, which is where a transition survives or dies. The second track is harder. If fiscal conditionality could transform mitigation by accident, what could adaptation measures achieve by design, without waiting for grants or loans? Agriculture uses more than 93 per cent of the country’s freshwater, yet produces under $1 of output per cubic metre, against a global average near $17. Solar has made matters worse: once panels are paid for, pumping groundwater costs nothing at the margin. The rice area grew by about 30pc between 2023 and 2025, even as Punjab’s water tables fell. Flooded paddies also emit methane, so the same expansion that drains aquifers adds to emissions. Islamabad and the provinces already hold the levers. Five would do the most. First, conditional solarisation. Punjab already subsidises tubewell conversion; each should require drip or sprinkler irrigation and a flow meter. The Punjab Water Act 2019 allows new solar wells to be registered. Second, volumetric abiana. The canal water charge is so low that sugarcane and rice grow where the water cannot support them. Charging by volume and crop is a provincial power, and it raises revenue. Third, retargeted fertiliser support. IMF pressure ended most of the feedstock gas subsidy to urea plants in 2024. Blanket urea still invites overuse; the excess escapes as nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas, or leaches into groundwater. Tying the remaining support, including Kissan Card credit, to soil testing and slow-release coated urea would cut the waste and the fertiliser bill together. Fourth, alternate wetting and drying for rice. Letting paddies dry between irrigations cuts both water use and methane. Punjab and Sindh can initiate this through their extension services and Kissan/Hari cards, with a low-carbon basmati label for buyers who increasingly ask about embedded emissions. Fifth, a satellite-enforced no-burn rule. Tying Kissan Card eligibility and wheat procurement to satellite checks on residue burning, already run for smog, is an administrative change. Lahore would breathe cleaner air. None of these needs a donor. The petroleum levy brought in a record Rs1.57 trillion in FY26, and the climate support levy is budgeted at Rs50bn this year. Ring-fencing a fixed share of the first, and all of the second, would fund them without waiting on donor cycles. The provinces, which own agriculture and irrigation, ran a combined cash surplus of Rs1.45tn last year, led by Punjab; only Balochistan’s was marginal. Those surpluses are an IMF target, which brings the Fund back in. Its $1.4bn Resilience and Sustainability Facility already lists irrigation water pricing, but its water measures are hostage to influential interest groups. The two remaining reviews in 2027 can begin the shift; the successor programme should put adaptation at the centre, with these five as structural benchmarks carrying provincial timelines. The Fund’s leverage transformed Pakistan’s energy use by accident. It can now be used on water deliberately. Some things need capital: flood protection, climate-proofed infrastructure, livestock systems that cut methane. That is where concessional finance belongs. A country that has done the rest with its own instruments makes a far stronger case for it. Phase one happened by accident. Phase two cannot. Islamabad should design the next transformation, in water as well as power, rather than wait for the next IMF programme to force it. The first revolution cut Pakistan’s fuel bill and emissions. Blanket ban on AJK regional, refugee job quotas abolished

In a judgement announced on Wednesday, a division bench comprising Chief Justice Raja Saeed Akram and Justice Khalid Yousaf Chaudhary also directed the Public Service Commission (PSC) to resume and complete all pending recruitment processes that had been suspended following the high court’s Aug 16, 2025 verdict. The bench, however, stopped short of restoring the decades-old quota percentages, directing the government to undertake a comprehensive review of the existing regional and refugee allocations and bring them in conformity with constitutional and statutory requirements. The court prescribed an interim geographical recruitment framework, under which vacancies in grades 1 to 7 would ordinarily be filled through competition among candidates from the relevant constituency, while those in grades 8 to 15 would be filled at the district level. Vacancies in grades 1-15 at divisional offices would be filled through competition among candidates from the division concerned, while posts in state-level institutions, irrespective of grade, and all posts in grades 16 and above would be filled on open merit among State Subjects. The court clarified that selection within each geographical field would remain strictly subject to prescribed qualifications, suitability and merit. If no suitable candidate was available within the primary geographical area, the recruitment process would be extended to a wider field in accordance with the applicable rules. Authored by Chief Justice Akram, the judgement held that the High Court had erred in treating every form of regional, area-based or refugee-related recruitment arrangement as inherently unconstitutional. It observed that the statutory framework under the AJK Civil Servants Act, 1976, and the Civil Servants Rules, 1977, recognised the government’s authority to provide proper representation to the component units of the AJK territory and refugees settled in Pakistan. However, the bench held that such authority was not unlimited and that historical quota arrangements could not be continued indefinitely without a rational and objectively ascertainable justification. The bench also dealt separately with the quota reserved for refugees settled in Pakistan, observing that their status as State Subjects was independent of the continuation of any particular preferential allocation. It held that neither the 19pc quota for refugees who migrated in 1947 nor the 6pc allocation for those who migrated in 1989 could be treated as a perpetual entitlement without a continuing constitutional and statutory justification. On educational admissions, the court ruled that regional preferences had to be examined independently of public-sector recruitment. The bench further set aside the high court’s directives concerning federal and provincial institutions and educational seats beyond the constitutional and administrative competence of the AJK government, observing that these were governed by their respective constitutional, statutory and inter-governmental arrangements. The SC ruled that appointments and admissions already finalised under the legal regime prevailing at the relevant time should not be disturbed solely on account of its decision. It held that the high court was not competent to declare the quota arrangements repugnant to the injunctions of Islam, as such jurisdiction vested in the Shariat Appellate Bench under the relevant law. The new system will also conceal the identity of officers responsible for deciding tax disputes. The rules are expected to be finalised this week, following feedback from stakeholders. Under the proposed system, the Commissioner (Appeals) will not be identified during hearings via video link, and their voice will be electronically modified to prevent disclosure of identity. The draft rules were notified through SRO 2136 of 2026 to amend the Income Tax Rules, 2002, by inserting a separate chapter on faceless appeals. A separate appeals wing/centre will be established in Islamabad, comprising appellate units, each headed by a Commissioner (Appeals). The wing will function under the administrative control of the FBR’s Member (Legal) Inland Revenue. However, the director general, chief commissioner or any other authority will not be allowed to exercise control over, or issue directions, to a Commissioner (Appeals) regarding the hearing or decision of an appeal. FBR or the Centre may also transfer pending appeals from one appellate unit to another through the automated system to ensure equitable workload distribution, efficiency and impartiality. All notices, requisitions, written submissions, arguments and evidence will be exchanged exclusively through the electronic portal. Neither taxpayers nor tax officials or their authorised representatives will be allowed to appear physically before the appellate authority. Taxpayers will, however, have the option of seeking a decision on their appeals based solely on written submissions. On Wednesday, the ICJ released a report — titled ‘Judicial Capture: Independence of the Judiciary in Pakistan after the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments’. It said that after analysing the institutional framework governing higher courts following the two amendments, the judiciary in Pakistan no longer enjoyed institutional guarantees of independence required under international law and standards. “The 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments were a decisive break from the model of judicial independence that Pakistan had built after the restoration of the judiciary in 2009,” the report noted. The report said that while the government argued that amendments were made to “check judicial overreach, reduce the backlog of cases, end the perceived politicisation of the SC’s original jurisdiction and give Parliament a role in judicial appointments comparable to that of legislatures in other democracies”, the claims did not justify “changing the judicial structure in a manner that is glaringly inconsistent with Pakistan’s obligations under international law”.
