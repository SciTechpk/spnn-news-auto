Balochistan’s requirements are to be met through available Pakistan Agricultural Storage and Services Corporation stocks. Heavy rain and strong winds from the “Nor-easter” battered the central and eastern United States, creating treacherous travel conditions during one of the country’s busiest holiday periods. As families were gathering for traditional holiday feasts, more than 900,000 homes and businesses were without power, with Pennsylvania, Maryland and Virginia particularly hard hit, according to tracking website PowerOutage. At airports, nearly 400 flights were cancelled and over 2,000 were delayed in the United States, mostly in the East, figures from FlightAware showed. The only other time the parade was not held on Thanksgiving Thursday was when it was cancelled for three years during World War II. Organisers would have been only too aware of the gusting winds that wrecked the parade back in 1997 when a careening 18-foot figure of the “Cat in the Hat” toppled a lamppost, knocking one bystander unconscious. “While we are heartbroken that our parade was postponed, the safety of our partners, the safety of our participants — and spectators — is and always will be our top priority,” said Macy’s parade executive producer Will Coss. Stay safe While flooding was reported up and down the East Coast, impacts were also felt inland, with powerful gusts and inundated soil leading to trees tumbling over onto power lines and homes. Authorities in the Maryland suburbs of the US capital, worried about the impact of power outages on the water treatment network, asked nearly two million customers to conserve tap water consumption. Officials urged people to limit road travel, while several major bridges were closed due to high winds. India-US trade agreement nearing completion: Indian commerce minister

India said Friday it was close to finalising a long-awaited trade deal with the United States after Washington proposed higher tariffs on the country’s goods if negotiations fail. “The US trade deal is close to culmination,” Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal told an industry conference, adding that differences were beginning to be resolved. “The process and pathway, which was always something which held back finalising the deal, is now taking some shape,” he said. Goyal’s comments came after US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer on Wednesday said Indian goods would face tariffs of 17 per cent if a trade deal is reached but rise to as much as 25pc if not. The new tariffs face a period of public consultation process before taking effect next year. The United States is India’s largest trading partner and the two have been negotiating a trade deal since reaching an interim accord in February. New Delhi has sought access to the US market for key export sectors including textiles, pharmaceuticals and engineering goods, while Washington has pushed for greater access for American products and closer alignment on trade rules. Since February, several rounds of talks have taken place but have been complicated by Washington launching a probe into unfair trade practices against multiple countries, including India. Last month, New Delhi’s Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman said trade deal talks between India and US had reached a “plateau”, after negotiations dragged on for months with the sides struggling to resolve sticking points. On Wednesday, the US unveiled proposed tariffs on 14 economies, including India, after a months-long probe into alleged excess production. The duties, proposed at up to 20pc, came over allegations that the economies “are promoting production and capacity divorced from the realities of demand and market principles,” a senior US official said on condition of anonymity.

Erdogan completes Turkiye's ratification of Makkah defence pact

President Tayyip Erdogan has formally approved the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement with Pakistan and Saudi Arabia, the country’s Official Gazette said on Friday, completing Turkiye’s ratification after parliament ratified it earlier this month. Turkiye’s parliament ratified the agreement on November 5, paving the way for Ankara to support Riyadh amid attacks by Yemen’s Houthi militia against the kingdom. Under the agreement, an attack on one party constitutes an attack on all. The approval comes as Pakistan hosts a two-day meeting of the tripartite alliance’s top decision-making body from Friday, with discussions also expected to feature the conflict between Saudi Arabia and Yemen’s Iran-backed Houthi fighters. The third session of the Strategic Political and Defence Committee (SPDC) is being held in Islamabad, where authorities have announced a public holiday and imposed special movement restrictions for the high-level gathering. A local holiday has also been announced in Rawalpindi. The meeting is expected to focus on finalising standard operating procedures (SOPs), establishing an implementation mechanism and defining coordination between the three countries’ political, defence and military institutions, a Pakistani official said. Defence Minister Khawaja Asif said on Thursday that the foreign and defence ministers of Saudi Arabia, Turkiye and Pakistan would participate in the meeting, adding that the “continuous turmoil south of Saudi Arabia will obviously be one agenda point in the meeting”. A ruling party lawmaker told Reuters last month that Turkiye would also submit a mandate to parliament seeking approval for the deployment of military personnel to Saudi Arabia under the agreement. Ankara’s ratification clears another step towards the implementation of the pact, which could allow Turkiye to provide military support to Riyadh amid attacks by Houthis against the kingdom. The alliance The three countries signed the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement on Aug 7. The pact provides that an armed attack on any one member will be considered an attack on all, obliging the other two to extend assistance through available means, including military support. The SPDC, which brings together the foreign and defence ministers and chiefs of defence staff of the three countries, is the alliance’s principal decision-making forum. A permanent secretariat has been established in Riyadh to coordinate its work, with Pakistan’s retired Lt Gen Nauman Mahmood appointed as its first secretary general for a three-year term. A working committee comprising officials from member countries is also functioning. Turkiye’s ratification of the agreement has completed the accession process for all three member states. The alliance has yet to publicly define its doctrine, the composition of forces, command arrangements or procedures governing a collective military response. An emergency SPDC meeting in Riyadh on Oct 5 had called for moving towards practical implementation of collective defence commitments, including arrangements for the rapid deployment of agreed forces and capabilities, subject to national laws. Wider regional tensions The alliance’s formation also comes against the backdrop of wider regional tensions involving Iran, the United States and Israel. While the three members share an interest in strengthening regional deterrence, their differing strategic priorities make it important to define the alliance’s scope without allowing it to become an instrument for wider military confrontation. Asif declined to comment on how the alliance would respond to a direct Iranian attack on Saudi Arabia, but identified Israel as a possible threat to other countries in the region. “The possibility of some conflict, or aggression from the Israeli side exists, so we need to close our ranks, the countries of this region,” he said. Managing relations with Iran will be particularly important for Pakistan, which has sought to preserve diplomatic engagement with Tehran while deepening defence cooperation with Saudi Arabia and Turkiye. A meeting in Tehran involving representatives of the three countries and Iranian officials, held on Pakistan’s initiative, reflected efforts to keep channels of communication open between the two sides. Pakistan brokered a June accord that led to a brief halt in fighting between the US and Iran and laid out a roadmap for reopening the Strait of Hormuz and negotiations on Tehran’s nuclear programme. However, the truce collapsed, with Washington and Tehran accusing each other of violating the agreement, prompting months of shuttle diplomacy led principally by Qatar and Pakistan. Asif said Pakistan had played “a very, very constructive role” between Washington and Tehran, but the outcome of ongoing mediation was “still fragile” and peace was “not yet embedded in a way people were expecting”. Turkiye had earlier stressed the defensive character of the agreement and remained cautious about involvement in combat operations in Yemen.

LAHORE: The Lahore High Court (LHC) on Friday issued notices to the Pakistan Medical and Dental Council (PMDC) and other respondents on a petition filed by five Afghan medical students seeking registration to sit their first professional MBBS examination in January. The petitioners — Forough Dihqanpur, Shahira Sharif, Zahra Nabawi, Faiza Noori and Shabana Rahimi — are enrolled in the first year of the MBBS programme at Sheikh Khalifa Bin Zayed Al Nahyan Medical and Dental College, Lahore, under the Allama Muhammad Iqbal Scholarship Programme run by the Higher Education Commission (HEC). Advocate Asad Jamal, counsel for the petitioners, informed the court that they joined the college on April 6 after undergoing a multi-stage selection process conducted by the HEC. They were subsequently issued student cards and bona fide certificates, he added. He argued that registration is a mandatory prerequisite for appearing in the first professional examination, scheduled to be held from Jan 4 to 11, 2027. He pointed out that the college has asked the University of Health Sciences (UHS) to open its registration portal for the students, but the university has declined, saying the PMDC has not permitted such admissions for the 2025-26 session. The counsel further said that the PMDC subsequently approved the placement of 68 nominated Afghan students in public-sector colleges beyond the approved intake. Relying on Regulations 6 and 7 of the PMDC’s 2025 admissions regulations, he argued that the affiliating university was required to verify and register admitted students and that UHS could not withhold a decision indefinitely. He asked the court to declare the inaction unlawful and direct the respondents to complete the petitioners’ registration. He also sought provisional registration in the interim to enable them to sit the examination. However, Justice Asim Hafeez issued notices to the respondents, directing them to submit their replies by Dec 1 to the main petition and the application for interim relief. The petition named the PMDC, UHS, the Punjab government, the college, the federal government, the health ministry and the HEC as respondents. In September, the LHC suspended the operation of PMDC directives ordering Afghan medical students to return to Afghanistan. The order came after 13 Afghan students, including seven women and six men, enrolled at different medical colleges in Lahore, challenged the directives. The PMDC barred medical and dental colleges from admitting or placing Afghan students for the current academic session and said those already enrolled in such programmes were required to return to Afghanistan. In October, the LHC extended its stay against the expulsion and deportation of Afghan medical students and granted the PMDC more time to submit its reply. Separately, the Peshawar High Court (PHC) temporarily restrained the deportation of 86 Afghan students enrolled in public-sector medical colleges in the province and allowed them to continue their studies.

Speaking at a seminar titled ‘Personal Safety and Justice in a Digital World’, organised by the Institute of Regional Studies in Islamabad, Justice Kayani said that the issues arising from the Nadia Sarwar case before the Federal Constitutional Court (FCC) could only be addressed through parliament. He said the current state of the Constitution was evident to everyone, yet people remained silent despite being aware of what was happening. “The 26th and 27th amendments have given rise to a conflict between two superior courts,” he said, adding that established judicial precedents were being disregarded and new, problematic precedents were being set. He said parliament was the appropriate forum for resolving conflicts between the superior courts, emphasising that legislation was its prerogative, while the interpretation of the Constitution was the responsibility of the courts. He urged lawmakers to debate legal matters in parliament before introducing legislation to ensure that parliamentarians fully understood the laws being enacted and their implications. The former judge also questioned the growing use of laws regulating online expression, particularly the Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act (Peca), saying that journalists, members of parliament and lawyers were among those affected by the legislation. He urged the authorities not to issue notices to media organisations under Section 26-A merely for publishing news reports. “For God’s sake, do not issue notices to the media under Section 26-A. It should not be the case that the moment the media reports a story, a notice is im-mediately issued against them,” he said, stressing the need to protect journalists. He questioned the effectiveness of establishing new agencies to enforce such laws, remarking that authorities often created new agencies only to appoint the same officers under different uniforms.
