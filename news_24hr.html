The increase will raise the monthly profit on an investment of Rs100,000 under these schemes to Rs1,080 from Rs1,060. Similarly, the profit rate on Regular Income Certificates has been increased to 12.12pc from 11.76pc. The revised rate will provide a monthly return of Rs1,010 on an investment of Rs100,000, compared with Rs980 previously. The profit rate on Special Savings Certificates has been increased to 12pc from 11.6pc for the first five profit payments. Under the revised schedule, investors will receive Rs6,000 on an investment of Rs100,000 every six months, while the sixth and final profit payment will be Rs6,400 at a rate of 12.8pc. The government has also raised cumulative profit rates on Defence Savings Certificates across different maturity periods. For six-year certificates, the cumulative return has increased to 88pc from 86pc, while the seven-year return has risen to 113pc from 109pc. Similarly, the eight-year return has increased to 142pc from 136pc, while the nine-year return has been raised to 176pc from 168pc. However, the profit rate on ordinary Savings Accounts has been maintained at 10pc per annum. One of Pakistan’s pioneering women triathletes, Sana learnt to swim only in 2021, and went on to complete three Ironman 70.3 races — a gruelling endurance event combining a 1.9km swim, 90.1km of cycling and a 21.1km run. Born in Kuwait and raised in Lahore before returning to Kuwait, she had built her triathlon career largely through her own determination and training. In 2026, that journey took her to Japan, where she and fellow Pakistani triathlete Anisha Ali from Hunza became part of the country’s first women’s triathlon contingent at the Asian Games. They were joined by male teammates Ali Hassan, Mohid Sadiq Lone and Muhammad Mustaghis Noor. Sana’s teammate Anisha, 26, brought a very different sporting background to the team. A national-level athlete in judo, martial arts, rowing, boating, tug-of-war and fencing, she was no stranger to competition or rigorous training. But triathlon presented a new challenge, requiring her to master three disciplines simultaneously. Hailing from Gilgit-Baltistan, she says her upbringing around mountains taught her to embrace challenges rather than shy away from them. This was a mindset she carried with her to the Asian Games. For Pakistan’s first women triathletes at the Asian Games, the competition was about more than medals. It was about simply showing up… For Sana, the call that changed everything came almost by chance. She was in Pakistan on holiday when a friend told her that trials were being held for the Asian Games triathlon team. The timing was fortunate. She went along with a handful of other athletes, some stronger cyclists, others accomplished runners. But Sana had one advantage that mattered in a sport demanding proficiency in all three disciplines: she could swim well too. A week later, she received a call from the Pakistan Triathlon Federation. She had been selected to represent Pakistan at the Asian Games. The selection marked a first. Pakistan was sending women triathletes to compete at the continental Games, and Sana and Anisha would be part of a small pioneering team, entering a competition in which the country had never before had female representation. Following her selection, Sana trained largely on her own. The federation president remained in regular contact, checking on her progress and advising her on areas that needed attention. But, beyond that, there was no extensive national high-performance training programme in place. For a sport that combines swimming, cycling and running — each requiring its own technical skills, coaching and equipment — much of the responsibility remained with the athlete. Even meeting her teammates was a relatively new experience. Sana had crossed paths with two of them during the trials, but they had been too occupied with the selection process to get to know each other properly. Their team chemistry would develop only after they arrived in Japan. When they did, something unexpected happened. The team became a source of strength for one another. “We all knew who we were competing against,” Sana says. “Everyone was friendly, shared their experiences and supported wherever it was needed.” There was also an awareness that they were entering the competition at a disadvantage. “Though we were the weaker team compared to others, none of us even for a second thought of giving up. We fought till the end.” That spirit extended beyond Pakistan’s team. Sana recalls meeting athletes from other countries, including competitors who were among the best in their respective nations and had professional-level experience. Rather than making her feel inadequate, they did the opposite. When she explained that the sprint format was very different from the long-distance races she was accustomed to, they reassured her. “They were there to calm me down and tell me that it was all okay,” she says. Some even pointed out what Sana herself had begun to realise: simply being there had significance. She was representing a country that had never before sent women triathletes to the Asian Games. That, in itself, was an achievement. The experience in Japan also challenged some of the complaints circulating about conditions for athletes at the Games. For the triathletes, the arrangements were, Sana says, excellent. The team was based in Gamagori, a city designated specifically for the triathlon. Their accommodation was close to the race venue and the beach, with an island visible from their location and a running track in the surrounding garden. Food was another pleasant surprise. There was a dedicated table for Muslim athletes, as well as vegan options — details Sana appreciated as an athlete travelling from Pakistan. “The staff and people were great, and there was a lot of security too,” she recalls. The competition itself, however, brought the inevitable nerves. “It was great, but there was also a lot of anxiety, stress and excitement — mixed emotions,” she says. There were no medals for Pakistan. But for Sana, the value of the experience cannot be measured by a podium finish. If anything, the race revealed the gap between Pakistan’s ambitions and the support required to turn those ambitions into sustained international competitiveness. Her diagnosis is blunt: Pakistan needs to treat the triathlon as a long-term high-performance sport rather than something athletes prepare for only when a major Games approaches. “If Pakistan wants competitive Asian Games athletes, they shouldn’t start receiving support six months before the event,” she says. The problems begin with the basics. Athletes need proper equipment, financial support and structured training. Sana points out that Pakistani competitors arrived on rented bicycles, while the tri-suits provided to them were also not suitable for the demands of the sport. They were not even made from the appropriate fabric required for the triathlon. It is difficult, she argues, to expect international-level performances without first investing in the athletes expected to deliver them. Her point goes beyond the triathlon. Her teammate Anisha believes more Pakistani women are doing sports, but says many talented girls — particularly those from rural, remote and mountainous areas — still lack the facilities, coaching, financial support and safe spaces they need. Representation matters too, she says: seeing Pakistani women compete internationally can help young girls believe that sport is a possibility for them as well. Pakistan has repeatedly produced athletes who reach international competitions through individual determination, personal sacrifice and, often, their own financial resources. But competing at the highest level requires more than determination. It requires a system. For Sana, the Asian Games were therefore both an accomplishment and a starting point. The experience showed her what elite triathlon looks like and, perhaps more importantly, what Pakistan needs to build if it wants to compete seriously in the future. The response at home has largely been encouraging. There has been criticism too, as there inevitably is whenever Pakistani athletes compete without winning medals. Sana, however, has little interest in allowing that noise to define the experience. “People will always have something to say whether you win or lose,” she says. “No one understands your struggle, and that is okay.” What she does remember is the overwhelming support. “The amount of positivity I have received from everywhere is unexplainable.” For a first appearance, perhaps that is the more important result. Sana, Anisha and their teammates did not return from Japan with medals. They returned with something less tangible but potentially more consequential: proof that Pakistani women can enter a demanding sport like triathlon, compete on an international stage and hold their own against athletes from countries with far more developed systems. The next step is no longer simply getting them to the starting line. It is giving the next generation the training, equipment, coaching and financial support to arrive there ready to compete. The first women have now gone. The question is whether Pakistan will build a path for those who come next. The writer is a journalist, documentary filmmaker and radio correspondent. Adopted at COP16 in Cali, Colombia, at the end of 2024, the fund began operating in February 2025. It proposes the payment of one percent of the annual profits of pharmaceutical, cosmetics, biotechnology companies or firms specialising in animal and plant breeding, or 0.1pc of their turnover. The Qanun-i-Shahadat Order, 1984, does not impose a legal duty to close a deceased person’s eyes. Rather, the practice is customary and religious, observed Justice Salahuddin Panhwar while dismissing jail petitions filed by Azmat Ullah alias Motu and Muhammad Luqman alias Gona. The petitioners had challenged their convictions in a 2011 double-murder case, upheld by the Lahore High Court (LHC) on May 18, 2022. Justice Panhwar was part of a three-member bench, headed by Justice Muhammad Hashim Khan Kakar. The bench upheld the convictions under Section 302(b) of the Pakistan Penal Code but maintained the high court’s decision to commute the death sentences to life imprisonment. In criminal trials, defence lawyers often argue that an incident was ‘unwitnessed’ if the post-mortem report records the deceased’s eyes as open, reasoning that close relatives would ordinarily close them after death. During the hearing, the petitioners’ counsel pointed to discrepancies in eyewitness statements and argued that the post-mortem and inquest reports, which recorded the eyes of both victims as open, indicated that the murders had not been witnessed. Article 129 versus rigid presumptions In a nine-page judgement, Justice Panhwar referred to Article 129 of the Qanun-i-Shahadat Order, which allows courts to presume facts they consider likely in the circumstances of a particular case. However, the judgement stressed that human behaviour could not be assessed in a vacuum or through rigid assumptions. Responses to trauma, terror and violent loss vary from person to person, making it unsafe to draw categorical conclusions from a single circumstance. It noted that relatives witnessing a gruesome incident may be overwhelmed by shock and trauma. Hoping their loved one might survive, they may focus on obtaining urgent medical assistance rather than performing customary rituals associated with death. Relatives and bystanders may also continue treating the victim as injured until a doctor or police official confirms death, which may not be immediately apparent at the scene. Other factors From a forensic perspective, the position of the eyelids is governed by physiological processes rather than the presence or absence of witnesses, the judgement explained. Following death, muscles initially become relaxed, allowing eyelids to remain open or partially open, a condition known as lagophthalmos. Handling or moving a body may also cause previously closed eyes to reopen, particularly before rigor mortis develops. As rigor mortis sets in, muscles become stiff and retain their prevailing position. The court also cited Locard’s Exchange Principle, which holds that every contact leaves a trace. Relatives, bystanders and rescue workers may avoid touching a body to prevent contamination or disturbance of blood-spatter patterns, trace evidence and DNA material before forensic experts arrive. It would therefore be unsafe to presume that every person knows how to handle a deceased body or feels obliged to close the eyes, the judgement said. Concluding that neither forensic literature nor the law recognises closed eyes as an indicator of a witnessed death, the court rejected the argument that open eyelids alone could establish that a crime was unwitnessed. The judgement further observed that minor discrepancies and natural variations in eyewitness accounts, arising from the passage of time and the rigours of cross-examination, do not undermine otherwise credible and consistent testimony. Arafat gathered the ball again and his slingy left arm, which had caused havoc in the preceding overs, produced a flat, direct throw that crashed into the stumps. It was the final act of an evening Arafat had made his own, leading Pakistan to an 87-run victory over England in the One-day International tri-series match at the Rawalpindi Cricket Stadium on Tuesday. The run-out capped an outstanding all-round performance from the left-arm spinner, who had earlier scored a crucial half-century before claiming six wickets for 40 runs in 8.4 overs to bowl England out for 215 in 38.4 overs while chasing 303. Pakistan players celebrate the run-out of England’s Sonny Baker.—AFP England began the chase confidently, with Tom Banton and Ben Duckett racing to 91 without loss in 14.3 overs. Duckett made 45 off 46 balls, striking eight fours, while Banton scored 57 from 53 deliveries with five fours and two sixes. Haris Rauf broke the opening stand when Duckett mistimed a pull and Fakhar Zaman took the catch at deep fine leg. Arafat then changed the complexion of the chase, removing Banton with his third delivery as the opener swung across the line and picked out Shaheen Shah Afridi at midwicket. The spinner struck again by bowling England captain Harry Brook for five with a delivery that gripped and straightened, leaving the visitors at 128-3. Naseem Shah subsequently ended Joe Root’s resistance, bowling him for 33 off 35 balls with a skidding delivery that kept low and struck the off stump. Sam Curran and Jordan Cox attempted to keep England in contention, but the visitors’ resistance steadily faded. Curran made 27 before top-edging a pull off Haris to substitute fielder Ghazi Ghori at deep backward square leg. Cox, who scored 18, was trapped leg-before by Arafat’s quicker delivery and lost his review. Pakistan pacer Haris Rauf celebrates dismissing England opener Tom Banton.—AFP Arafat continued his dismantling of the lower order, dismissing Jamie Overton for one when Hasan Nawaz took a catch at long-on after the batter charged down the pitch. Will Jacks briefly threatened a counterattack, striking Haris for six, but fell for 19 when he miscued Arafat to Shaheen at short midwicket. Arafat trapped Adil Rashid leg-before for a first-ball duck in the 39th over, completing his six-wicket haul. The final run-out of Atkinson then sealed Pakistan’s commanding win. England captain Brook acknowledged that his side had made a promising start but failed to capitalise after losing the opening partnership, with wickets falling in clusters. England captain Harry Brook looks stunned after being cleaned up by Pakistan’s Arafat Minhas.—AFP “Banton and Ducky set the tone beautifully, but we lost the wickets in clumps and could not put on a partnership and ended up on the wrong side,” Brook said. He also felt England had missed an opportunity to put Pakistan under pressure by being too cautious against the spinners. “We were a bit too much circumspect against the spinners. We could have put them under pressure, I feel,” he said, adding that England would discuss their shortcomings and look to bounce back in the next match. Pakistan’s total of 302-9 had been built on a spirited recovery led by Hasan Nawaz and Arafat after the hosts slumped to 133-5 following England’s decision to field. The pair added 78 runs off 74 balls for the sixth wicket, reviving an innings that had threatened to unravel. Hasan struck 79 off 74 deliveries, including five fours and four sixes, while Arafat contributed 59 off 58 balls, hitting nine fours. Pakistan’s Arafat Minhas (L) and Faheem Ashraf run between the wickets.—AFP Faheem Ashraf then provided a late surge with 39 off 26 balls, and captain Shaheen’s brisk five-ball 10 helped Pakistan cross 300. Arafat said the partnership with Hasan had been crucial in rebuilding the innings, with the pair determined to bat deep after he came to the crease. “We needed an important partnership when I walked in the middle. Hasan and I discussed that we need to bat deep,” he said at the presentation ceremony, speaking to Urooj Mumtaz. After Hasan’s dismissal, Arafat took responsibility for carrying the innings forward, helping Pakistan reach a total that ultimately proved beyond England. “I took the charge after Hasan got out and I tried to bat until the end of the innings, which helped in taking the score beyond 300,” he said. Pakistan had made a disastrous start when Atkinson dismissed Fakhar Zaman for a first-ball duck, caught by Joe Root at short midwicket. Saim Ayub briefly counterattacked before falling for eight, skying Sonny Baker to Sam Curran in the sixth over with Pakistan at 31-2. Babar Azam and Saad Baig attempted to stabilise the innings, but Babar’s 34 off 52 balls ended when Will Jacks breached his defences in the 15th over. Adil Rashid then removed Salman Ali Agha for 16, caught by Atkinson at deep fine leg, leaving Pakistan at 81-4. Pakistan’s Babar Azam is clean-bowled by England’s Will Jacks.—AFP Saad and Hasan offered resistance, adding 52 for the fifth wicket before Atkinson returned to dismiss Saad for 35 off 54 balls. Hasan and Arafat then took control, with Hasan finding the boundary through cover and backward point while punishing loose deliveries over the ropes. Hasan’s innings ended at 211-6 when Jamie Overton beat him with a yorker in the 40th over. Arafat continued the recovery alongside Faheem, whose aggressive strokeplay included consecutive boundaries against Rashid and a six off Root. Faheem fell for 39 in the 49th over, caught by Jacks at deep midwicket, while Arafat was dismissed for 59 after skying Baker to Harry Brook at cover. Shaheen’s late boundaries ensured Pakistan finished with a formidable total. Reflecting on his performance with the ball, Arafat credited the slower surface for offering more assistance to the spinners than in the previous match and said he had worked on varying his deliveries to deceive batters. Arafat Minhas walks off the field with team-mates after helping Pakistan crush England.—AFP “I have kept my plans simple. There was not much help for the spinners in the first match. The pitch was on the slower side today, which helped me,” he said. “Variations are very important for a spinner as they help in deceiving the batters. I have worked on my variations.” Pakistan captain Shaheen praised the contributions of Hasan, Arafat and Faheem in rescuing the innings but acknowledged that the top order needed to be more consistent. “It was really good how Hasan Nawaz, Arafat Minhas, and Faheem Ashraf batted, but we also need our top-order to fire,” he said. Shaheen described Arafat as a promising all-rounder whose performance was an encouraging sign for Pakistan as they build towards the World Cup. “The form of Arafat and fast bowlers is good signs for us. Arafat is a very good talent and he is a proper allrounder, which bodes well for us. Hopefully, he will deliver for us in many more games in the future,” he said. While pleased with aspects of Pakistan’s performance, Shaheen stressed that the team still had room for improvement ahead of the global tournament. “As a team we have done some good things but we need to improve further as we are looking to build towards the World Cup,” he said.
