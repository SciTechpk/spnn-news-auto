PM Shehbaz says water ‘lifeblood’ of region, must never be weaponised

Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif on Tuesday said water was the “lifeblood” of the region and must never be weaponised, as he addressed a summit of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) in Kyrgyzstan. Speaking at the SCO Council of Heads of States (CHS) meeting in the Kyrgyz capital Bishkek, the premier said the region cannot “realise its full potential without lasting peace”. Without naming India directly, he highlighted the issue of the Indus Waters Treaty (IWT) and demanded that it be honoured. “Water is the lifeblood of our region. It sustains millions of lives, nourishes our land and secures the very future of our coming generations,” he noted. He stressed that it “must never be weaponised; it cannot be used as an instrument of coercion, nor can it ever be exploited for political leverage”. “The treaties governing our shared waters are solemn, binding international commitments. They are not matters of political convenience to be cast aside at your will. They must be honoured unconditionally, in both letter and spirit.” In a later statement on X, the premier said he was honoured to address the ceremony as Pakistan assumed chairmanship of the SCO, reiterating that water “is the lifeblood of our region” and must never be weaponised by any state. He also noted that Pakistan made “immense sacrifices in the fight against terrorism”, and added that Pakistan will conduct its chairmanship of the body under the theme ‘Turning Vision into Action: Connectivity, Innovation and Shared Prosperity’. “I am confident that the SCO will continue to grow from strength to strength and bring meaningful improvements to the lives of its more than 3 billion people,” he writes. The prime minister’s speech comes a day after the Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA) ordered India to uphold the IWT with Pakistan and suspend work on a hydro project in occupied Kashmir. The Hague-based court backed Pakistan’s stance, declaring that the IWT between the two countries remained fully in force, as India had no justification for ending or suspending the agreement. Water and the IWT remain a contentious issue between India and Pakistan, following New Delhi’s unilateral abeyance of the accord in April last year — a move that was followed by a brief military conflict between the two sides in May 2025. ‘Terrorism one of the gravest threats’ Speaking on the issue of terrorism, PM Shehbaz said it remained “one of the gravest threats that has affected our entire region in its various forms and manifestations”. As chair of the SCO Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure (RATS), Pakistan has “worked actively to strengthen regional counter-terrorism cooperation”, he highlighted. “Few nations have fought against the scourge of terrorism as resolutely and valiantly as Pakistan. In the past two and a half decades, we have sacrificed more than 90,000 lives and endured economic losses of over $150 billion in combatting terrorism.” Pakistan sought a peaceful and stable Afghanistan, he said, adding: “But let there be no ambiguity; so long as terrorists continue to use Afghan territory to shed the blood of innocent Pakistanis and other nations, lasting peace will remain beyond our reach.” In his speech, the prime minister also mentioned Pakistan’s mediation efforts in the US-Iran war and laid out the country’s plan as it assumed the SCO CHS chair. Terrorist attacks increase but fatalities decline during August: PICSS

ISLAMABAD: The number of terrorist attacks increased significantly in August despite sustained counter-terrorism pressure, but their human impact declined compared with July, according to a monthly security assessment by an Islamabad-based think tank. According to monthly data compiled by the Pakistan Institute for Conflict and Security Studies (PICSS), 199 terrorist attacks were recorded in August, compared with 144 in July — an increase of 38 per cent. Violence linked to terrorist attacks and counter-terrorism operations had intensified sharply in July, making it the deadliest month of 2026. However, the institute added that despite the increase in attack frequency, their human impact declined from the previous month. Data showed that the 199 attacks recorded during August resulted in 158 deaths and 181 injuries, compared with 268 deaths and 216 injuries in 144 attacks during July. Deaths resulting from terrorist attacks therefore declined by 41pc, while injuries declined by 16pc. Security forces’ continuing counter-terrorism operations during August resulted in 211 deaths, including 203 terrorists. The operations also resulted in 21 terrorists being injured and 26 suspected terrorists arrested. Security forces killed 115 terrorists in Balochistan, 56 in mainland Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and 32 in the merged districts during the month. The institute also recorded three suicide attacks during August, including two vehicle-borne suicide attacks. PICSS noted that this represented a substantial decline from July, when eight suicide attacks were recorded, including five vehicle-borne suicide attacks — “the highest number of suicide attacks recorded in a single month during the past decade”. Kidnapping incidents declined by 36pc, falling from 66 in July to 42 in August. In terms of attack typology, the report noted that tactical assaults emerged as the most frequently used tactic during the month, with 47 incidents, followed by target killings with 46 and attacks with improvised explosive devices (IED) with 36. Tactical assaults resulted in 40 deaths and 44 injuries, while target killings caused 50 deaths and 23 injuries. IED attacks resulted in 15 deaths and 50 injuries. According to the PICSS report, the security situation remained most severe in Balochistan, where the number of terrorist attacks increased by 7pc from 83 in July to 89 in August. Deaths resulting from these attacks declined from 162 in July to 76, while injuries declined from 86 to 47. Meanwhile, security forces’ actions in Balochistan resulted in 118 deaths, including 115 terrorists, compared with 192 deaths, including 189 terrorists in July. Mainland KP, excluding the merged districts, recorded 54 terrorist attacks in August compared with 36 in July, representing an increase of 50pc. Deaths resulting from terrorist attacks declined from 69 in July to 44 in August, while injuries declined from 96 to 70, representing a decrease of 27pc. Security forces’ actions in the province resulted in 60 deaths, including 56 terrorists — also lower than the July numbers. The merged districts of KP, formerly known as FATA, recorded 52 terrorist attacks in August, an increase of 126pc compared with 23 in July. Deaths resulting from these attacks declined slightly from 36 to 34, while injuries increased from 33 to 59. Security forces’ actions resulted in 33 deaths, including 32 terrorists, compared with 43 deaths, including 41 terrorists, during July. In Punjab, the think tank recorded two terrorist attacks resulting in three deaths and five injuries. Security forces conducted two notable operations in the province and arrested five suspected terrorists. Sindh also recorded two terrorist attacks in August, in which one person was killed, while security forces conducted two operations and arrested two suspected terrorists. Islamabad, Azad Jammu and Kashmir, and Gilgit-Baltistan witnessed no terrorist attacks during August, the report added.

Boat builders keep Karachi's fishing heritage afloat

The rhythmic hammering of nails and the whine of saws echo around a coastal Karachi neighbourhood, where small figures of men work atop massive wooden fishing hulls that loom beneath a pale summer sky. Ibrahim Hyderi, a maritime town on the outskirts of the port city believed to date back centuries, is renowned for the construction of traditional fishing boats. “This is the area that gave birth to Karachi, and that provided Karachi with its fishing sector,” said Mehran Ali Shah of the Pakistan Fisherfolk Forum, which advocates for the local community. This photograph taken on July 8, 2026 shows workers building traditional wooden boats at a yard in Ibrahim Hyderi in Karachi. —AFP/File Despite the rise of industrial shipbuilding, such practices — where handcrafted vessels are assembled timber by timber — have stood their ground. Boat-builder Muhammad Asif, 22, learnt the craft from his father as a teenager, and is now proud that he can work on his own. “I was drawn to this work, so I work at it with great dedication,” Asif tells AFP as sawdust coats his face and sun-bleached hair. “It has always been my passion, that is why I chose this line of work.” This photograph taken on July 8, 2026 shows craftsman Muhammad Asif building a traditional wooden boat at a yard in Ibrahim Hyderi in Karachi. —AFP/File Asif, like other workers, starts at 7 am every day and heads home for dinner around 7 pm, only putting down his tools during a two-hour lunch break. He earns up to Rs2,500 a day, working six days a week. The workers brave the elements all year round, including the sweltering heat and humidity of summers and the soaking rains of the monsoon seasons. The process begins with a fisherman selecting a boat design. A master craftsman drafts a template and the workers take it from there, drawing on their years of experience to painstakingly build the vessel. The carpenters choose from different types of timber, such as local eucalyptus wood, or African teak for resilience against seawater. This photograph taken on July 8, 2026 shows workers building traditional wooden boats at a yard in Ibrahim Hyderi in Karachi. —AFP/File The heavy blocks of wood are carefully cut using well-worn vertical and other saws, following hand-drawn pencil markings. The boats are then assembled from the ground up, block by block and nail by nail, with the workers clambering up on rickety ladders as the hulls rise higher. The time-consuming process means even a small boat measuring just several metres long could take up to three months to construct and cost Rs1 million, Asif says. This photograph taken on July 8, 2026 shows traditional wooden boats at a yard in Ibrahim Hyderi in Karachi. —AFP/File Across the yard, the completed boats are given splashes of colour by painter Muhammad Ismail. The weather-beaten 38-year-old, his hands and nails tinged with blue, is painting petals on the hull of a vessel — one of many he has worked on over a decade. “Most of our fishermen prefer floral designs rather than fish,” he tells AFP, adding that he experiences “great satisfaction” when an owner appreciates his artwork. This photograph taken on July 8, 2026 shows craftsman Muhammad Ismail painting a traditional wooden boat at a yard in Ibrahim Hyderi in Karachi. —AFP/File “Some people ask me to create a design according to my own ideas, while others tell me exactly what they want.” At a nearby harbour, fisherman Qadir Bux sits in a small boat owned by his family for at least two decades as he mends fishing nets. The fisherman hopes to give the wooden vessel a facelift soon. “It’s like looking after something very precious, or even like caring for our own child,” he says. Ousted UK PM Keir Starmer quits parliament to focus on international affairs

Keir Starmer will stand down as a member of parliament, he said on Tuesday, some six weeks after he resigned as Britain’s prime minister having lost the support of his Labour Party. Starmer guided Labour into power in 2024 with one of the biggest parliamentary majorities in Britain’s modern history, completing a remarkable turnaround having become Labour leader four years earlier after its worst election showing since 1935. But the former lawyer, whose tenure as prime minister was marked by several damaging U-turns, failed to connect with voters and steadily lost the support of the public and his party. Starmer had previously said he would remain as a lawmaker until the next general election. But he told the Camden New Journal, a local newspaper in his North London constituency, that he had changed his mind and intended to “concentrate on international affairs”. He confirmed his resignation in a statement on X. Despite the sagging popularity at home that cost him his job, foreign affairs were often seen as a strength during Starmer’s premiership. He worked closely with French President Emmanuel Macron to establish the so-called Coalition of the Willing in support of Ukraine, reset ties with China, recognised a Palestinian state and had mixed success in efforts to build relations with US President Donald Trump. The newspaper report did not give a timeframe for when an election would be held for Starmer’s seat in the constituency of Holborn and St Pancras. Starmer comfortably held the seat in 2024 with a majority of just over 11,500, though an independent candidate who campaigned primarily on opposition to Israel’s assault on Gaza took nearly 19 per cent of the vote. He was replaced as prime minister in July by Andy Burnham, who became Britain’s seventh prime minister in a decade. Burnham’s accession has led to a bounce in the polls for Labour, though he faces a sterner test when parliament resumes later on Tuesday.

Why first 1,000 days of a child’s life are critical for long-term health

The first 1,000 days of a child’s life are critical for nutrition, immunity, brain development and long-term health. In Pakistan, however, newborn care, immunisation, feeding support and developmental monitoring are often delivered through separate programmes, rather than being connected through a single system that follows a child continuously. This raises questions about whether children receive continuous care from birth through early childhood. If these services are fragmented, there is a concern that a child might receive one service but miss another as they grow. Dr D.S. Justice Raheel Kamran passed the judgement while disposing of four connected petitions arising from a matrimonial dispute between Dr Rukhsana Kausar and Shahid Nazeer. Both parties had challenged the findings of a Muzaffargarh sessions court concerning gold ornaments and other matters, including maintenance, dowry and ownership of the couple’s house. The marriage was dissolved through Khula in 2012. The family court initially held that the wife was required to return the dower received as Badl-i-Khula (compensation for Khula), while other disputes, including maintenance, dowry articles, gold ornaments and delivery expenses, remained pending. In 2018, the family court partly decreed the suit, awarding maintenance and ordering recovery of dowry articles. It also directed the wife to return 11 tolas of gold, or its value, to the husband. The appellate court upheld these findings in 2020, reducing only the annual increase in the minor child’s maintenance from 15 to 10 pc. On maintenance, Justice Kamran upheld the concurrent findings, noting that the husband had withheld his complete salary record and produced only a certificate showing his basic pay. The courts were therefore entitled to draw an adverse inference regarding his actual earning capacity. The judge also noted that the husband’s brother had testified that he owned a Honda Civic, further undermining his claim of limited means. The court held that it would not permit a “financially capable parent” to understate his resources to reduce his obligations towards his child. Regarding the matrimonial house, the judge upheld findings that the wife’s father, who was working abroad at the time, had financed its entire purchase through a documented chain of banking transactions. Bank officials corroborated the transactions, and their testimony remained largely unchallenged during cross-examination. Since the husband had never actually paid for the house despite its inclusion in the dower, the judge held that it could not be reclaimed as Badl-i-Khula. He also upheld the wife’s subsequent transfer of the house to her father and the decree granting him possession. On the claim for 11 tolas of gold, Justice Kamran found that the lower courts had erred by treating the absence of documentary evidence as decisive while overlooking corroborating circumstances. Given the husband’s established financial incapacity, the judge held that it was more probable that the gold, too, had been financed by the wife’s father. “Consequently, the respondent cannot claim return of the said ornaments as Badl-i-Khula,” the judge held, because they did not represent dower financed or paid for by him from his own resources. Regarding 22 tolas of dowry gold, the judge upheld the findings against the wife, noting that she had never claimed the jewellery was forcibly taken from her. Her inconsistent accounts about when she left the matrimonial home, coupled with her refusal to produce a video of her sister’s wedding showing her wearing jewellery after leaving her husband’s home, also supported an adverse inference. Justice Kamran upheld the dismissal of the wife’s Rs63,000 claim for maternity/delivery expenses. Published in Dawn, September 1st, 2026

Dubai property no longer safe haven

KARACHI: The Gulf war has curbed the outflow of black money from Pakistan to Dubai, while funds already invested are returning and being diverted into property in the country, according to sources in the property and currency markets. Thousands of Pakistanis have invested hundreds of millions of dollars in Dubai, particularly in property. Pakistan was identified twice as the second-largest foreign investor in Dubai property. It was widely believed that black money created in Pakistan had a safe haven in Dubai and was a catalyst for property price hikes there. “About $60 million in illegal (or black) money is created in Pakistan per month and has been invested in Dubai, but this has now stopped,” said All Pakistan Builders Association Chairman Hassan Bakhshi. Currency dealers said the situation has reversed, with black money in Dubai stuck and recovery difficult due to the ongoing war. Pakistanis struggle to recover investments as black money fuels Karachi property rally “The higher remittances from Dubai reflect that Pakistanis are sending back their liquid assets to Pakistan,” said a currency dealer. The war has changed Dubai’s appeal as a destination for investment and tourism. Inflows from Dubai have increased. The impact is that illegal money being created in Pakistan has once again diverted towards the property business. “Property prices in Defence went up 50-60 per cent after the Gulf war started. Since the title of the Defence property is safe (meaning no double filing or fake dealing), most of the money is going towards the area,” said Mr Bakhshi. Not only was black money going to Dubai, but many tech companies had also shifted there before the war because of a hurdle-free business environment. They were also moving abroad due to frequent internet connectivity issues and excessive interference from the tax authorities. Thousands of Pakistanis were using Dubai as a third country and conducting legal business with countries such as India and Bangladesh. Now they are stuck and trying to recover their investment from Dubai, as the war-like situation still prevails. Currency dealers said that once the situation normalises, hundreds of millions of dollars would return to Pakistan from the Gulf, as Dubai has lost the confidence of foreign investors. Mr Bakhshi also endorsed reports that Pakistani investors were trying their best to recover their investments from Dubai, but property prices in the war-affected country have fallen to a minimum level. Property dealers said that prices in Pakistan have started to increase after the war, and trading has also increased. Both buying and selling have increased due to improved liquidity. “The government is also taking measures to boost the construction industry, and the rebound in property prices was the result of this effort. Govt increases petrol price by Rs0.77, cuts high-speed diesel price by Rs1.03

The government on Monday raised the price of petrol by Rs0.77 per litre and cut that of high-speed diesel (HSD) by Rs1.03 per litre. Following the revision, petrol will retail at Rs342.79 per litre while HSD will cost Rs370.41 per litre. The government continues to levy Rs114 per litre in taxes and duties on petrol and Rs100 per litre on diesel. According to the Petroleum Division’s notification, the new prices will be applicable on September 1 (Tuesday). The price of HSD has come down from a peak of Rs520.35 recorded on April 3. Its price had started rising from Rs281 per litre after the US-Iran war broke out on February 28. The petrol price had peaked at Rs458.41 on April 3 after beginning its upward trajectory from Rs266 in the first week of March. On July 17, Petroleum Minister Ali Pervaiz Malik announced that fuel prices would now be fixed on a daily basis due to fluctuations in international market prices following renewed hostilities between Iran and the US. Prior to this, the government had been announcing weekly revisions to fuel prices since early March, alongside measures for the conservation of fuel amid possible oil supply disruptions due to the ongoing conflict in the Middle East. The federal government in April also announced targeted relief measures to provide subsidised fuel. The petroleum minister stated that the cabinet and the prime minister had decided to give the Oil and Gas Regulatory Authority (Ogra) the responsibility of deciding fuel prices on a daily basis based on international market trends. Petrol is mainly used in private transport, small vehicles, rickshaws and two-wheelers, and changes in its price affect the middle and lower-middle classes. Similarly, changes in diesel prices also impact the public at large, as it is mainly used in the heavy transport sector, power plants and large generators. Petrol and high-speed diesel (HSD) are the major revenue earners, with monthly sales of about 700,000 to 800,000 tonnes, compared to just 10,000 tonnes of monthly demand for kerosene.

'Technical fault': Driverless car crashes into police van in Islamabad

KARACHI: A video clip went viral on social media after a remote-controlled car crashed into a police van in Islamabad, reportedly due to a “technical fault” caused by an internet disruption. On Sunday, journalist Muhammad Aalijah uploaded a video on his official social media account showing him seated in the front passenger seat of a car at Islamabad’s D-Chowk, which appeared to be operated using a remote-control device. As Aalijah explains the car’s features and it moves forward, the steering wheel, controlled remotely by an operator, suddenly goes out of control. The car consequently bumps into a police van parked along the road. The video also shows Aalijah pulling the handbrake in an attempt to prevent the car from hitting the police van, but his efforts prove futile. Since being uploaded, the video has been widely circulated on social media, with users adding witty captions to the footage. When contacted, Aalijah told Dawn that he had been asked by technology enthusiast Ehsan Zafar of Abbottabad to report on his tech-based automated-driving innovation. “Everything was fine until a technical fault occurred due to an internet disruption, causing the car to go out of control and crash into a police vehicle,” he said. Aalijah explained that the car was controlled remotely over the internet and that a sudden loss of connection caused it to go out of control. In a social media post on Monday, Aalijah said his intention in making the video was “never to defame or target this young man”, who owned and operated the car. “The sole purpose was to raise awareness and urge people to remain cautious until any new experiment or technology has been thoroughly tested, proven safe and established as a complete success,” he said. “This was a message of caution, not an attempt to discredit anyone.” Sharing the video online, Aalijah said the car’s system was not yet “completely safe and reliable”. “The vehicle was being operated through satellite and internet connections, but during external control, it went out of control as soon as the internet connection was disconnected and collided with a police vehicle,” he wrote. The incident occurred in a high-security area with very little traffic. “If the same situation had occurred on a busy road, the consequences could have been extremely dangerous, even fatal,” he said. “This video is not criticism; it is a responsible effort to raise awareness and issue a warning — by all means, experiment, but ensure safety first,” he added.
