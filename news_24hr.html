Provinces under fire

THE liquidation of the KP Assembly is an event rooted in economic, more specifically fiscal, constraints. For the time being, it is unlikely that the trajectory will continue to the other provinces, but it is worth keeping an eye on the resource envelope available to the state as the primary fount of this event. Here is how it works. Every time the state is faced with a major demand for resources, usually for security-related matters, financial managers point to the NFC Award as the reason why that demand cannot be met. In the middle of the 2010s, for example, the then PML-N government faced a major demand for resources to pay for the military operations underway in the wake of the Army Public School massacre by the TTP. The then finance minister Ishaq Dar had said, in December 2016 for instance, that the government had to spend over Rs300 billion on the security operations since they began. He proposed a cut of seven per cent from the Federal Divisible Pool before provincial shares were calculated, in order to meet the expenses going forward. The next year, Miftah Ismail, the then special assistant to the prime minister on finance, again pointed to the NFC Award and provincial expenditures as the reason why the federal government had to run such a large budget deficit and why demands for releases from the development budget might have to wait. That was in October 2017, when Dar was no longer finance minister. In 2019, prime minister Imran Khan told a rally in Ghotki that the 18th Amendment had bankrupted Pakistan, and his government had no resources left to pay for development or relief after paying for debt servicing, defence and transfers to the provinces under the NFC Award. In 2020, his own information minister was telling TV channels that his government might have to “change the law” on the NFC Awards because its allocations had become unsustainable. In 2024, Miftah Ismail was quoted in Dawn as saying that “the federal government is teetering on the brink of bankruptcy while the provinces are flush with cash”. Shortly afterwards, the current Finance Minister Muhammad Aurangzeb was quoted in Washington, where he had gone for the annual IMF and World Bank meetings, as saying that Islamabad would revisit the NFC Award, if the IMF loan was approved, through either expenditure-sharing or incentives for provinces to widen the tax base. Many of these people subsequently took their statements back, or at least added caveats that their government had no intention to “roll back” the NFC. The main reason for the backtracking is that every major political party in the country — the PPP, PML-N and PTI — controlled a province, and none wanted to become a party to any attempt to roll back provincial rights. What began as a search for resources turned into a search for a new political arrangement. But the litany of complaints about the enhanced provincial shares under the last NFC continued. Ahsan Iqbal repeated them last year in Karachi, and Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif publicly floated the idea of changing the composition of the divisible pool, perhaps by removing customs duties from it. This remark triggered a little conversation of its own, with some delving into how and why these duties were part of the divisible pool in the first place. All along, almost from the very first year when the empowered provincial government’s scheme went into effect from 2009, blaming them has become the favourite excuse for failure in Islamabad. But the scheme itself, which consists of the 18th Amendment and the NFC Award, endured because of the power of consensus behind it. In recent years, though, with the arrival of the ‘hybrid-plus’ government, the conversation around the federative scheme of empowered provinces has turned more menacing. An attempt was made by the federal authorities, for example, to usurp Sindh’s share of water resources, and a demand for some amount of reverse-flow from the provinces to the centre found concrete form in an MoU signed in the NFC meetings as part of the budget for FY27. An amount of Rs1.04 trillion is supposed to be transferred back to the federal government by three provinces — Punjab, Sindh, and KP — this fiscal year. What began as an economic conversation acquired political and security substance as it rolled through the system. As part of the complaints, questions were raised about what the provinces were doing with the incremental resources flowing their way since 2009. Some demanded to know where we should look for the results. A World Bank report landed in the middle of all this, showing that the bulk of incremental resources was consumed in salaries and other current expenditures, while social indicators showed little to no improvement since the scheme of enhanced provincial powers went into effect. That added fuel to the fire. What began as a search for resources turned into a search for a new political arrangement, with some in government publicly saying a “reset” was coming. It acquired a security dimension as the PTI government was called upon to play its part in the counterterrorism operations in KP. And it fed an opportunistic suggestion for wrapping up the whole system and bringing in “administrative units” to replace provincial governments altogether. Somewhere in all this a perception seems to have taken root that the state’s federative structure is too expensive for a resource-starved country like Pakistan. If the provinces have nothing to show for all the funds they have consumed since their powers and resource commitments were enhanced, why keep them longer, ask the proponents of this thinking. They don’t yet have an alternative structure to propose. But in the months to come, it is likely that the governments of Sindh and Punjab will be asked to grant more space to the federal government in the control of the resources they command. Don’t derail the transition

PAKISTAN has reached a staff-level agreement with the IMF after completing the fourth review under its programme. Implementation of the Extended Fund Facility is on track, with progress on macroeconomic stability. The challenge is to translate the gains into sustained growth. Political and administrative disruptions that can derail this difficult transition must be avoided. The fiscal deficit has been contained at 2.6 per cent of GDP. The primary surplus has strengthened, supported by provincial cash surpluses and exceptional grants to the federal budget. Revenue targets have been met, though raising petroleum levies amid rising global prices adds to inflationary pressure. The external current account has been broadly balanced, largely because of stronger-than-expected workers’ remittances, despite declining merchandise exports. Sovereign credit ratings have improved, though remaining below 2006-07 levels. Access to global capital markets for long-term financing stands restored, and diversification into instruments like Panda bonds has begun. Foreign portfolio inflows have recovered, but FDI remains disappointing. Talks with the US Treasury on a proposed 10-year Exchange Stabilisation Facility continue, while tariff reduction discussions with the US Trade Representative remain inconclusive. Foreign exchange reserves accumulated by purchasing dollars from the intermarket rather than borrowing now cover three months of imports, up from a trough of $4.3 billion in FY2023, helping stabilise the exchange rate and strengthen the balance of payments. External public debt has declined to 25pc of GDP; the share of short-term debt has fallen from 24 to 16pc, partly after a Saudi deposit was converted into a three-year facility. Yet ordinary citizens see little improvement. Poverty, joblessness, poor public services, unfair taxation, intrusive regulation, and high energy prices constrain households and businesses. Savings and investment are weak, and exports lack the dynamism to sustain growth. We must expand productive capacity, employment, and exports without reigniting inflation or destabilising the external account. This requires completing the structural reform agenda. Federal and provincial governments must be right-sized, civil services professionalised through specialist recruitment and better incentives, and public administration digitised. The FBR must broaden the tax base instead of burdening compliant taxpayers; provinces must effectively implement agriculture income and property taxes. Energy and wheat markets must be made more competitive, with BISP protection for poor households. Restructuring and privatising SOEs, reforming the Export Facilitation Scheme, implementing the National Tariff Policy, and expanding financial access for small businesses, farmers, women entrepreneurs, and housing are equally important. Export financing and insurance must reach new exporters. Delays or reversals will weaken investment, employment, and competitiveness, undermining the stability painstakingly achieved. Long-delayed infrastructure projects must advance — the Karachi-Peshawar ML-1 railway, the Sukkur-Hyderabad motorway, oil pipeline extensions, oil refinery upgrades, the Quetta-Zhob highway, five special industrial zones, and revamping the Karachi Circular Railway. Skills upgrading must complement these investments. Pakistan is midway from crisis management to sustainable growth. Industrial, automobile, and strategic trade policies must be harmonised in consultation with the private sector and announced without delay. A credible implementation framework with clear milestones, assigned duties, and firm timelines is needed. Regular monitoring, inter-agency coordination, and timely bottleneck removal must rep­lace announcements without execution. External risks make the transition more urgent. The US-Iran war, disruptions to energy flows through the Strait of Hormuz, and instability in Bab el-Mandeb can sharply raise import costs, intensifying uncertainty over inflation, the current account and exchange rate. Suspension of the Indus Waters Treaty compounds water insecurity. Meanwhile, AI is transforming labour markets, creating uncertainty about job opportunities. Against this backdrop, three domestic sources of disruption demand particular attention. First, security in KP and Balochistan must be restored through dialogue and an effective counterterrorism strategy. Poverty and conflict reinforce each other. Reversals in poverty reduction are linked to conflict-affected districts, where social indicators are among the weakest nationally. The NFC framework should give greater weight to disadvantaged districts, alongside empowered local governments (LGs). Federal and provincial governments must jointly own a practical CT plan. The apex committees set up after 2014 showed the value of coordinated action in restoring peace. Imposing emergency and governor’s rule in KP could, instead, intensify political tensions and worsen law and order amid terrorist attacks. Economic relief, jobs, and better public services must complement security operations. Second, the movement of goods and services ac­ross the country must be uninterrupted. LEAs re­­quisitioning containers, trucks, and oil tankers disrupt trade. Imports must reach factories on sc­h­­­­­­edule, exporters must honour delivery comm­it­m­­ents, and farmers must transport perishable pro­­duce to urban markets without delay. Supply disruptions raise prices and create household shortages. Internet shutdowns are equally damaging, disrupting payments, business transactions, logist­ics, information flows, and the work of freelancers and firms exporting IT and IT-enabled services. Economic activity requires predictable rules and uninterrupted movement of goods, people, and information. Administrative measures intended to address immediate security concerns must not impose disproportionate costs on the economy. Third, the debate over new provinces/ administrative units shouldn’t become another source of instability. It’s a contentious proposal; the Sindh Assembly has opposed dividing the province. Administrative reorganisation requires constitutional clarity, fiscal assessment, and political consensus. Failing to do so would divert scarce resources to new bureaucracies, increase administrative expenditure, and weaken provincial cash surpluses transferred to the centre. Fiscal consolidation will then suffer, jeopardising macroeconomic stability. Strengthening LGs and improving service delivery within existing arrangements should take precedence. Pakistan is midway through a demanding transition from crisis management to sustainable growth. Political leaders hope that the present IMF programme will be the country’s last. Fulfilling that aim needs continuity, discipline, and determined implementation. External shocks can’t be controlled, but their impact can be mitigated through sound policies and domestic consensus. The priority is clear: restore peace through dialogue, keep goods and services moving, complete reforms already initiated, and avoid divisive experiments. Over the skies of Pakistan, the Afghan Taliban are proving just how low the barrier to aerial warfare has fallen. Five days later, on Sept 28, the Foreign Ministry issued a diplomatic protest note to the Taliban over what it called “provocative incursions”, reporting that 23 drones had entered Pakistani airspace from Afghanistan since the beginning of September. The Taliban have increasingly relied on unmanned aircraft since open conflict erupted between the two neighbours in February. Psychological warfare An examination by France24 of photographs and videos showing drones intercepted by the Pakistan Army revealed that Taliban’s suicide aircraft appear to run on DLE55 engines. Those 55cc single-piston engines are readily available for commercial purchase online. Unmanned aerial vehicle expert Mark Grozel told France24 that these drones measure approximately two metres across and carry between half a kilogram and one kilogram of explosives. Because they fly slowly and at low altitudes, they offer limited military effectiveness and are simple to shoot down once spotted, though detecting them remains a challenge, Grozel said. While a foreign government could be supplying the aircraft, Grozel noted that building a homemade fixed-wing drone is no longer difficult. Autopilot trajectory software is widely accessible, engines and batteries are sold on the e-commerce platform Alibaba, and airframes that are cheap to make can be constructed using 3D printers, polystyrene or balsa wood, according to Grozel. Despite relying on rudimentary radio navigation, basic engines and small payloads, the unmanned aircraft inflict heavy psychological damage. “They allow you to tell your adversary: ‘I can threaten your infrastructure at any moment’. Pakistan accuses the Afghan Taliban of harbouring and aiding the TTP, which has waged an insurgency inside Pakistan for nearly 20 years. Possessing aerial superiority, Pakistan has struck suspected TTP hideouts deep within Afghan territory. The Afghan Taliban have answered such strikes with ground assaults and drone attacks. The question before the FCC was whether the respondents qualified as police officials entitled to the allowance under the relevant laws or whether a distinction existed between uniformed police personnel and administrative/ministerial staff. The respondents, who were employees of the KP Police establishment, claimed entitlement to risk allowance on the grounds that it was being paid to other employees and staff of the department and that denying them the benefit amounted to discriminatory treatment in violation of Article 25 of the Constitution, which guarantees equality. After the police department denied the grant of the allowance, the respondents challenged the decision before the PHC, which allowed their petition. In its judgement, the FCC observed that administrative/ministerial staff had their own set of rules governing their terms and conditions of service. Under the relevant rules, the FCC stated, such employees had their own method of recruitment, eligibility and composition of service. “Nowhere was it provided that a person appointed as ministerial staff under the rules will be expected to perform active police service,” the judgement said. It added that it was a settled constitutional principle that the guarantee of equality did not mean that all persons must invariably be treated alike, irrespective of the nature of their duties, responsibilities, classifications or conditions of service. The court observed that while Article 25 of the Constitution prohibited discrimination, it did not prevent reasonable classification based on “intelligible differentia having a rational nexus to the object”. “In the case at hand, there exists a clear distinction between uniformed police personnel performing active and operational duties and employees forming part of ministerial establishment,” the court noted, adding that the former may be required to perform duties involving physical danger, law and order operations, arrests, raids, security assignments, crime control and other functions associated with active policing, whereas the latter performed administrative, clerical and ministerial functions.
