ISLAMABAD: The Pakistan Information Commission (PIC) has directed the National Assembly Secretariat to provide detailed information regarding expenditures, travel allowances, perks and privileges of chairpersons and members of parliamentary standing committees and subcommittees. In the order, available with Dawn, the PIC directed the secretary of the NA Secretariat to provide clear and specific responses to 13 outstanding questions under the Right of Access to Information Act 2017 (RTI Act) within 10 days. As per the order, Mazhar had submitted an information request on June 21, seeking details about the functioning and financial expenditures of parliamentary committees from January 2025 onwards. She had requested lists of standing committees and subcommittees, the number of meetings conducted, total expenditure incurred on those meetings and travel allowances paid to committee members and chairpersons, as well as the complete details of perks, privileges and other payments made to lawmakers serving on these committees. After receiving no satisfactory response from the NA Secretariat, she filed an appeal with the PIC on July 5. National Assembly Litigation Officer Sehrish Mushtaq represented the Secretariat during the proceedings. The NA Secretariat submitted responses which were shared with the applicant, but she maintained that, except for the first question concerning the list of standing committees, the remaining 13 questions remained unanswered. After examining the responses and information available on the NA’s official website, the PIC acknowledged that substantial parliamentary information had been published online but observed that it did not adequately address the journalist’s specific questions. “The information provided does not meet the specific requirements set out in the information request,” the PIC stated, stressing that public bodies must provide objective and specific responses to information requests in accordance with the RTI Act, even where relevant information is already available online. The implementation proceedings have been scheduled for Nov 19 to review compliance with the directive. Homes repeatedly turn into hell for children as our criminal justice system is unequipped to intercept and prevent attacks within the home. Statutes such as the ICT and Sindh Child Protection Acts create institutional bodies that address minors’ welfare, but they lack the authority to adopt anticipatory, prompt, and intrusive measures to ensure child safety. Our laws are reactive rather than anticipatory. He said that the premier had introduced a tariff policy, the purpose of which was to lower the cost of production and the import bill of raw materials. “You will see that prices of cars will come down … the manufacturing cost of automakers will come down; these were the prime minister’s specific instructions. The prices of all vehicles, except luxury vehicles, will come down,” Akhtar said. He explained that prices would come down gradually as the duties for completely built units (CBUs), assembly plants and components would be reduced each year, eventually leading to competitive tariffs after five years. The PM’s industries aide said localisation had been made mandatory, with targets to be given to manufacturers. He added that a “rewards and penalty” system will also be introduced to boost exports, under which exporters will get 5 per cent Duty Drawback on Local Taxes and Levies (DLTL), with an additional 10pc as exports increase. “But if you do not achieve the targets, a penalty will be imposed as well,” he warned, adding that car manufacturers will not get DLTL unless they achieve the entire export target as well as their local manufacturing target. Sales tax on the equipment for charging stations has been reduced to 1pc, while customs duty has been removed. This principle is also “reverberated across the world with one voice by dint of a legal maxim ‘Vigilantibus et non dormientibus jura subveniunt’, thereby implying that the law assists the vigilant, not those who sleep over their rights”, observed Justice Muhammad Ali Mazhar in an eight-page judgment issued on Saturday, which restored two civil revisions that were dismissed for non-prosecution after the petitioner’s counsel could not appear due to Coronavirus infection. A three-member bench, headed by Justice Mazhar and including Justices Musarrat Hilali and Justice Shahid Bilal Hassan, allowed the civil petitions by converting them into appeals and setting aside the Feb 23, 2026 Lahore High Court’s (LHC) rejection of the restoration applications. Last September as well, the SC, while rejecting three civil miscellaneous applications seeking the restoration of review petitions, regretted that litigants and their counsel sometimes treat the court’s benevolence and leniency as a mere procedural formality, disregarding the solemnity of its categorical directives. The present issue arose when the petitioner, Safdar Ali Butt, filed a case before the LHC, but on Feb 2, 2026, both revisions were dismissed for non-prosecution because the petitioner’s counsel, Shahid Azeem Bajwa, could not reach the bench despite being present at the court as he was suffering from Coronavirus. Upon being called, the counsel rushed inside but could not approach the bench due to the crowd in the courtroom. Demonstrating diligence, the petitioner then filed restoration applications the very next day on Feb 3, but these were also dismissed by the LHC on Feb 23, prompting the present appeal to the SC. On Saturday, Justice Mazhar emphasised that, if an advocate lacks a sense of his professional duty, the ultimate sufferer was the client who engaged the advocate on reposing confidence in him. At the same time, Justice Mazhar highlighted that the client should also be vigilant and pursue his case diligently without leaving everything to the lawyer and should not opt to put out of sight or play hide-and-seek tactics based on an advocate’s inattention or negligence. “To ensure effective performance of professional ethics and further to avoid allegations of professional misconduct or violation of canons of practice, the lawyers ought to properly track fixation dates, cause lists and court benches to avoid missing cases due to human error. “They should also make all best possible efforts to handle every facet and dimension of the lis to protect his client’s interests,” the judgement emphasised. The lawyer should also keep his client updated on case progress, risks and even the settlement offers, if any and also avoid conflicts of interest, Justice Mazhar reminded. “In case of overlapping and simultaneous commitments in different courts of law, advocates ought to make alternate arrangements for holding brief even for adjournment requests or must file proper application for adjournment in advance with prior information to the other side’s counsel as a matter of professional ethics,” Justice Mazhar explained. “Sometimes the court dismisses the lawsuit when, despite various opportunities afforded by the court to proceed with the case or producing evidence, the party and his counsel persistently and calculatingly disappear, either due to lack of interest or to drag the proceedings to avoid well-timed disposal on merits,” regretted the judgment. Dismissal in default of a case on the negligence of an advocate without well-grounded sanity is one of the most serious blemishes which disparage and tarnish the professional image and reputation, the judgment highlighted. “Universally, a dismissal due to non-prosecution is considered a plainspoken ineptitude of the lawyers and also a breach of clients’ trust, which also sets the stage for filing complaints against the lawyers alleging professional misconduct to the Bar Councils under the Legal Practitioners and Bar Councils Act, 1973,” Justice Mazhar emphasised. At the same time, Justice Mazhar also observed that courts cannot turn a blind eye to the well-settled doctrine of restoration of a case, which gives carte blanche to courts to resuscitate the lawsuit dismissed in default if sufficient cause is put on record and the demands of justice so demand. “If the sufficient cause is pleaded through a promptly filed application for restoration within the limitation period and satisfactorily explains the absence on the date of hearing, the eventual consensus of the courts across the board is to lean toward compassion,” Justice Mazhar said. “The all-encompassing philosophy of justice articulates that lawsuits must be decided on merits rather than technical knockouts or due to procedural oversights or inadvertences.” Albeit the benefit of a lenient approach cannot be regarded as absolute, he added. Justice Mazhar emphasised that the “scale of justice neither tilts towards automatic dismissal nor automatic restoration, but it should be with a balanced frame of mind and also subject to the law of limitation to ward off and protect a genuine litigant from suffering due to counsel’s blunders, while delineating a strict dividing line against premeditated recklessness and genuine and honest grounds pleaded for restoration of the case to its original position”. He observed that force majeure circumstances or unexpected situations, including physical incapacitation or medical emergencies, are ordinarily acknowledged as convincing grounds to exercise inherent or discretionary powers by courts for restoring cases to “meet the ends of justice with or without imposing costs”. This was because the “indispensable purpose of a court is to determine the actual disputes as an elementary virtue of the administration of civil and criminal justice”. “The rules of procedure are meant to serve justice and not obstruct it,” the judgment concluded.

Welcoming the secretary-general’s review of peacekeeping operations, Pakistan outlined six priorities for reform: giving primacy to political solutions; ensuring focused and adequately resourced mandates; preserving the institutional strengths of peacekeeping; preparing for future military and police requirements; improving the safety and security of peacekeepers; and maintaining peacekeeping as a collective UN responsibility. All four members of the regulator unanimously held the Power Division and its subordinate entities responsible for failing to fulfil their legal obligations within the prescribed timelines, pushing Nepra into an unprecedented deadlock and potentially increasing costs for electricity consumers across the country. At the centre of the controversy was an incremental support package (ISP) of Rs22.98 per unit introduced for industrial consumers in December last year. Industrialists have been protesting against the package since May, seeking its suspension on the grounds that it had failed to benefit them and was instead increasing their electricity bills. The package was due for a review after the completion of its first six months in June, based on the lessons learnt during its implementation. Following a belated request from the Power Division, despite repeated reminders from the regulator, Nepra on Friday revised the ISP rate to Rs25.30 per unit. The decision was strongly opposed by its two most relevant members, those responsible for tariff and development matters. Interestingly, Nepra Chairman Waseem Mukhtar fully endorsed the written arguments and positions taken by the dissenting members but subsequently exercised his special casting vote to approve the revised rate of Rs25.30 per unit instead of retaining the existing rate of Rs22.98. The authority was divided 2-2 on the matter. Member Tariff Amina Ahmed and Member Development Maqsood Anwar, in a joint dissenting note, maintained that even the proposed revised rate of Rs25.30 per unit was a “random number” unsupported by evidence. They said Nepra’s own institutional calculations put the marginal cost at Rs31.83 per unit rather than Rs25.30. “The revised incremental rate of Rs25.30 per unit proposed by the Ministry of Energy (MoE), and allowed by the honourable Chairman and Member (Law), is based on the generation mix apparently assumed at the design stage of the package, with updated fuel prices,” the two members observed. They called for the immediate suspension of the ISP until authentic data became available. The dissenting members argued that compliance with the federal cabinet’s decision and Nepra’s own directives required a reassessment of the existing rate of Rs22.98 per unit to determine whether it should be retained, revised or suspended. However, this exercise had not been carried out, resulting in the continuation of the package at a rate below the actual cost of supplying incremental electricity. “The resulting misalignment has had implications for consumers in two ways. First, to the extent incremental consumption increased the system’s average fuel cost, the resulting impact was recovered from all consumers through the FCA mechanism. Second, because incremental units are excluded from quarterly tariff adjustments, the exclusion of such units increases the quarterly adjustment borne by consumers not availing the package,” they said. The two members, therefore, recommended the temporary suspension of the tariff package rather than choosing the proposed rate of Rs25.30 or above per unit. They questioned how a package based on the principle of cost-reflectivity could continue without a validated cost-reflective rate. They argued that since the alternative rate had no credible link with the actual marginal cost, continuation of the package was no longer justified. They said other consumers should not be made to bear the cost of a review that the MoE was required to submit five months ago but had yet to provide. In his casting vote, Nepra Chairman Waseem Mukhtar observed that the dissenting members’ conclusions “need serious consideration”. However, he said suspending the package at this stage would not be appropriate and that the Ministry of Energy should be given another opportunity to provide the information repeatedly sought by the regulator, including in its latest determination. All four members agreed that the Power Division was required to submit revised calculations six months ago but had failed to do so despite repeated reminders from Nepra. They also regretted that the ministry had not responded adequately to the questions and queries raised by the regulator. “The marginal price, in proper form, was not provided to the regulator by either the Independent System and Market Operator (ISMO) or the Power Planning and Monitoring Company (PPMC), which were duty-bound to do so. LHC rejects plea for directions to legislation on marital rape

LAHORE: The Lahore High Court (LHC) has dismissed a public interest petition seeking directions to the federal and provincial governments to legislate on marital rape, holding that a constitutional court cannot compel the legislature to enact a particular law or create a new offence. Justice Abher Gul Khan clarified that the ruling neither endorses violence or abuse within marriage, nor prevents the Parliament or any provincial assembly from taking up the matter. Areej Sohail Bhutta, a lawyer, filed the petition, stating that she had encountered women clients who complained of non-consensual sexual conduct within their marriages. She argued that the law lacked a distinct statutory framework for sexual violence within marriage. She sought directions for legislative measures, including amendments to sections 375 and 376 of the Pakistan Penal Code. A court can’t force the legislature to enact a law or create an offence, observes the judge Justice Khan observed that the Constitution divides powers among the legislature, the executive and the judiciary, and that judicial review does not authorise courts to assume legislative functions. She said Article 199 empowers the high court to issue directions for the enforcement of fundamental rights. However, she maintained, there is a distinction between directing an authority to perform an existing constitutional or statutory obligation and compelling the legislature to enact a particular law or create a new offence. The judge noted that articles 9, 14 and 25 protect the security, dignity and equality of a person, while articles 25(3) and 34 allow special protections for women and promote their participation in national life. She said Article 35, which requires the state to protect marriage and the family, does not provide a licence for violence against a spouse. Justice Khan observed that foreign laws can provide comparative guidance, but cannot override Pakistan’s Constitution or replace the judgment of its legislature. She said any proposed legal framework also has to be examined in the light of the Constitution, existing laws and the injunctions of Islam. The judge noted that under Article 142(b), the Parliament and provincial assemblies are competent to legislate on criminal law, procedure and evidence. “The question whether an additional offence, a separate statutory provision or a further protective mechanism is required, is essentially a matter of legislative policy,” the judge observed. Citing Supreme Court judgments, including Tika Iqbal Muhammad Khan versus Pervez Musharraf (PLJ 2008 SC 500), the judge observed, “A court cannot issue a mandamus compelling the legislature to enact a particular law.” The judge noted that the petitioner had neither challenged a specific statutory provision as unconstitutional nor identified an authority’s failure to perform an existing legal duty. She said the relief sought would require the court to direct legislation according to a model proposed by the petitioner, amounting to interference in the legislative process. The judge stressed that the decision does not diminish a woman’s dignity or bodily integrity because of marriage, or endorse coercion and abuse within marriage.
