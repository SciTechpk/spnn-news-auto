Few chances to play The shortage of play is not limited to football. One report suggests children in Pakistan get less time for play at school than their counterparts elsewhere in South Asia. At a recent training of 57 master trainers organised by the PFF under its Football in Schools initiative, fewer than 20pc came from girls’ schools. Outside school, football and futsal compete with property developers and other sports for scarce space. Where grounds are available, boys are often given priority. Women’s sport also ranks low among social priorities and still meets disapproval in many quarters. “For a young girl, external voices are so loud, it’s hard to stand in your own space,” a former national player said. No clear pathway Even for girls who play at school, in academies or at clubs, the road ahead is murky, and parents may feel the time and money are being wasted. “Parents often ask, ‘khel kar kya karay gee?’” the same player recalled. Without a visible competitive ladder, coaches have to get creative to keep players, and their families, motivated. They lean on the few national competitions that exist and the chance of university sports scholarships. “All of my girls want to play at the highest level that Pakistan has to offer,” said a youth women’s coach. Talented younger girls are sometimes moved into older age groups, where they face physically more developed senior women. That can spur some on, but others lose heart when ambition collides with physical reality. Results over development Players picked for the national team get inconsistent coaching. Training tends to be dictated by ad hoc international fixtures, and players rarely get the chance to train and play together regularly, leaving them short of the match experience and cohesion needed at the international level. A parallel and equally opaque route exists for diaspora players, who live and train abroad and are brought into the national side without necessarily featuring in domestic competitions or camps. This leaves domestic players unsure how selections are made and what they must do to progress. “The only consistency is the inconsistency of how things are done.” — former national player From strategy to action The strategy begins to tackle some of these problems. But it is an ambitious agenda with no clear resource allocation or budget, which raises doubts about delivery. Experience suggests that available funds will decide which activities get priority. To ensure implementation is guided by evidence and players’ needs, rather than the interests of particular lobbies, the PFF should pick a handful of practical priorities for the first phase. Three stand out. First, bring continuity and predictability to governance and competition. The PFF should publish an annual calendar of domestic events across youth, men’s and women’s categories. A reliable calendar would let players, coaches, clubs and families plan ahead, and show that the federation is committed to regular competition. Second, communicate better and be more transparent. The PFF should update its website, make sure the FIFA Connect platform works properly, and align messaging among its officials. Regular social media updates would also help nurture the emerging women’s football ecosystem and make it easier for stakeholders to understand what the federation is doing and how to take part. Third, set up youth national teams for girls. These would create a pipeline to the senior side and give younger players something to aim for. Coaches should be properly trained, and international safeguarding principles must be upheld at all times. Better senior coaching and clearer recruitment routes for the national team, including trained scouts and more systematic links with diaspora players, could lift Pakistan’s international performance. But such long-term gains rest on a strong foundation: more girls playing regularly, with real chances to develop. The immediate focus should be on building the game from the bottom up. “We want to ensure that more girls play football from a young age,” a PFF official said. The hurdles facing girls also differ across urban, semi-urban and rural settings. The social barriers for a girl in rural Pano Aqil are not the same as the access problems confronting one in inner-city Orangi or Saddar. The PFF’s outreach must recognise this and ensure opportunities are distributed fairly. Players in action during a Gilgit-Baltistan Girls Football League match in Passu, Hunza in 2022. The missing link: a league Management, coaches and players all identify the same gap: there is no proper development structure built around an established women’s league. The strategy addresses this directly. The PFF’s SHE League (under-23) and SHE League Juniors (under-15), played in Islamabad in October, show the federation is trying to build some structure. But details on the design and frequency of the competitions remain thin. A well-run league does more than produce a winner. It gives players regular matches, helps coaches assess performance, and provides a framework for developing and identifying talent. What role the PFF should play in organising, regulating and supporting women’s club football is still an open question. Neighbouring countries offer different models. The Bangladesh Football Federation (BFF) has relied on a national women’s league of roughly 11 clubs. Reports have raised concerns about its competitiveness, including claims that the concentration of national team players at one better-resourced club has put other clubs off. The BFF’s licensing rules also require men’s clubs to have a youth women’s team in official competitions, a requirement that can link club football to national-team development if properly implemented and monitored. The All India Football Federation (AIFF) has a more tiered system, with the Indian Women’s League (IWL) at the top, a second division, and U-15 and U-17 leagues. State and regional competitions add further levels at which players and teams can progress. In September 2026, the AIFF announced a 15-year partnership with Capri Sports to strengthen the IWL and IWL 2. Here, the federation organises and regulates competitions, clubs manage their teams, and a commercial partner is expected to develop the league’s revenue potential. The Football Federation of the Islamic Republic of Iran (FFIRI) plays a direct role in organising and regulating women’s club football. The league has three divisions and is run by the Iran Football League Organization, which operates under the FFIRI, placing domestic competition within a wider framework for player development and talent identification. However, restrictions on spectators and media coverage limit the visibility, and commercial potential, of the women’s game. These examples show the range of choices open to the PFF as it defines its own role and those of clubs and commercial partners. Several questions must be answered first. Does it have the capacity to oversee or run such a league? What would it cost, and how would it be financed? Which clubs would take part, and what resources could they draw on? Should there be youth and premier divisions? How would players’ health and safety be ensured? Answering these will take time. In the interim, clubs and regional associations must register with FIFA Connect, organise women’s matches and keep raising public awareness. The women’s game cannot be left to the PFF alone. Players, parents, clubs, academies, private-sector funders, men’s clubs and affiliated associations all have a part to play. Turning ambition into progress Regular leagues for youth, women and men are essential to expanding competition and building a sustainable football ecosystem. Initiatives such as the SHE League, FIFA Connect and Football in Schools can help identify stakeholders and sharpen coaching skills. But they cannot substitute for sustained institutional commitment from the PFF. Until the federation shows that women’s football is a priority, and that the priority will last, these initiatives may not be enough to repair the trust eroded among generations of Pakistani players and coaches by repeated governance breakdowns. As one former player put it: “The only consistency is the inconsistency of how things are done.” Perhaps it is time that changed.
