Talking about the exchange rate

THERE is an enduring consensus in Pakistan that the exchange rate should not be allowed to move by much as a matter of routine, and that determining its level is a matter of discretion for the state. In some areas we call this “Dar-o-nomics”, after Ishaq Dar, whose name came to be attached to exchange rate policy not because he invented it, but because he has been its most important and visible advocate. Otherwise this thinking dates back to Ghulam Ishaq Khan, who was of the mind that the state should liberalise the economy in such a way that it does not lose its commanding position as the arbiter of all economic outcomes. From deciding the exchange rate, to deciding who gets to make how much profit and what should be the normal indexed rate of return on private capital in the economy, GIK wanted a model of liberalisation that kept all these decisions firmly in the hands of the government. Nowhere is this thinking more clearly stated, and perhaps to more devastating effect, than around how it treats the question of the exchange rate. Over the years many have given voice to this thinking in different ways, and their arguments cluster around three basic assertions. One, devaluations lead to a hike in the rupee-denominated debt service burden. Two, devaluations do nothing to boost exports, they only provide a short-lived impetus that is quickly frittered away as costs of imported inputs rise accordingly, and exporters are forced to pass the price benefit on to their buyers. Three, devaluation causes inflation that burdens the poor disproportionately. None of these assertions is wrong. What is wrong, however, is deploying them in defence of a fixed exchange rate. What all those who have used these assertions over the years have failed to do is actually study Pakistan’s own episodes of exchange rate devaluation. Were they to do that, they would realise that devaluations in our own history have nothing to do with restoring export competitiveness or anything quite so grand. In truth, devaluations are forced upon us because the supply of dollars relative to the supply of rupees in the economy shrinks to a point where a mass stampede breaks out with everyone rushing for the exits. Devaluations have to be undertaken to reflect the new reality of supply and demand of FX liquidity, and to halt the stampede. That’s all. To all those who want to talk about the exchange rate, I ask them to study three episodes from Pakistan’s own history when devaluations happened. The first was in 2007 and ran till late 2008. The second began in late 2017 and ran till 2019. The third episode began in the middle of 2021 and ran till the middle of 2023. These three cycles teach us everything we need to know about exchange rate management. Devaluations in our own history have nothing to do with restoring export competitiveness or anything quite so grand. Each episode happened when Pakistan’s foreign exchange reserves were depleting fast in the face of skyrocketing import and debt service bills. In each episode the government chose to first hold the ground and finance the outflows using borrowed money. In each episode they hit a point where further financing was insufficient to meet the pace of the reserve erosion. And in each episode the government hit a point where they had no choice but to reprice the dollar to reflect the new reality of its supply versus demand. In short, in each episode the government chose to drive blindly towards a cliff. So the question to ask is what they were telling themselves along the way. The pattern is the same every time. Reserves peak, the government of the day boasts about having built “record high reserves”, and then the trade deficit begins to swell. For a few months remittances cover the trade deficit so there is no urgency to act. Then the current account swings into deficit and remains there, and then the deficits begin to grow. The government borrows to replenish the reserves. The borrowing hits a limit, the current account deficit marches along, and reserve depletion accelerates. What are they telling themselves during these times, when the pressure is mounting but has not yet reached catastrophic proportions? It has been my unpleasant duty to have covered all three of these cycles over the past two decades, and I can confirm that in each episode they tell themselves the same thing. First, that the current account deficits are temporary, or driven by one-off factors. When the reserve erosion begins to trigger a flight into the dollars, they argue that administrative measures are required to stem the outflows and bring stability back. When they are advised to reprice the dollar before things get out of hand, they respond that exchange rate adjustments do nothing to increase exports, while they produce harm for the country in the form of higher debt service bills and inflation. Eventually they all succumb. They succumb for the simple reason that you cannot endlessly spend money that is not yours to spend. And when they succumb, they grumble and complain. But they never derive any lessons from what they have just lived through, and what they have just seen happen in front of their eyes. What is it they say about those who fail to learn from their own history? They are doomed to repeat it. This is why we have been through this cycle three times already. And yet, today, one more time, we can see people in positions of power, once again, start to repeat the same old nonsense all over again. Once again people who have no idea of what Pakistan’s experience with the exchange rate has been are trying to build arguments on how to manage it. And once again, they are trotting out the same tired old tropes, thus setting the stage for round four of this unhappy roller coaster for yet one more ride. EXPLAINER-Makkah pact and defence of the Kingdom

THE Makkah alliance has moved, in less than two months, from a political declaration towards practical cooperation, though it remains a few steps short of becoming a fully operational military alliance. Its Strategic Political and Defence Committee, which met in Riyadh on Oct 5, decided “to move immediately to the practical implementation of the collective defence commitments”. The operative part of the decision, however, is narrower than some of the headlines it has generated. The three countries agreed to “take the necessary measures to provide the agreed military forces and capabilities and ensure their rapid deployment in the Kingdom, in accordance with the approved arrangements and the national legislation of the Parties”. The Saudi urgency to see the alliance working is understandable. Through September and into early October, Yemen’s Ansar Allah group, commonly known as the Houthis, fired missiles and drones towards southern Saudi cities and energy facilities, with Riyadh saying the attacks also targeted the holy cities. One such strike hit a power station in Madina supplying Masjid-i-Nabvi. Progress so far includes political commitment, the appointment of an institutional head and formation of the Riyadh secretariat No public authorisation yet for Pakistani or Turkish forces to participate in combat inside Yemen; Turkiye only providing defensive and technical support so far At the same time, the Aden-based Presidential Leadership Council announced a counteroffensive to reverse Ansar Allah gains along the Red Sea coast. Government forces, backed by coalition air support, claimed to have recaptured Mocha and positions near Bab al Mandeb, although those claims remain contested. The Riyadh meeting and developments in Yemen came within a day of each other and are politically linked, even if they are not demonstrably part of the same operational plan. The committee’s statement, importantly, made no reference to Mocha, Bab al Mandeb or operations inside Yemen, and did not name the Houthis. The alliance has nevertheless covered considerable ground since its inception on Aug 7. An inaugural summit was followed by a meeting of the Strategic and Political Committee in Istanbul at the end of August. On Sept 25, the three countries’ military chiefs met in Riyadh and agreed to pursue intelligence sharing, military coordination and capability integration, though they made no mention of a joint command. On the sidelines, Pakistan’s Chief of Defence Forces and Chief of Army Staff Field Marshal Asim Munir and Saudi Defence Minister Prince Khalid bin Salman also discussed continuing “joint efforts” against attacks on the Kingdom. The Oct 5 meeting took the next institutional steps, endorsing retired Lt Gen Nauman Mahmood as the alliance’s first secretary general, placing the Riyadh-based secretariat under his charge and authorising the rapid deployment of military forces and capabilities already “agreed”. The progress so far includes a political commitment, an institutional head and secretariat, two committee meetings, a meeting of military chiefs and a decision to begin implementation. What remains missing is a standing assigned force, a joint commander, publicly defined rules of engagement and a published military doctrine. There is also no public authorisation for Pakistani or Turkish forces to participate in combat operations inside Yemen. In fact, Turkish officials told Reuters on Wednesday that Ankara was “sending mainly defensive and technical support to Saudi Arabia to help it fight the Houthis, adding that the support expected from Turkiye was only in the form of military equipment, intelligence sharing, and weapons systems, and that no other form of deployment had taken place as part of the alliance so far. Between the lines Understanding the alliance requires answers to basic questions that remain unresolved as to what constitutes a threat or an attack, against whom has the alliance been formed, what would trigger a response, and who would decide on action and through which command structure? In the absence of a published charter and doctrine, its working principles have to be pieced together from the August agreement, subsequent government statements and the Oct 5 decision. The threat, in the language used by the parties, is an armed attack on a member’s security, sovereignty or territorial integrity, with particular emphasis on protecting Makkah and Madinah. There is, however, no public definition covering attacks on forces, ships, diplomatic missions or personnel outside national territory. The Houthis are impliedly identified as the attackers, while Iran is widely seen as their enabling power, but neither is named in the agreement. Collective deterrence appears to mean making the commitment credible through military readiness, coordination and the ability to respond. No punishment strategy has been published, while a response seems contingent on a request from the country under threat, agreement on the measures to be taken and compliance with each country’s national laws. Pakistan’s Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar has described the agreement as defensive, without an offensive agenda. Defence Minister Khawaja Asif has said Pakistani and Turkish forces would play a supporting role, including reconnaissance, while Saudi forces conduct combat operations. The Oct 5 decision, which provides for deployment inside the Kingdom rather than a joint campaign, is consistent with this narrower interpretation. The capabilities under discussion, including air and missile defence, reconnaissance, intelligence, counter-drone systems and technical support, also point to a defensive design. Great expectations Yet the gap between the public positions of Pakistan and Türkiye, and Saudi expectations, is already visible. Prince Khalid said after the meeting that the committee had affirmed solidarity “in confronting the sinful aggressions that targeted the Kingdom and the Holy Sanctuaries, activating collective deterrence against these aggressions, and against all those standing behind them”. The reference to Houthi attacks is clear enough, but “all those standing behind them” is a broader Saudi political signal absent from the joint statement and not further defined. Pakistan and Turkiye have maintained that the alliance is not directed against another country. The three partners may not wish to emphasise their differing interests, but these are clear. Riyadh wants protection for its territory and greater pressure on those attacking it, whereas Pakistan wants to honour its commitment to Saudi Arabia without being drawn into another Yemen war or a confrontation with Iran and Türkiye seeks a larger regional security role but faces its own political and legal constraints. The agreement can accommodate these differences as long as its mission remains defensive. Its language is carefully circumscribed as it refers to capabilities already “agreed”, rather than whatever forces Riyadh might request, and specifies deployment inside the Kingdom under national legislation. An Article 5 like commitment that still requires consultation, agreement and action under national law does not amount to an automatic military response. Saudi Arabia can therefore expect defensive reinforcement on its territory, political backing and supporting capabilities for its existing campaign. It cannot yet treat the agreement as an automatic commitment to operations beyond its borders or as a joint military doctrine against unnamed states or groups backing the Houthis. The reference to national legislation is particularly important. In Pakistan, a wider military role could revive debate over the unanimous parliamentary resolution of April 2015, which called for neutrality in the Yemen conflict while pledging that Pakistan would stand shoulder to shoulder with Saudi Arabia if its territorial integrity or the two Holy Mosques came under threat. A deployment to defend Saudi territory and the holy sites, including the Pakistani presence already there, can be presented as consistent with that resolution. Participation in fighting around Mocha, however, would be much harder to reconcile with its language. Any move beyond defensive deployment could therefore require fresh political authorisation, raising questions about how far the government could depart from the earlier parliamentary position. Turkiye faces a different constraint as Article 92 of its constitution requires parliamentary approval for deploying troops abroad, and no such approval has been publicly reported. Equipment, technical teams and specialists can move faster than military units, allowing Ankara to contribute within a legal process different from Pakistan’s. Previous pacts The trilateral framework is also building on, rather than replacing, the existing Pakistan-Saudi military relationship. Under the 2025 Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement, Pakistani air and support elements were already deployed in the Kingdom. This was possibly what Inter-Services Public Relations Director General Lieutenant General Ahmed Sharif Chaudhry meant when he recently told a CNN interviewer that Pakistani troops have been in the Kingdom “for decades”, and across multiple domains. The understanding between Field Marshal Munir and Prince Khalid to continue cooperation against the attacks kept that bilateral channel active while aligning it more closely with the Makkah Alliance. The additional capabilities envisaged under the Oct 5 decision are more likely to involve air defence, intelligence, reconnaissance and specialist support than a large Pakistani ground contingent for Yemen, which would sit uneasily with both the 2015 resolution and the government’s stated position. The next test is how far implementation goes and whether additional Pakistani air defence or air assets are moved to Saudi Arabia, whether Turkish systems and specialists arrive, and whether the supporting role remains confined to Saudi territory. Any extension into fighting along the Red Sea coast would turn the difference between the agreed text and Riyadh’s wider interpretation of “all those standing behind them” into a substantive issue. There is also a practical obstacle to an integrated defence alliance, which is that interoperability remains undeveloped. The three militaries operate different equipment ecosystems, and there is no public evidence of a common air picture, joint fusion centre or system through which a Turkish or Pakistani sensor can routinely cue a Saudi weapon. Intelligence cooperation has indeed been agreed, but cooperation does not in itself mean integration. Chain of custody

LAST month, at the London School of Economics’ Environment Week, the International Growth Centre asked a roundtable of policymakers, researchers and funders how evidence systems should change to support harder climate choices. I opened with a case: a major climate decision Pakistan took without the evidence it needed. The obvious case is the 2022 floods. The obvious diagnosis is that the evidence did not exist. The case is obviously right. The diagnosis is obviously wrong. When a third of the country went under water, every decision that followed, from evacuation to the Geneva appeal, was taken in the dark. Yet the Met department had forecast an above-normal monsoon. The national socio-economic registry held geo-coded records on 38 million households. River and satellite data sat with Wapda, the Federal Flood Commission and Suparco. The knowledge existed, in different buildings. No one was mandated to bring it to the table. Pakistan does not have a data shortage. The usual verdict is poor coordination. That is the wrong word. Coordination is voluntary. What Pakistan lacks is a chain of custody: someone legally obliged to carry evidence to the point of decision. The five federal agencies that hold hazard data share no protocol. The roundtable framed three tensions: whether evidence serves the decisions governments actually take, whether it arrives in time to be used, and whether it supports implementation or only design. In Pakistan, each tension marks a break in that chain. Each break has a price tag. Evidence and decision: Pakistan’s research agenda follows what funders find easy to finance, not what the state must prioritise. A warning system has four parts: knowing the risk, forecasting it, getting the message out, and making people act on it. Since before the 2022 floods, the money has gone to the first two. Radars can be procured, counted and inaugurated. Dissemination and response are institutional work. They are not as lucrative as procurement tenders. Nor has the state compiled basic baselines: tested district plans, embankment condition, maintenance costs. That absence is political economy. The reason is elite capture: new schemes bring contracts, win constituencies and visibility. Maintenance brings none. Speed and usability: Pakistan models slowly what it needs fast and leaves its fastest evidence unconnected. The five federal agencies that hold hazard data share no protocol. In Buner in August 2025, a red alert was in force and households still did not move. No one told them what to do, and local governments had no assigned role in last-mile delivery. An SMS that says a flood is coming is information, not a warning for immediate action. The BISP registry is a simpler case. Laid over hazard maps, it could tell a provincial authority within hours who stands in the path of a surge and how to pay them. It is the most decision-ready evidence Pakistan owns. It costs nothing new to produce. Usable evidence in Pakistan rarely needs a better model. It needs a legal duty to share and an interface between databases that already exist. Implementation and capability: This is where costs are highest and evidence thinnest. After 2022, cash transfers were the state’s fastest instrument. They reached only households already on the registry. Exclusion ran to a third in some districts, and nearly half the unregistered did not know how to register. The design was sound. No one was tasked with the evidence that would have exposed the gap during delivery. Damage assessment has no owner either. No platform links damage to recovery, and there is no loss and damage registry. The 2022 needs assessment was built for the Geneva appeal, not the reconstruction. Pakistan chaired the G77 when the loss and damage fund was agreed at COP27. A country that cannot document its losses will struggle to draw on the fund it fought to create. After the 18th Amendment, disaster management and irrigation are provincial; hydromet data, the registry and international finance are federal. Evidence must cross that line twice: up for financing, down for delivery. When a donor project closes, its capability leaves with it, because the analysts sat in a project unit, not a line department with a budget head. The cost of moving blind: These are not administrative inefficiencies. They are why Pakistan’s development agenda is now set by its flood cycle rather than its plans. The 2010, 2022 and 2025 floods each erased growth, reopened IMF negotiations and diverted development money into rehabilitation and reconstruction. Every rupee spent rebuilding what better evidence could have protected is a rupee taken from schools, clinics and the investment that would shrink the next shock. The logic is simple: the Global Commission on Adaptation estimates that 24 hours’ warning of a coming storm can cut the damage by 30pc. High-tech or grassroots: My notes for the roundtable ended with a question for funders: should a country like Pakistan invest in predictive modelling or in grassroots readiness? It is a false choice. The answer is sequence. Better models will lengthen lead times and should be pursued. But a 72-hour forecast is worthless to a household off the registry, in a town with no local government, receiving a non-specific message. The next rupee should buy a messenger, not a model. Moving forward: Three steps, in sequence. First, assign ownership. Notify a binding data-sharing protocol across federal and provincial agencies, and name one owner for assessment data. Informal sharing must become a legal duty. Second, connect what exists before the next monsoon. Link the registry to hazard maps in every province. Compile the missing baselines and open a loss and damage registry. Give local governments a formal role in warnings. Third, commission evidence to decisions. Funders, including those around that table in London, should finance all four parts of the warning chain, not the two that are easiest to procure, and embed analysts in provincial departments under budget heads that outlive the project. Pakistan does not lack evidence. It lacks a chain of custody. Until someone is responsible for carrying knowledge to the point of decision, every flood will end with the same verdict: the data existed, and no one was duty-bound to use it. Marriage can be dissolved on a woman's initiative on grounds of shiqaq even if cruelty is not proven: SC

ISLAMABAD: The Supreme Court ruled on Wednesday that a marriage could be dissolved on grounds of shiqaq (marital discord) under a residuary provision of the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939 even when cruelty was not proven. The residuary provision of the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 2(ix), explains the grounds on which a woman can obtain a decree for the dissolution of her marriage. In its judgement, the SC stated: “A marriage may be dissolved by a court, but neither spouse should be judicially deprived of a lawful right, nor judicially burdened with a stigma, without the foundation of law and evidence.” The judgement was issued by a five-member bench, headed by Justice Shahid Waheed and comprising Justices Naeem Akhter Afghan, Muhammad Hashim Khan Kakar, Ishtiaq Ibrahim and Miangul Hassan Aurangzeb. The judges had taken up two connected petitions, filed by Zainab Bibi and Imran Naz Begum, against separate orders of the Peshawar High Court (PHC). Both cases arose from family suits where wives sought dissolution of marriage on the ground of cruelty and claimed maintenance, dower and dowry articles. The family court found cruelty unproven in both cases, but dissolved both marriages through khula — a woman’s right to seek release from a marriage. The family courts also directed the two women to return their dower, while the appellate court and the PHC largely upheld these decrees. The two women raised two principal grievances before the SC: first, that their marriages could not lawfully be dissolved through khula because they neither sought nor consented to it, as their cases were founded on cruelty; and second, their financial claims should have been allowed in full. The SC’s 15-page judgement on their please, authored by Justice Waheed, drew a distinction between khula and shiqaq and explained that khula proceeded from a wife’s voluntary election to obtain release from the marriage and ordinarily carried financial consequences. Shiqaq, by contrast, arose from an objectively established condition of serious matrimonial breach and did not depended on a wife’s surrender of dower or any benefit as consideration for her release, the judgement stated. The SC held that family courts committed an error in converting the petitioners’ claims for the dissolution of their marriages on the ground of cruelty into decrees of khula without obtaining their clear and voluntary consent or election. Those decrees, to the extent that they rested upon khula, could not be sustained, the judgement said, though the SC did not set aside the dissolution altogether. “The matrimonial relationships have undergone a serious and continuing rupture; reconciliation has failed; the evidence does not establish the statutory ground of cruelty; and the record, taken as a whole, establishes a condition of ‘shiqaq’ of the kind contemplated by verse 35 of Surah An-Nisa of the Holy Quran and recognised under Muslim law, thereby bringing the cases within the residuary provision contained in section 2(ix) of the Act of 1939,” the SC explained. The dissolution could therefore lawfully be sustained, but upon a juridical basis different from that adopted by the family courts, the SC judgement said, adding that marriages of the petitioners were liable to dissolution on the ground of shiqaq falling within section 2(ix) of the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939, and not on the basis of khula. However, the directions requiring the petitioners to return, surrender or waive their dower merely as a consequence of khula should consequently stood set aside, the SC ruled. Their entitlement to dower would be determined in accordance with the Act of 1939, it declared. The SC observed that the Quranic emphasis upon reconciliation in cases of shiqaq was meaningful precisely because it contemplated the possibility that reconciliation may fail. “The law should neither rush towards separation nor prolong a matrimonial relationship which has ceased to serve its lawful purposes,” the judgement held. Where reconciliation had failed, and the relationship had reached the condition of shiqaq, the judicial task was to bring the matter to a lawful conclusion without unnecessary attribution of blame or deprivation of rights, the judgement explained. The SC emphasised that courts must distinguish between an unhappy marriage and one in a state of shiqaq. “Mere incompatibility, occasional disagreement, anger, short separation or the existence of matrimonial litigation cannot, by themselves, satisfy the test. The courtroom should not become an institution through which matrimonial discord is converted into a permanent injury to the reputation and dignity of either spouse,” it explained. The judgement further stated: “The law must determine rights; it need not aggravate human conflict. Conversely, where the evidence establishes that the matrimonial relationship itself has descended into a state of serious and continuing rupture, the absence of proof of a particular matrimonial wrong does not necessarily require parties to remain bound to one another indefinitely.” The judgement explained that shiqaq was not established by every quarrel, disagreement, incompatibility or temporary estrangement in a marriage. “It requires a genuine and continuing rupture of such character that the essential purposes of marriage cannot realistically be fulfilled, coupled with failure of reasonable efforts at reconciliation.”
