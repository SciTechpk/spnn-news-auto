Pakistan in uncharted waters as ‘super El Nino’ multiplies threats

According to the World Meteorological Organisation (WMO) and climate scientists, a very strong El Nino increases the likelihood of significant shifts in temperature and rainfall patterns across the globe, including Pakistan, whose southern provinces can experience drought-like conditions. The global mean surface temperature has already increased by 1.4°C since the pre-industrial era, but this “super El Nino”, with its natural warming, could add 0.3°C to this number, pushing surface temperatures to 1.7°C above pre-industrial levels. The World Food Programme noted El Nino is set to sharply increase hunger, with 274 million people in 45 countries forecast to be acutely food insecure by 2027-end — 49 million more than today. World Health Organisation’s Teresa Zak­aria also branded it “a significant public health threat” and a “risk multiplier”. “The most severe impacts will be experienced while climate shocks intersect with weak hea­l­th systems, limited access to healthcare, food insecurity, displacement and humanitarian crisis,” she warned, urging immediate action. ‘Gigantic El Nino’ Dr Fahad Saeed, a leading climate scientist at Berlin-based Climate Analytics, compared the current ‘gigantic El Nino’ to the one in 1877-78, the “most severe on record”, that caused millions of deaths. “Such an intense event may impact the monsoon patterns and bring droughts, particularly in southern provinces,” said Dr Saeed. “But it is not clearly written on the horizon,” he said, pointing to multiple factors that can influence rainfall. The WMO said it may peak towards the end of the year, with scientists suggesting that its impact will last well into 2027. Dr Regina Rodrigues, professor of physical oceanography and climate at the Federal University of Santa Catarina in Brazil, said not every event is the same — they start, peak, and end at different times. She said they usually start around June-July and peak around December-February, but the current event is unusually strong, so it is difficult to pinpoint its peak or strength. “…What I expect for 2027 is that it will be the warmest year on record if El Nino continues as it generally does. The impact on several places is also harder in the second half — a lot of extreme events happen in the second half of El Nino,” she said. Pakistan has already received below-normal monsoon rainfall in June (19 per cent deficit), July (11pc), and August (23pc), according to the Pakistan Meteorological Department. This outlook was not surprising, because multiple studies point to increased monsoon rainfall variability and extremes. How El Nino will impact next year’s rainfall is uncertain at this point, but historical trends suggest South Asia experiences dry spells during such periods. Heat and droughts Dr Ali Mirchi and Dr Arfan Arshad, who authored an important study on 2022 floods, said rainfall during El Nino has not followed a “completely consistent pattern” in Pakistan. “Historically, El Nino has generally been associated with weaker summer monsoon rainfall, but it does not mean that every El Nino year is dry. A Pakistan Meteorological Department study examining rainfall from 1951 to 2012 found that eight out of 11 El Nino events were associated with below-normal monsoon rainfall. However, three El Nino years — 1977, 1994 and 1997 — experienced above-normal rainfall,” they said, adding that the data shows that El Nino increases the likelihood of a weaker monsoon, but it is “not a guarantee of drought”. For Dr Saeed, one of the major consequences of warmer temperatures driven by El Nino could be deadly heat waves in the southern districts of Pakistan. He referred to the deadly heat waves of 2015, 2016 and 2024 (all El Nino years), saying that most of the deaths due to heat in Pakistan were not counted. Over 1,000 people reportedly died in the 2015 heatwave. If heat conditions persisted, it could impact agriculture, said Dr Saeed. For instance, the country’s worst modern drought ran from 1999 to 2002, with agricultural output hit hardest in 2000-01 and significant migration out of Sindh and Balochistan. It followed the 1997-98 El Nino but coincided largely with La Nina conditions, which shows how indirectly ENSO (El Nino–Southern Oscillation) can translate into drought on the ground. Becoming frequent Dr Rodrigues noted that climate models show that strong El Nino events were becoming frequent. The period between two strong El Nino events used to be around 15 years (1982-83, 1997-98, 2015-16 events), but it was then reduced to nine years when we had the 2023-24 El Nino. “And now we are having another really strong El Nino, and it’s been only three years since. This is exactly what climate models forecast: increased frequency and stronger events,” she added. As scientists point to a possibility of an increase in such events, experts urged the government to take a proactive approach to deal with any eventuality arising out of these natural climate variations that can adversely imp­act agriculture through droughts and floods. 451,000 deaths This year’s El Nino weather pattern could lead to 451,000 additional heat-related deaths worldwide through February as it turbocharges global temperatures, according to projections published on Wednesday, according to AFP. The Sahel region in Africa could have an additional 66,800 heat deaths compared to a normal year, with nearly half of that number in Nigeria alone. An additional 19,300 are projected in Indonesia and 19,400 in the Philippines, Vietnam, Thailand, and Cambodia. The toll could reach 15,800 in India and 13,300 in Brazil. K-Electric had challenged Nepra’s revised MYT determination in the Sindh High Court and secured a stay against its implementation. The court later referred the matter to Nepra’s Appellate Tribunal, being the appropriate forum under the law. A three-member tribunal on Wedn­esday upheld Nepra’s revised determination and rejected KE’s position. Nepra subsequently issued four interrelated gazette notifications and clarified that KE consumers would not be charged the tariff determined by the regulator. In his address at the ministerial meeting of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (OIC) Contact Group on Jammu and Kashmir on the sidelines of the 81st session of the UN General Assembly in New York, Dar said: “The treaty is a binding international agreement and contains no provision permitting its unilateral suspension. “International obligations can’t be set aside when they become inconvenient.” Recalling the conflict between Pakistan and India in May last year following a deadly attack in occupied Kashmir’s Pahalgam, Dar said it “provided a stark reminder of the consequences of leaving the Jammu and Kashmir dispute unresolved”. The foreign minister also mentioned that following the Pahalgam incident, India levelled baseless allegations against Pakistan without credible investigations and embarked upon military aggression. “Pakistan exercised its inherent right to self-defence under Article 51 of the United Nations Charter in a measured and proportionate manner while acting responsibly to prevent further escalation,” he stated. DPM Dar emphasised that the “lessons from [the] dangerous confrontation from May 2025 must not be lost”. “Two nuclear-armed states came into a direct military conflict over a dispute that has remained unresolved for almost eight decades,” he noted. “Whether in South Asia or the Middle East, military force can’t resolve longstanding political disputes. Dialogue, diplomacy and just resolution of the root causes in accordance with the international law remain the only sustainable way forward,” he stressed. The foreign minister added that India’s unilateral attempt to hold the IWT in abeyance has introduced “another dangerous dimension”, explaining that the treaty was a binding international agreement and contained no provision permitting its unilateral suspension. He also referred to the PCA’s decision on the matter, saying the court reaffirmed that “the unilateral action by one party can’t deprive the court of its competence and has underscored the continued force of the treaty’s obligations, including those governing the Western rivers”. ‘Sustained effort to silence people’ At the outset of his address, Dar expressed “profound appreciation” to the OIC secretary general and members of the group for their “steadfast and principled support for Kashmiri people”. “For almost eight decades people of Jammu and Kashmir have waited for the international community to honour a solemn promise: the right to determine their future through a free and impartial plebiscite under the UN auspices,” he said. He mentioned a series of illegal and unilateral measures taken by India since Aug 5, 2019, saying New Delhi’s “unmistakable intention is the unlawfully changing the demographic, critical and cultural character” of India-occupied Kashmir (IoK). The foreign minister noted that India had systematically “dismantled political and civic space” in the region. “Kashmiri leaders, activists, journalists and youth continue facing arbitrary detentions, torture and persecution under draconian laws, including the Public Safety Act.” He noted that political organisations had been outlawed and legitimate dissent was criminalised. “This suppression is accompanied by a deliberate effort to alter the identity of the occupied territory.” He added: “New domicile and laws, manipulation of electoral constituencies, addition of non-Kashmiris to electoral roles, confiscation and demolition of properties and measures undermining Kashmiri Muslim’s cultural and religious heritage are used to change realities on the ground.” Meanwhile, India has “simultaneously sought to shut the window through which the world can see these violations”, Dar highlighted. “Media freedom has been systematically curtailed; journalists face intimidation, surveillance and detention; Kashmir Press Club was forcibly closed; social media accounts have been blocked; and international media and independent observers continue to be denied unhindered access.” DPM Dar pointed out that the international community had independently corroborated these concerns, including in a joint communication by the UN Special Procedures issued on Oct 16, 2025. That report mentioned the “arrest and detention of around 2,800 individuals, including journalists and human rights defenders; demolition of homes, blocking of around 8,000 social media accounts; and incidents of hate speech, intimidation and dehumanisation targeting Kashmiris and Muslims”. Dar stressed that the incidents were not isolated violations. “Taken together, they reveal a sustained effort to silence people, transform the identity of their homeland and foreclose their right to self-determination and their own future,” he said. In his address, DPM Dar also mentioned multiple laws promulgated by India for the annexation of Jammu and Kashmir. He asserted that New Delhi cannot “invoke domestic law to negate” a United Nations Security Council (UNSC) resolution on Kashmir, adding that Kashmir remained an internationally recognised dispute on the UNSC’s agenda. “No domestic legislation, judicial verdict, electoral exercise or demographic manipulation can substitute for the Kashmiri people’s inalienable right to self-determination.” He reaffirmed Pakistan’s commitment to a just and peaceful settlement of the dispute in accordance with the relevant UNSC resolutions and wishes of the Kashmiri people. “We will continue extending our fullest support to the Kashmiri people until the right is realised,” the deputy premier reaffirmed. ‘Sustained diplomatic action’ Extending his gratitude to OIC for consistently standing up with the Kashmiri brothers and sisters, Dar noted: “Our challenge now is to translate this principled solidarity into a sustained diplomatic action.” Expanding upon those actions, Dar urged the OIC and its member states to “maintain a unified and visible … position at the UN and other international fora and intensify efforts for implementation” of the relevant UNSC resolutions. The foreign minister further urged the OIC to “call upon India to reverse all illegal measures taken since Aug 5, 2019; halt demographic engineering and attacks on Kashmiri political, religious and cultural identities; and release all political prisoners and those arbitrarily detained”. Third, Dar called on the OIC to demand unrestricted access to IoK for the OIC special envoy and fact-finding mission, the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR), UN Special Procedures, international media and independent observers. He also sought OIC members’ support for “international accountability for serious human rights violations” in the region. Fourth, Dar urged the forum to strengthen coordinated OIC diplomacy in New York and Geneva and support the OIC’s call for an updated OHCHR report on Kashmir. He pointed out: “Kashmiri people are only asking the international community to deliver what was provided to them decades ago. The right to self-determination is already recognised, and the mechanism for its exercise has already been prescribed by the UNSC. “What remains is the fulfilment of the solemn international commitment.” Dar also urged the OIC to keep the Jammu and Kashmir issue high on the international agenda, oppose attempts to alter the character of the disputed territory and advance a just and peaceful settlement in accordance with the relevant UNSC resolution and the aspirations of Kashmiris.
