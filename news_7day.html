Senate panel passes bill proposing death penalty for perpetrators of acid attacks

ISLAMABAD: The Senate Standing Committee on Interior on Monday passed a series of key criminal law amendments, including a bill proposing the death penalty for acid attacks, as it moved to strengthen protections for vulnerable groups and public safety. The bills were passed despite opposition from State Minister for Interior Tallal Chaudhry, with PPP lawmakers also voting in favour. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Bill moved by Senator Muhammad Abdul Qadir, seeking to amend Section 336B of the Pakistan Penal Code (PPC) relating to hurt caused by corrosive substances, was passed in the absence of the mover. Citing “the recent incident of acid attack on a lady doctor in Quetta”, the bill states that despite the 2011 amendment, acid attacks continue to cause lifelong disfigurement and trauma. It proposes the punishment of “death or imprisonment for life or imprisonment of not less than 14 years and a fine of Rs2 million” to serve as a strong deterrent. The committee also unanimously passed the Criminal Laws (Amendment) Bill moved by Senator Sarmad Ali, proposing to criminalise the abuse of domestic workers. The Statement of Objects and Reasons notes that “domestic workers constitute one of the most vulnerable segments of society, often subjected to physical violence, sexual abuse, forced labour, and other forms of mistreatment by employers.” It adds that “existing provisions… do not specifically address the abuse arising from dependency and control inherent in domestic work settings”. The proposed section covers physical violence, sexual harassment, threats, forced labour, wage theft, confiscation of identity documents, denial of food or medical care, and humiliating treatment. The proposed penalties are “imprisonment for a term which may extend to 10 years and fine which may extend to Rs100,000” and “imprisonment for life and fine which may extend to Rs500,000” if the offence causes serious injury or death. The bill also makes employers liable if abuse is committed by household members, “unless the employer took reasonable steps to prevent such abuse”. Schedule II of the Criminal Procedure Code has also been amended to make the offence non-bailable and triable by a court of session. A realistic understanding of the challenge requires recognising two facts: the international community has not provided all the climate finance it pledged, and Pakistan has also not developed strong enough institutions and systems to effectively access and use the funding available. Talking to Dawn, the European envoy acknowledged serious unease within the European Commission, which plays an influential role in monitoring compliance and decisions concerning the execution of the scheme. “Indeed, both from reading the report and from discussions with experts, there are serious doubts within the European Commission about the effective implementation of these conventions,” he said. The EU’s GSP+ regime is tied to compliance with international conventions on human rights, labour rights, environmental protection and good governance, which Islamabad has already signed up. Pakistan has remained a beneficiary since 2014 and is the scheme’s largest beneficiary. The stakes for Pakistan are, therefore, considerably high. Clothing, textiles, leather products, prepared foods and beverages are among the major beneficiaries. The fear of losing the preferential access is quite real, as the scheme provides for partial or full temporary withdrawal of preferences in cases of serious failure to comply with the underlying conventions. There are precedents at both ends of the spectrum, with Bolivia facing partial withdrawal and Sri Lanka previously losing its preferences altogether. Mr Karoblis, however, said no determination had so far been made in Pakistan’s case. “So far, it requires further investigation as to whether the regression in these specific areas has already reached the threshold concerning the implementation of the regulation, and whether it has reached the level that could trigger a partial or full temporary suspension,” he said. Mr Karoblis said Pakistan had already ratified the five additional conventions required under the new scheme and therefore would technically face no legal difficulty on that count. The real test, he stressed, was implementation. “As far as the benchmark is concerned both the current GSP regulation and the new one sets two substantive conditions that a country needs to meet,” he said. “One is the vulnerability of the country. We are speaking about the level of economic development, which unfortunately is not very high. But, on the other hand, because of that, Pakistan is eligible for the system.” The other condition is the increased number of international conventions. “But the main emphasis is on implementation. Both under the present regulation and in the future, the important condition is the absence of serious failure to implement the United Nations conventions in these areas. That is the benchmark, actually,” the envoy said. Therefore, Pakistan’s reapplication will also require more than a general political commitment. “There is also a procedural requirement. For the reapplication, there should be an action plan of the country,” the ambassador said. “This means that the emphasis is first of all on implementation, not only on reapplication, with very concrete measures, timelines and performance indicators.” Problem areas Among the issues likely to figure prominently in the EU’s engagement with Islamabad are enforced disappearances, restrictions on freedom of expression, blasphemy related concerns and the broader rule of law situation. The latest assessment was particularly critical of the human rights situation, noting that enforced disappearances and extrajudicial killings had increased without accountability for perpetrators. It also expressed concern over legislative measures that could affect judicial independence and access to fair trial. The ambassador said Brussels was also continuing its dialogue with Islamabad on improving the rule of law situation more broadly. On freedom of expression, Mr Karoblis specifically referred to serious concerns over specific pieces of legislation, and how they may hamper the effective implementation of international conventions,” he said. “Looking at the mechanisms, it is indeed the laws we have mentioned, including the recent amendments to the PECA law and the Punjab Defamation Act, among others… It is about the right to freedom of expression,” he said. The issue of blasphemy also remains on Brussels’ agenda, despite the government’s recent assertions that it has acted against elements exploiting allegations for harassment and persecution. Mr Karoblis acknowledged that the number of cases had declined from the peak witnessed several years ago and referred to the release on bail of more than 100 people in Islamabad who had been arrested in cases linked to so called ‘blasphemy gangs’. “But there are still issues and concerns for us,” he said, noting that hundreds of people remained imprisoned in Punjab.
