What you need to know about the 2026 US midterm elections

Early voting has been underway for weeks ahead of the November 3, 2026, midterm elections, when US voters will elect members of the House of Representatives and Senate and governors in states across the country. Held in the middle of each four-year presidential term, the midterms by definition do not involve the president. But for anyone who thinks they lack drama or importance this year, consider these two statistics: More than 100 million people likely will cast ballots in this year’s midterm voting, if turnout is anything like the previous midterm in 2022; and as of October 12, a record $3.8 billion had been spent on political advertisements that have been aired so far. Top spending is in battleground US Senate races in Texas, Ohio and Michigan, according to AdImpact, which tracks the activities. Elon Musk alone has spent about $366m to support Republicans, according to campaign finance records. Still, midterms generally attract fewer voters than the higher-profile presidential races. More than 155m votes were cast in the 2024 presidential election, for example. Control of Congress at stake Plenty is at stake in this midterm election as Democrats try to win majority control of the US Congress. Currently, Republicans hold a 218-214 majority in the House, where there are two vacancies and one independent who caucuses with Republicans. Democrats must win a net gain of four seats to win control. Democrats are also fighting to win the Senate, where Republicans hold a 53-47 majority. They must net four additional seats to get control. Ten races, including those in Maine, Ohio, North Carolina, Iowa and Georgia, could decide the outcome. Can midterms affect the presidency? Midterm elections generally are dangerous territory for the political party that holds the White House and/or Congress. For 2026, that means that President Donald Trump and his Republicans controlling the US Senate and House of Representatives are in perilous political positions. The midterms can be an opportunity for voters to, in effect, grade the president’s performance, and possibly trigger White House course corrections if they receive low marks. Midterm election results can also give aspiring presidential candidates hints on how to tailor their messages to voters in the next presidential campaign, which in this case will be in 2028. What are polls showing? Approval of Trump’s performance is at an all-time low of 32 per cent, according to a September Reuters/Ipsos poll. Domestic issues like rising consumer prices are likely to have an impact on the outcome of many races and could erode support for Republicans. At the same time, there is evidence that Democrats are more enthusiastic about voting this year than Republicans. If early voting trends continue, it could point to a Democratic win in November. There are also signs that Latino voters who veered to Trump in 2024 might be returning to Democrats because of frustration over the economy and some of Trump’s immigration enforcement tactics. However, a landslide for the Democrats is far from assured. Republicans could benefit from continued scepticism towards Democrats, a Republican spending advantage on campaigns, and fears from moderate voters over some primary wins by left-wingers. Recent midterm history Recent history tells the story of the shockwaves a midterm election can produce. In the 2010 midterms, during the Democratic presidency of Barack Obama, Republicans scored major victories, winning 63 additional House seats amid an underperforming US economy and weak support for Obama’s landmark healthcare reform legislation. Democrats lost their majority. In the Senate, they held onto control despite losing six seats. The Republican wave was a “shellacking,” Obama lamented at the time. He subsequently faced Republican demands for significant reductions to non-defence spending. Democrats returned the favour in 2018, with Trump in the White House. They gained a net total of 40 House seats, but lost a net two Senate seats. This year’s elections also have races for governors in 36 of the 50 states and voting in nearly every state for seats in their state-level legislative bodies.Currently, Republicans control 55pc of state legislative seats across the country, while Democrats hold 43.8pc, according to Ballotpedia. Who will be speaker? If Democrats manage to win the House, the next speaker second in line for the presidency after the vice president is likely to be Democratic Representative Hakeem Jeffries of New York. He is currently the House Democratic leader. Jeffries, who would become the first Black American to hold the powerful office, would be in a position to direct House committees to launch investigations into a range of controversial activities by Trump and his associates. Jeffries repeatedly has said Democrats would look into what he has called “unprecedented levels of corruption” during the Trump administration. Jeffries also would steer Democrats’ legislative priorities through the House. He has said Democrats would focus on making healthcare, groceries, housing, energy and other costs more affordable. A Republican win, on the other hand, would ensure that the speaker’s gavel remains in that party’s hands, either with current speaker Mike Johnson or whichever Republican prevails if he is challenged for the job. Republican priorities are likely to be focused on additional tax cuts, huge increases in defence spending, and deeper social safety net reductions, building on Trump’s 2025 “One Big Beautiful Bill.” Marquee races Among the most-watched House races are Republican Representative Mike Lawler’s bid for reelection to a New York district that Democratic candidate Kamala Harris won in her 2024 presidential campaign and an open seat in a Nebraska swing district where farmers and truckers face sharply higher fuel prices as a result of the US war against Iran. Also high on Democrats’ list of House seats they hope to take from Republicans are two in Pennsylvania and one in New Jersey. Hovering over all of this are questions over whether Trump will try to meddle in the elections, which are administered by the 50 individual states. Since 2020, he has repeatedly insisted there is widespread cheating in US elections, despite a lack of evidence. State election officials and Democrats in Congress have been preparing for possible legal challenges to any moves by his administration to try to intervene.

Lahore Gymkhana’s low rent attributed to ‘decades of oversight failures’

LAHORE: A special committee of the Punjab Assembly, chaired by MPA Samiullah Khan, came down hard on decades of government oversight failures after Lahore Gymkhana Club Chairman Salman Siddique conceded that the annual rent of Rs5,000 paid for over 100 acres of prime state land in the provincial capital was wholly inadequate. Appearing before the committee alongside members of the club’s management, Mr Siddique expressed surprise at the nominal rate given the vast acreage involved and the club’s vastly improved financial footing. Contrasting the institution’s current financial standing with its constraints during the 1990s, he admitted that the club should be paying at least Rs10 million annually, while expressing willingness to cooperate with the ongoing inquiry and share records. Committee members noted that this admission from the club’s own top leadership underscored the urgency of determining why successive state authorities allowed such a massive concession to persist without meaningful scrutiny. They stressed that the onus rested squarely on public institutions mandated to protect state land, enforce lease terms, and ensure transparent usage. During the session, the member colonies of the board of revenue revealed that then chief minister Sardar Muhammad Arif Nakai, who was also a member of the club, had verbally directed the fixation of a token Rs5,000 annual rent for the lease extension in 1996, bypassing proper land valuation and relevant legal principles. Perturbed by how a mere verbal instruction could underpin such a major agreement, lawmakers demanded the complete documentary record detailing the legal authority and approval process behind the 1996 lease deed. PA body constitutes fact-finding committee to frame specific queries, verify records, and present comprehensive report within 10 days Emphasising that the land ultimately belongs to the people of Punjab, the committee was presented with estimates valuing the prime property between Rs76 billion and Rs218 billion. Members questioned the state’s failure to secure a fair public return on an asset of this scale and firmly rejected any notion that the club’s internal corporate governance shielded it from state oversight, maintaining that institutional autonomy cannot supersede accountability for public property. Touching upon structural approvals, Mr Siddique affirmed that while the club fulfills its corporate and tax obligations under the Companies Act 2017, he has struggled to locate internal records regarding official building sanctions. In response, lawmakers called for strict verification of sanctioned building plans, statutory permissions, and compliance with lease terms, raising safety concerns over structures lacking documented approvals. On the issue of an independent state audit, Mr Siddique noted that the matter remains pending before the Supreme Court and assured adherence to its eventual verdict, a position echoed by management committee member Tasneem Noorani. The panel also explored proposals aimed at broader public utility, such as opening the club’s cricket ground to the general public or relocating the facility near the Ring Road, while questioning why public funds were ever spent on an exclusive recreational club occupying state land. To take the probe forward, the committee directed the Lahore Development Authority, Parks and Horticulture Authority, Federal Board of Revenue, and other relevant bodies to submit detailed jurisdictional reports. Furthermore, a fact-finding committee headed by the Advocate General Punjab, comprising officials from the Board of Revenue, Metropolitan Corporation Lahore, Law department, Agriculture department, and the deputy commissioner, had been assigned to frame specific queries, verify records, and present a comprehensive report within ten days. Concluding the session, the chairperson directed that legal questions stemming from the proceedings be forwarded to the high-powered Law Reforms Committee, headed by Speaker Malik Muhammad Ahmad Khan, for joint deliberations aimed at fixing responsibility for oversight lapses and ensuring the lawful use of public land. What makes desperate families adopt suicide as ‘escape’?

• Mental health experts explain how diseased mind perceives and rationalises the extreme act• Identify socio-economic distress, anguish, despair and mental health conditions as key factors• Stress that psychiatric conditions are treatable with therapy and medication KARACHI: How could one spouse persuade the other to participate in a joint suicide and kill their own children? Two recent incidents in Karachi, in which parents allegedly killed their own children and a household member before ending their own lives, make answering this troubling question particularly urgent. In September, the decomposed bodies of a doctor, his wife and their three daughters were found in a locked apartment in Ahsanabad. This month, five members of another family were found dead inside a locked portion in Gulshan-i-Hadeed. While investigations are still underway, police authorities treat both cases as suspected murder-suicides. When mental health experts were asked to address this troubling question of how someone could persuade their spouse to participate in such an extreme act, they explained the psychiatric and psychological conditions that could lead up to such acts. The experts also pointed to severe socio-economic distress, rising unemployment, and a lack of accessible crisis support systems. Speaking to Dawn, senior psychiatrist and retired Brigadier Dr Shoaib Ahmed, Executive Director of Dr Abdul Qadeer Khan Institute of Behavioural Sciences of Dow University of Health Sciences, said: “When someone is moving towards suicide, their mind does not work the way a mentally healthy person’s mind does. “In such cases, when couples find themselves completely overwhelmed by severe poverty and relentless socioeconomic issues, they often reach a breaking point,” he said, adding: “The amount of pressure and the multitude of issues become too much for them to deal with, which is why they move towards suicide.” Addressing the specific nature of joint suicides, Dr Ahmed discussed suicide pacts — an agreement made between more than one individual facing extreme distress. He said that while such occurrences happen worldwide under severe circumstances, they stem from a profound sense of helplessness. “In extreme cases, couples not only choose to end their own lives to escape a world of torment but also tragically decide to take their children’s lives as well, in what they perceive as an act of mercy killing,” the senior psychiatrist said. Driven by despair, they rationalise that leaving their children behind without any parental support in a painful world would only expose them to further suffering, effectively viewing the devastating act through a distorted lens of mercy, Dr Ahmed added. The senior psychiatrist warned that financial pressures, job drain among educated professionals, and escalating poverty are pushing individuals to the brink of hopelessness. However, Dr Ahmed added that the psychiatric conditions are treatable with therapy and medication. ‘Susceptibility’ Psychiatrist and Psychotherapist Dr Usama Bin Zubair believes that brain chemistry can play a role in suicidal thoughts, but suicide is rarely explained by “brain chemistry alone”. Responding to Dawn queries on the telephone, the CEO of Pakistan Institute of Mental Health, Rawalpindi, explained biological, psychological and social factors behind such suicides. “The issue spans biological, psychological, and social dimensions,” Dr Zubair said. Shedding light on suicide pacts and cult suicides, in which even hundreds of people die by suicide together, he said that there is also the concept of rational suicide. “In rational suicide, couples mutually decide that their life’s hardships have become far greater than death itself,” Dr Zubair explained. It is a situation where a person believes, after thinking about their circumstances, that ending their life is a logical or reasonable choice. He said personality factors also play a critical role, including susceptibility, where one partner suggests the act and the other possesses a personality inclined to follow. ‘Contagious anxiety’ Responding to the question of why neither partner in a couple would stop the other from carrying out such an act, senior Clinical Psychologist and Counsellor Atia Fareed spoke about contagious anxiety and shared psychological distress, explaining the dynamics of joint suicide pacts. “Anxiety is often considered a contagious, catching illness in our society. If someone is dealing with mental health issues in a household, its effects invariably bleed into the lives of everyone living there,” the psychologist warned. “When both partners are suffering from extreme mental agony, neither is capable of pulling the other out of danger. Instead of offering hope, they can drag each other deeper into despair because both individuals become so profoundly incapacitated that neither is in a position to help the other.” She said that growing hopelessness can compel a couple to validate each other’s destructive thoughts. However, she added that the recent Karachi incidents constitute a “murder-suicide” by an individual rather than a collective “mass suicide”. “True mass suicide requires mutual consensus among all participants,” the clinical psychologist stressed. Dominant personality or mental illness “There are two possibilities. Both individuals may be experiencing mental illness, though not necessarily to the same degree,” said senior Psychiatrist Dr Syed Abdurrehman. Alternatively, one person’s mental illness may cause significant stress for the other family members, creating a shared psychological impact, added the managing director of the Karachi Psychiatric Hospital. “The dominant personality in a family can also influence others. A husband can affect his wife, just as a mother can affect her children”. He added that when parents decide that the entire family should die, it may be because both are experiencing mental illness, or because one person’s strong psychological influence affects the other. “Brain chemical imbalances or any other cause, like genetic inheritance of a disease, are also very major causes,” Dr Abdurrehman added. Social and economic pressures Psychiatrist Dr Bilal Zafar also agreed with the experts’ views. The director of Willing Ways, originally founded as Sadaqat Clinic, said severe financial crises, debt, profound loneliness, despair, and a sense of helplessness could drive people to take such drastic steps. “A disturbed brain understands this as the only way out — to end life,” Dr Bilal Zafar added. “Secondly, husbands and wives have a deep relationship. The thoughts of one can affect the other. If one is hopeless, the other automatically absorbs that hopelessness by watching them and also becomes hopeless.” While financial pressures are significant stressors, reducing complex mental health crises strictly to economic factors is misleading and dangerous, the expert added. The experts believe that in such cases, families and friends often overlook warning signs such as social withdrawal, isolation, and significant changes in behaviour. Families and friends bear a critical responsibility to monitor their loved ones for such symptoms, they added. They also stress the need to move past social stigmas, maintain open lines of communication with distressed loved ones, and ensure access to affordable, professional psychiatric help before minor psychological struggles escalate into crises. Talking taxes

IN the two days before writing this column, I had the pleasure of being immersed in a series of discussions in Islamabad on all things related to taxes in Pakistan. Hosted by the finance ministry and the IMF, the discussions brought together senior practitioners, advisers, and stakeholders for a detailed, high-level debate on what all needs to change in Pakistan’s tax system for it to play a more enabling role in undergirding stability for the private sector and promoting growth rather than being a drag on it. What struck me during the discussions was the large gulf between the community of experts the IFIs bring to the tax conversation and the people who legislate and oversee the making and implementation of tax policy in Pakistan. The gulf is evident in how they approach the question of tax, the priorities they bring to the table, how they measure success, and the language they use to talk about taxes. The experts present — sadly, the content of the discussions was “off the record”, so only broad themes can be discussed here — emphasised sustained commitment, balanced compliance, and measuring outcomes, “not activity”. Special emphasis was put on the harmonisation of GST, predictability of policy, and the mutually reinforcing dynamic between various tax heads — for example, how a properly administered GST can help strengthen income tax as well. FBR representatives underlined their tax reform efforts. However, participants still pointed out that the base remains narrow, that withholding plays an outsize role — one of the charts showed how 90 per cent of withholding taxes are collected under 10 heads only — and that this role is increasing. There was broad consensus that Pakistan’s tax system remains inequitable, riddled with “distortions”, and serves more as a drag on growth and investment than as their promoter. It is surprising how underdeveloped the template governing data disclosure on revenues is. There is a reason why this is so. The priorities that have shaped Pakistan’s tax system over the years differ from what tax experts suggest it should serve. In Pakistan, raw revenue is the metric of success — and not efficiency, buoyancy, or the self-reinforcing elements of tax administration. Tax policy responds heavily to voice in the system. Over the years, it has evolved along the path of least resistance, so to speak, causing the bulk of the burden of generating new revenue to fall on those with no voice, or the least organised voice, in the system. Even in the section of industry that likes to highlight the quantum of its contribution to the revenue effort, it is highly probable that the bulk of the taxes are passed through to their consumer base rather than being borne by the owners and controllers of capital. Over the years, this course has led to a tax system that is highly regressive, very concentrated, barely buoyant, and heavily reliant on withholding as the basis for its collection. As participants grappled with the vexing question of tax reform in Pakistan, I felt one modest but concrete proposal might help refocus the conversation on evidence rather than emotion. Given how central the question is to Pakistan’s economic future, it is surprising how underdeveloped the template governing data disclosure on revenues is. The monthly revenue collection data we all see reported in the newspapers is informally taken by reporters from unnamed FBR sources and is often inconsistent. We only see revenue-collection data when the Fiscal Operations data is released every quarter, but even then it provides only headline numbers, and its release frequency is too low to be useful. One thing that is sorely needed in Pakistan is a more robust, mandatory data release template for the FBR, with monthly frequency at the very least, if not more often. The IMF should use its access to the highest levels of government to urge that monthly data releases on revenue collection become mandatory for the FBR. The World Bank should offer its expertise to help develop this template, with uniform accounting conventions governing matters such as how to treat withheld refunds and how to break down the releases. Hence, they serve as a meaningful source for discerning the direction of the fiscal outlook during the fiscal year. The government should legislate this requirement and make any necessary modifications to FBR rules to ensure that a dedicated team is always at work within the tax authority, gathering, compiling, and preparing the next data release as per a predetermined schedule. While we’re at it, let’s also mandate a similar release requirement on the finance ministry on expenditures. Q Block already submits a monthly statement of all expenditures to the IMF, as per the accounting treatment given by the IMF itself. It would be relatively simple to make this public. There is no good reason not to do so. And many participants at the conference did point out that the question of revenue is connected to that of expenditure. The bad practices that characterise much of Pakistan’s tax system are a symptom of the environment in which tax policy is administered. That environment is riddled with discretionary powers whose sway in the tax bureaucracy is at the root of a lot of what ails the tax effort. The best antidote to discretionary powers is daylight, or transparency. Open a window. Let the light of day shine in the otherwise dark rooms in which the tax machinery is used to operating. Let them grow accustomed to the discipline a regular data-release template brings. Let them answer the questions that will naturally arise once the data starts to flow. And watch the tax conversation find the rails it needs to ride into the future. Spurring economic growth

THE World Bank’s Reclaiming Momentum Towards Prosperity report presents a sobering reality: poverty has climbed back to 25.3 per cent on the national line; and close to 45pc of Pakistanis fall below the bank’s new $4.20-a-day threshold. Stabilising our way into deeper poverty has exposed the limits of conventional macroeconomic management. Speaking last week at the International Conference on Inclusive and Resilient Economic Empowerment and Growth, organised by the Pakistan Poverty Alleviation Fund in Islamabad, I put one question at the centre: how do we break this cycle? Accelerating growth requires recognising where real growth originates. Pakistan’s largest capital investment of this decade never appeared in a Public Sector Development Programme (PSDP), Annual Development Programme (ADP), or Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC). Farmers, shopkeepers and households imported about 17 gigawatts of solar panels in 2024, and the same again in 2025. No PC-1. No donor. No climate finance unit. They used their own savings, because diesel costs and the unwinding of unsustainable energy subsidies made it rational. The lesson for macroeconomic policy is direct. Prolonged, distorted subsidies are not a safety net. They are an obstacle to citizen-led growth. When fiscal pressure forced the state to pare them back, citizens moved faster than planners to protect their own balance sheets. The state’s response was to tax panel imports and revise net-metering terms. So when we ask how planning can accelerate growth, we should first admit that citizens are already ahead of the plan. The question is whether the state will remove the friction in their way or keep chasing donors. Three shifts would make the difference. Unpriced risk: Macroeconomic stability cannot survive risk that no one has priced. Pakistan already has a climate budget. It is not the one captured by the recently introduced climate budget tagging. It is the one written after each disaster. Major floods now produce post-disaster needs assessments, reconstruction budgets, and emergency appeals. That is our real climate expenditure: unplanned and largely borrowed — a permanent drag on fiscal space. The fix is not more tagging of development spending. It is pricing climate risk before it arrives. Pakistan already has a climate budget — the one written after each disaster. In practice, this means treating climate losses as a contingent liability of each province, the way we already treat circular debt and SOE guarantees. The Fiscal Risk Statement mentions disasters but puts no number on them. Each province can estimate its average annual loss from floods, heat, and drought, and appropriate a share of it every year as an expected cost. Budget theorists call this ‘expected-value budgeting’. A loss that is budgeted becomes a decision. A loss that is not budgeted becomes a bailout, often paid through the Benazir Income Support Programme’s emergency transfers. Maintenance first: Economic productivity depends on assets that work. That makes resilience a maintenance problem before it is a development problem. Embankments, drains, canals, and roads do not fail because nobody built them. They fail because their recurrent budgets are starved. Yet our economic conversation stays inside the development budget. The federal PSDP alone carries a throw-forward of about Rs11 trillion, and new schemes keep joining the queue, while existing assets go unmaintained. The ADP builds the embankment. The operations and maintenance line decides whether it holds. Protecting maintenance allocations for water assets would preserve existing capital and buy more resilience per rupee than most new projects. One can see where local money is actually spent. The most local spending stream is not an SDG-linked programme. It is the parliamentarians’ development schemes: tube-wells, link roads, drains, and culverts. They are quick, uncoordinated, and climate-blind. Nobody screens them. A single check asking whether the asset will survive the next monsoon would protect more local capital than every formal climate project combined. Green growth at the local level will not come from specialised projects. It will come when ordinary public spending stops creating new fiscal liabilities. District arithmetic: Growth targets fail when they stay abstract. The NDC is written in tonnes of carbon and gigawatts. A district plans in culverts, acres, and tube-wells. Nobody has done the translation. A union council cannot own or deliver a national target expressed in metrics it does not know. The first mechanism is a translation layer. Each district needs a short climate investment and growth plan that converts national targets into local units: acres under laser levelling and drip irrigation, tube-wells solarised, kilometres of drains cleared.
