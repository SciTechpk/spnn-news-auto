Built in the 19th century, according to Tauseef, the facade of the temple in the city’s main commercial Raja Bazaar area is crumbling. Over the honking of motorcycles jostling through the crowded streets, a jewellery maker plies his merchandise from its ground floor. Before the partition of British-ruled India in 1947, Rawalpindi was a religiously diverse city where non-Muslims made up the majority of the population. The bloodshed and mass migrations of partition saw Hindus and Sikhs flee to India and Muslim refugees pour into Pakistan, permanently transforming the city’s demographics. Legislative scrutiny

PAKISTAN’S parliament and the provincial assemblies have an elaborate infrastructure of parliamentary oversight and scrutiny of the executive. There are more than 200 parliamentary committees in the six houses, including 35 in the National Assembly, 45 in the Senate, 45 in the provincial assembly of Punjab, 34 in Sindh, 38 in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and 15 in Balochistan. Each committee comprises 20 to 30 members varying from house to house. These members elect a committee chair in their first meeting; the chair is entitled to two personal staffers, a chauffeur-driven and fully maintained official car and an exclusive office. The committee members and the chair are entitled to travel and daily allowances for the days of the meeting and two days before and after the meeting. All this is paid for from the taxpayers’ pocket and is fully justified in view of the work assigned to these committees. Unlike the British parliamentary committees, which meet only during the plenary sessions of parliament and therefore do not incur any extra travel or daily allowance cost, parliamentary committees in Pakistan mostly meet when the legislature is in recess and hence, extra travel and daily allowances are to be paid separately. The Indian parliament has adopted another measure and constituted joint committees of the Lok Sabha (equivalent to our National Assembly) and the Rajya Sabha (like Pakistan’s Senate), which not only speeds up scrutiny in the committees, but results in some savings as well. In Pakistan, we have independent and separate committees both for the National Assembly and the Senate. Although the Indian joint committees system is efficient, it deprives parliament of a double layer of scrutiny which is available in Pakistan. The problem arises when important bills are summarily processed within days or even hours. In all systems of governance, whether presidential like the US, parliamentary like the UK and Pakistan, the parliaments — whatever name these are known as — rely on the committees to do most of the substantive work, especially the oversight of the executive, which primarily includes scrutiny of legislation. In any work which requires detailed scrutiny like the annual budget, a constitutional amendment, ordinary legislation or, as in the case of the US, confirmation of senior appointees in the executive and judiciary, committees play a pivotal role. It is not practical to do such work in plenary. Rules of almost all the legislatures in Pakistan provide that each legislation, after it is introduced in the plenary, will be referred to the relevant committee for detailed deliberations and scrutiny. In extraordinary circumstances, the plenary has the authority to suspend the rule and consider the legislation in the plenary without referring it to the committee concerned. One of the criteria by which the quality of democracy and effectiveness of legislative oversight in a country is assessed is the percentage of legislation which is not referred to the committees; the higher the percentage, the less democratic and effective the country and the legislature are considered. An associated criterion is the time taken and the number of meetings held to scrutinise the legislation in a committee. If legislation is referred to a committee to fulfil a formality and the committee returns it to the plenary in a day or two, it reflects very negatively on the democratic credentials of the parliament. As far as the number of bills passed by the legislatures is concerned, Pakistan has a reasonably good record. In the National Assembly, an average of 50 bills have been passed per year during the past four assemblies. The problem arises when the bills, especially the most important ones like constitutional amendments, are summarily processed within a couple of days, and sometimes within hours. For example, a record 279 bills were passed during the previous 15th National Assembly (2018-2023), which is the highest compared to the previous three assemblies starting from 2002. Although legislative activity had increased, the way these bills were passed without debate and scrutiny of the committees was widely criticised by legislators, media and political commentators. The coalition government passed 73 bills in the last session alone within 21 days. Almost half the bills (36) were not referred to the standing committees concerned and rushed through the plenary without a meaningful debate. The current 16th National Assembly has so far passed 162 bills in its 32 months of existence. The National Assembly data shown on its official website does not indicate the statistics about the bills referred to the committees or otherwise. Transparency demands that this particular information should be available to citizens and media. In the absence of such data, time-consuming research needs to be undertaken bill by bill to know the extent to which the standing committees were involved in the legislative process. According to analysis carried out by Pildat, 39 per cent of the bills passed by the current assembly so far were not referred to the committees and instead, the rules were suspended to pass the bill in the plenary directly. This translates to 48 (38pc) government bills and 15 (42pc) private members’ bills. This, by no means, is a good record. The situation becomes even more disappointing when we see that such important legislation such as the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments — which have had far-reaching impact on state institutions like the judiciary and the armed forces — are passed within a couple of days. The 26th Amendment was introduced in the Senate on Oct 20, 2024 and was passed the same day. The National Assembly passed it the next day and the president gave his assent later in the day. It was notified in the Gazette later in the evening. Such important legislation was wrapped up in two days. The 27th Amendment was also passed with the same hurry. Laws decide the fate of a nation for a long time. It is about time our legislative leadership gave more time to the committees and the plenary to improve the quality of legislation. On the one hand, Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari floated the idea of a fresh election within six months instead of a referendum on new provinces. Later, he said he wants Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif to be the first prime minister to complete a five-year term. Mr Bhutto-Zardari also conceded at one point that neither a referendum nor a fresh election could be held before electoral reforms. Clearly, while he has some ideas, he does not have a viable way out of the current conundrum. On the other hand, the PML-N’s parliamentary affairs minister insisted that a referendum “will happen” and that his party would make every effort to bring the PPP round to the idea. The defence minister also echoed that sentiment, while the information minister backed a referendum if the public also supported it. The PM has yet to make his position publicly known. The party chairman, on the other hand, said it was premature to comment on the idea. The referendum can be made part of the same exercise. Whichever party wins the election should then be bound to implement the public’s will. This seems to be the best way forward. Summonses for India’s security czar, ex-spymaster sent to Delhi court

WASHINGTON: A US federal court has sent legal summonses for Indian National Security Adviser Ajit Doval and former Research and Analysis Wing (R&AW) chief Samant Goel to three district courts in New Delhi, seeking assistance in serving them in a civil lawsuit alleging Indian government conspiracy to assassinate Sikh nationalist leader Gurpatwant Singh Pannun. US District Judge Katherine Polk Failla authorised the transmission of the legal documents through the Indian judicial system, after earlier attempts to serve them through India’s designated central authority and diplomatic channels failed. The summonses were sent by FedEx on Friday to Patiala House, Dwarka and Tis Hazari court complexes under provisions of the 1965 Hague Convention and India’s Code of Civil Procedure. The Patiala House district court was requested to facilitate service on Doval, while the Dwarka and Tis Hazari courts were approached in connection with Goel. US district judge allows alternative way after previous attempts failed in Pannun case The lawsuit, filed in September 2024, alleges that Indian officials conspired to arrange Pannun’s assassination in New York. It names the Indian government, Doval, Goel, former R&AW officer Vikram Yadav, Indian national Nikhil Gupta and 50 unidentified individuals as defendants. Pannun is seeking damages, alleging that the defendants conspired to arrange his assassination in retaliation for his advocacy of an independent Sikh state of Khalistan. The legal route was considered after the Indian Ministry of Law and Justice, the country’s designated central authority for processing foreign requests under the Hague Convention, returned the documents. The authority stated that the summonses did not fall within the specified categories. The US judge, however, held that the Indian authority’s brief response did not establish that the Hague Convention was inapplicable to the case. She concluded that Article 19 nevertheless permitted the use of India’s domestic judicial procedures. Previous attempts to serve Doval personally during his visits to the United States had also failed, according to court filings. Pannun’s lawyers told the court that security personnel had prevented them from delivering the documents on three occasions. While rejecting the lawsuit, India’s External Affairs Ministry spokesperson Randhir Jaiswal reiterated New Delhi’s position, saying: “I have said earlier that we reject such baseless lawsuits.” Earlier on Nov 10, Jaiswal had described the allegations against senior Indian officials as fabricated and malicious and maintained that requests for serving judicial documents in India could only be routed through the Ministry of Law and Justice. The case has acquired renewed significance following the conviction and sentencing of Gupta in a separate US criminal proceeding on Friday. The federal court in New York sentenced Gupta to nearly 20 years in prison after he pleaded guilty in February 2026 to participating in a murder-for-hire conspiracy targeting Pannun. According to US prosecutors, Yadav, an Indian government employee associated with the Cabinet Secretariat that houses India’s intelligence service, had recruited Gupta in 2023 to arrange Pannun’s killing in New York. Prosecutors said Yadav agreed to pay $100,000 for the assassination, with $15,000 delivered as an advance payment. Gupta was arrested in the Czech Republic in June 2023 and extradited to the United States in 2024. Yadav has also been charged in the criminal case but remains outside US custody. Till the early 1970s, it was often believed that South Asians who travelled to Western countries for studies or migrated there, often adopted ‘Western values.’ In early Indian and Pakistani movies, Pakistanis and Indians returning from the West were often portrayed as enlightened, rational people who scoffed at the superstitious beliefs of their countrymen and women. By the mid-1970s, though, the portrayal of West-returned Pakistanis and Indians changed. They began being portrayed as stupid, dope-smoking, guitar-slinging hippies. But in the decades that followed, reality turned out very differently. Instead of becoming less religious or adopting ‘immoral’ lifestyles, many South Asians living abroad actually became much more religious than their relatives back home. Anyone travelling between major South Asian cities and the suburbs of Western cities can see this contrast. In South Asia, religion is a natural part of everyday life, heard in the call to prayer or seen at local temples. People do not have to work hard to keep it alive. Abroad, however, mosque and temple attendance becomes stricter, food rules become more rigid, and dogmatic religious rules often replace the relaxed, mixed traditions practised back home. According to the American anthropologist Steven Vertovec, moving to a new country rarely weakens religious belief. Instead, it often makes people more conscious of their faith. This change happens because of a deep desire to protect one’s culture, and the political reality of living as a minority in a foreign land. Leaving South Asia means leaving behind extended families, friends, familiar streets and unspoken social rules. In Western countries, society focuses heavily on individual independence and small nuclear families, which can make newcomers feel lonely. Religion fills this void. South Asian immigrant communities in the secular West have often developed stricter religious identities than their counterparts back home. What explains this paradox? According to the Terror Management Theory developed by psychologists Jeff Greenberg, Sheldon Solomon and Tom Pyszczynski, when people feel anxious because their familiar world has disappeared, they hold on tighter to their core beliefs to feel secure. The mosque, temple or the gurdwara in a Western country offers this, more than they do in the homeland of the immigrants. In his book Religions of Immigrants from India and Pakistan, the American sociologist Raymond B. Williams posits that religious outlets abroad become the main places where immigrants reinstate their otherwise unsure presence. Inside these spaces, they are no longer treated as outsiders. Worried that their children will forget their roots, immigrant parents use special religious classes outside the curriculum in secular Western countries. The British scholar Pnina Werbner, in her studies of British Pakistanis, talks about a “cultural freeze.” While people in South Asia continue to debate, modernise and change their views over time, immigrant communities often freeze the religious and social rules of the exact decade they left. A family that left Lahore or Ahmedabad in the 1980s might strictly follow conservative rules that their own cousins back home are likely to have moved away from. The Pakistani-American historian Ayesha Jalal and the French scholar Olivier Roy point out that the blended traditions of South Asia do not travel well. According to Roy’s idea of “deculturation”, when religion is separated from its home soil, what survives is a simpler, literalist and stricter version of it. Becoming more religious abroad is also driven by politics, shaped by political tensions brought over from South Asia. In many Western countries, especially under multicultural policies, governments organise immigrant groups by their religion rather than their language or region. As the British-Pakistani political sociologist Tariq Modood has demonstrated, British citizens from South Asia quickly realised that local councils, politicians and the media in the West do not deal with them as members of an ethnic group, but as ‘British Muslims’, ‘American Hindus’ or ‘Canadian Sikhs.’ Due to the fact that the government recognises general religious labels, people have a strong reason to organise around their faith if they want a political voice. At the same time, rising prejudice, Islamophobia and anti-immigrant attitudes trigger what American sociologists Alejandro Portes and Rubén Rumbaut call “reactive religion.” According to their research, when a minority group feels judged, turning toward religion becomes a way to defend themselves. Wearing visible religious symbols such as a hijab, a turban or a tilak becomes a public statement of strength — at least to the wearers. This is made even stronger by what the Irish political scientist Benedict Anderson terms as “long-distance nationalism.” Free from the daily struggles of living in South Asia, many immigrants develop an idealised version of their homeland, or what it ought to be like. The American anthropologist Arjun Appadurai explains in his book Modernity at Large that television and social media bring the bitter religious/sectarian/communal conflicts of South Asia straight into immigrant living rooms. With more money to spare and a desire to prove their loyalty to their home country, some people abroad eagerly fund religious groups and political parties back home, taking part in homeland politics from a safe distance. The old movie image of the modern, non-religious ‘West-returned’ traveller gave way to fears of ‘Western immorality’ and finally to a reality where immigrants became more conservative and orthodox. But here’s the irony: many South Asians leave their countries looking for better jobs, fair laws and personal freedom. Yet, once they arrive in secular countries, they often rebuild the exact religious walls they left behind. Instead, it prompts them to self-consciously institutionalise and reconstruct sharper religious boundaries than those they navigated back home.
