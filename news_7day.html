Some respite in Sisyphean quest to end polio in Karachi

KARACHI: They are foot soldiers. Their only weapon: polio drops in a small blue icebox with an ice pack to keep the vaccine cool, even though they themselves may be sweating and exhausted in the scorching heat. These health workers (mostly women as they are allowed inside homes) fight for the eradication of polio in Pakistan, one of only two polio-endemic countries in the world alongside Afghanistan. Sadly, in both Pakistan and Afghanistan, the fight against the crippling disease has run into rumours and misinformation, especially among the uneducated. Many not just refuse to let their children receive the drops, they even turn violent if shown some sense. And yet in the face of all adversity the women carry on with their duty. From 8am to 5pm, they walk miles as they go door to door asking if there is any child there under the age of five to administer the drops to. Some 80 to 90 homes within a district are covered daily and the cycle must be repeated twice, even thrice sometimes, during a seven-day campaign. For safety reasons each team is also accompanied by a support team, including police and other personnel from the commissioner’s office and the Sindh government’s health department. It was during such a campaign last year when in the field the Deputy Commissioner of District Central, Taha Saleem, heard of a problem that they had been overlooking while concentrating more on covering areas to administer vaccines, target number of children and the children missed because of family refusals. Through small lunches and Qinqi rickshaws, District Central administration helps make the enormous task of vaccinating thousands of children less daunting One of the polio workers quietly diverted his attention to the amount of walking they have to do while covering areas. Though her colleagues laughed it off, the deputy commissioner got thinking. There happened to be tens of thousands of homes in his district to be visited by around 4,000 polio workers who had to walk from eight to 10 kilometres daily to complete their task. In order to arrive on duty at 8am, many of the workers left their homes before dawn, around 6am. And their work doesn’t usually finish precisely at 5pm. Realistically, it is 7pm as they update their data. The area supervisors who look after three to four teams even stay till 9pm. During this while they also barely ate even though their stipend of Rs1,500 per day included food and transportation allowance, which they would not spend to save money. So it came up to working for over 14 hours on an empty stomach. Something had to be done. And it included food and transportation for the polio workers. Memorandums of Understanding (MoUs) were signed for this purpose with Saylani Welfare Trust and the Bohra Community for food and the Town Municipal Corporation for transportation. In District Central now, from 1pm to 2pm lunch is supplied during campaigns and they are also provided with transport in the shape of seven- to nine-seater rickshaws or qinqis. The qinqis bring the workers to the field and they remain parked there until the workers are done. The drivers of these vehicles have each polio worker’s number, too, to make sure no one is missed while transporting them from here to there. According to Taha Saleem, the little consideration shown to the polio workers has increased their turnover. “Since last year, when we started facilitating them, we have also been able to retain the workers. They have all returned this year,” he said. Dr Aneel Kumar, who liaisons between the DC Office and the health department, said that thanks to transportation provided to the workers, they are also able to keep the vaccine fresh the entire day. “Their ice packs used to melt. But now they are able to go back to their facilitation centres to get fresh ice packs,” he said. Kanwal Bashir, a polio worker, told Dawn that earlier to save money they used to have a samosa or a packet of chips, which would serve as lunch for them. “But now we get to eat better. The transport also helps us replace our ice pack every three to four hours to keep the vaccine fresh,” she added. The next polio campaign starts on Monday (today). Tapping the potential of 5G

In March, Pakistan’s three mobile operators paid about $510 million for something no one can see, hear or touch. The long-delayed 5G auction nearly tripled mobile airwaves, but airwaves alone change nothing. A licence does not teach a child, and a faster network will not, by itself, create a job or make a hospital more capable. 5G is infrastructure, and infrastructure is judged by what is built on it. The question is no longer how fast Pakistan can connect, but what it can become when people, machines and institutions connect, compute and respond in real time. Great infrastructure is built for what is possible, not merely for what is demanded. When telecom operators launched the country’s first 4G service in 2014, mass demand for mobile data was not yet evident; it arrived with the capability. Pakistan stands at a similar threshold. Broadband penetration has more than doubled since 2019 to nearly 66pc, according to the Pakistan Telecom Authority (PTA), and ICT exports reached a record $4.6 billion in 2025-26. The digital economy is not waiting to be born; it is already here. 5G will decide how far, and how fast, it grows. Faster smartphones are only 5G’s most visible layer. Just as an express lane eases every other lane of a congested motorway, new spectrum draws off data-hungry users and frees capacity for those still on 4G. The larger prize lies beyond the handset. A factory can use sensors to detect faults before they halt production; a small business can draw on cloud computing and AI without its own servers. Here, 5G stops being a telecom story and becomes an economic one. Faster smartphones are only 5G’s most visible layer, the larger prize lies beyond the handset; with cloud and AI, 5G forms a new operating layer for the economy Picture a midwife in rural Sindh consulting a Karachi specialist by video, a wheat grower in south Punjab receiving irrigation advice in Punjabi, or a Faisalabad entrepreneur serving clients in Dubai without leaving home. The technology largely exists; the task is to join up network, cloud, AI and data with, above all, skilled people. But ambition without inclusion risks widening the very divides it seeks to close. Though networks cover over 92pc of Pakistan, the GSMA estimates only 58pc of adults used mobile internet in 2025, held back by affordability, digital literacy and social norms. The gender gap in usage shrank from 25pc to 8pc in a year, yet Pakistan still has the widest phone-ownership gap among surveyed countries: 68pc of women against 93pc of men. The goal, therefore, is not the fastest network but one within everyone’s reach. That requires affordable devices, digital skills, dependable power and stronger backhaul, as well as rationalised taxes, local device assembly and the Universal Service Fund resources for underserved communities. No Pakistani should be shut out by geography, income or language. The goal should be a network within everyone’s reach. That requires affordable devices, digital skills, reliable power, rationalised taxes, local device assembly and the USF resources for underserved communities. China is an example. By end-2025, nearly two-thirds of Chinese mobile users were on 5G. More telling is its strategic shift from a carrier of calls and data to a provider of computing power and intelligent services, with a 6G testbed fusing communication, sensing, computing and AI. The lesson: 5G creates most value as a platform, not an end in itself. With cloud and AI, it forms a new operating layer for the economy. Fixed wireless access can bring broadband where fibre is uneconomical, AI can tune networks in real time and sensors can bring visibility to farms and supply chains. Pakistan need not wait for 6G to think this way. Global capability, however, creates value only through local relevance. Pakistan needs a large language model that understands its languages and culture, along with an AI assistant accessible through SMS, USSD and voice calls. This would ensure that people using basic mobile phones are not left behind. The future must be built for the next 100m users, not just those already connected to the internet. The digital value Pakistan creates should be captured, and compounded, at home: freelancers exporting services, start-ups scaling on local cloud, talent competing globally without emigrating. No single sector can deliver this alone. Operators must invest; the government must offer predictable policy; the regulator must enable efficient spectrum allocation and infrastructure sharing; device makers must cut costs; universities must build AI, cloud and cybersecurity skills; and public institutions, among the largest users of technology, can accelerate adoption. Pakistan’s 5G journey has begun; the next chapter is about turning connectivity into capability. Success will be measured not in speed tests but by whether a doctor can reach a distant patient, a farmer can make a better decision, a freelancer can win a global contract and a Pakistani business can compete internationally without leaving Pakistan. The real question is not how much faster 5G can make a phone, but how far it can take Pakistan. The network is being built. Now comes the harder task: deciding what to build on it. BRICS accelerates green transition

THE BRICS countries are united by the collective goal of combating climate change. As BRICS membership has expanded, so has the potential for the BRICS countries to contribute to the global green transition. This is especially true regarding climate finance and technology, where the BRICS collectively possess deep capacities. Recent international challenges have strengthened the case for BRICS cooperation. According to the OECD, the United States’ aid cuts resulted in the largest ever decline in foreign aid on record in 2025, while growth in global climate finance lost momentum. In June, the World Bank scrapped its target requiring 45pc of its financing to produce climate co-benefits, a move widely tied to US pressure. That makes practical South-South cooperation a necessity in global green finance and technology. The BRICS-founded New Development Bank provides the institutional basis for mobilising resources, with a solid pipeline of projects. The NDB took shape while BRICS countries were simultaneously negotiating the Paris Agreement. The bank became fully operational in 2016 — the year the Paris Agreement entered into force. This means that unlike earlier development banks, the NDB was designed when climate was already a top global concern. This enabled members to embed green finance into the NDB from day one. The treaty establishing the NDB — the bank’s supreme legal instrument — tasks it with mobilising resources “for infrastructure and sustainable development projects”. What this means for climate finance is spelled out in plans and policies. The NDB’s 2022-26 general strategy targets 40pc of financing to be approved for climate mitigation and adaptation. The same strategy rules out financing any new coal-fired power capacity. The NDB approved $5.59 billion in climate finance during 2022-25, representing 44pc of total financing over the period and beating its own 40pc target during this period. While these activities are similar to those of other development banks, the NDB has engaged in legal experimentation that reflects its nature as an institution wholly owned by emerging economies and developing countries. Its green debt framework, while designed to align with the global benchmark Green Bond Principles and broadly consistent with the sustainable finance taxonomy of the European Union, also leaves scope to issue bonds aligned with the standards of individual BRICS members. The bank has also pursued local currency financing to reduce foreign exchange and other risks to borrowers. Its first green bond was denominated in the renminbi and was the first green bond issued by an international institution in China’s domestic market. In 2025, local currency financing accounted for about 46pc of new project approvals, a record share, mainly in renminbi, rand and rupee. Overall, 29.3pc of all NDB financing was in the currencies of bank members by the end of 2025. However, the vast majority of the NDB portfolio remains denominated in US dollars. After a decade of operations, the NDB has established itself as a growing source of climate finance. With the expanding of membership, the bank’s strategy during the next five-year period is likely to continue to target greater financing in member currencies while strengthening focus on technologies. However, the NDB’s impact is constrained by the bank’s scale. Since inception, the NDB has approved almost $43 billion by early 2026 across all projects. That is meaningful for the Global South, but scaling the BRICS contribution to climate action requires direct cooperation among the BRICS members. The members of the grouping recently started to explore the potential for closer climate cooperation within BRICS frameworks, welcoming a memorandum of understanding on a BRICS Carbon Market Partnership in 2024. In 2025, the BRICS leaders endorsed a broad climate leadership agenda and adopted a cooperation framework for climate finance. In addition to potential cooperation on a BRICS Carbon Markets Partnership to share expertise, build capacity and link carbon markets with climate finance, the framework identified potential to develop a catalogue of national sustainable finance taxonomies to identify areas of convergence, while also considering promotion of “interoperability” of different approaches. This activity to promote cross-border green finance flows could draw lessons from the EU-China (later joined by Singapore) common ground taxonomy and ASEAN’s sustainable finance taxonomy. There are also positive signs for greater cooperation between the NDB and national development finance institutions. Another 2025 document proposed options for enhancing BRICS cooperation on climate tech, including setting up a BRICS climate “patent pool”, as one of several non-binding models, alongside humanitarian and open-licensing proposals, to widen access to climate-related technologies. The key question is how many of these activities will be put into practice and how many will remain on paper only. The voluntary, non-legally binding format of BRICS cooperation provides flexibility but can also mean limited clarity about how to implement plans and assess results. Finally, BRICS climate action could be strengthened through deeper engagement with the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change institutions and other development banks. Following the BRICS leaders’ call on multilateral development banks to enhance collaboration with climate funds, the NDB could explore partnership agreements with the Green Climate Fund and the Adaptation Fund. The NDB could also build on its initial engagement with the UNFCCC’s technology transfer body, the Climate Technology Centre & Network, to develop a pipeline of de-risked climate-tech projects. While the NDB already participates in the Multilateral Development Bank climate action group, with common methodologies and joint reporting, it has engaged in little joint lending with other MDBs, unlike the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank. Greater co-financing would be another way to stretch the NDB’s resources. Following next month’s APEC meeting in Shenzhen, attention in China will turn to China’s chairing of BRICS in 2027. The opportunity will be to build out the BRICS contribution to an evolving ecosystem of climate action standards and financing. A might-is-right world

THE world is passing through one of the most volatile periods in modern history. It is more unstable today than at any time since the end of the Cold War. The international system is fragmenting with rising big-power tensions. The old order with whatever rules it had is gone. Power shifts continue to reshape the international landscape, marking the advent of a multipolar era. The disregard for international law and norms by big and regional powers has left countries having to navigate a rule-less terrain at a time of great uncertainty. Hard power has made a comeback. Powerful countries and regional powers are using military force or other coercive instruments to try to bend other states to their will. The threat or the use of force has become an option of first and not last resort, and the means to prevail over others. These actions defy international law and global norms but continue with impunity. Diplomacy only happens after missiles have been fired. Conflicts proliferate across the world — two major wars, between the US-Israel and Iran and between Ukraine and Russia as well as Israel’s genocidal war in Gaza and military actions against its neighbours. There are a record number of conflicts today. A study by a Swedish university has counted the highest number of conflicts between states in 2025 since World War II. There are 65 active conflicts according to its data for last year. The risk of a global conflagration is also heightening. Can this push the world to the brink of a third world war? A new book seeks to answer this frequently raised question. War is Interested in You: The Breakdown of International Order and the Threat of World War Three by distinguished British columnist Gideon Rachman is an insightful book on a subject that is especially relevant today. He borrows the book’s title from Trotsky: “You may not be interested in war, but war is interested in you” — meaning war will come whether anyone wants it or is thinking about it. Rachman identifies three fundamental reasons for the surge in conflicts and breakdown of international order. The first is the shifting global balance of power which is challenging US dominance. The second is the rise of what he calls ‘strongmen leaders’ who regard war as a necessary and legitimate tool to achieve their ends. The third is a consequence of the first two; as these trends take hold international rules and law are breaking down. “A law-governed world is giving way to a might-makes-right world,” he writes. In explaining these causal factors, Rachman argues that the relative decline of the Western alliance and America’s retrenchment from its global role created an “opening” for “revisionist” powers dissatisfied with the present world order to take more risks. He cites Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and China’s more aggressive posture on Taiwan as cases in point. Trump’s use of military force against Iran, in defiance of international law and ignoring the views of its allies, has further contributed to the collapse of global order. Can distinct regional conflicts merge to push the world to the brink of a third world war? Greater international insecurity also stems from the emergence of a new generation of self-styled strongman leaders — Putin, Trump, Netanyahu — who deride the idea of a rules-based international order. Their acceptance of territorial annexation and belief in ‘might-makes-right’ has heightened the risk of international conflict. Rachman suggests that both Trump and Putin subscribe to the “spheres of influence” theory, which they see as a more reliable guarantor of peace and stability than a rules-based order. The starkest expression of this, he asserts, is Trump’s revival of the Monroe doctrine for the Western Hemisphere. In what Rachman characterises as a “might is right” world, growing global insecurity is driving up defence spending. Global rules and international institutions are being upended by strongman leaders, increasing the risk of more wars. While acts of war are the most dramatic examples of the breakdown of the rules-based order, Rachman argues that international institutions are crumbling in other ways too. Trump’s trade war and protectionist policies are, for example, accelerating this erosion. Rachman examines today’s major wars and argues that while they have been distinct regional conflicts, they intersect at various points. But as these separate conflicts escalate, they risk eventually merging. His concept of an ‘axis of adversaries’ of the West or ‘axis of upheaval’ challenging the US-led world order greatly exaggerates the degree of cooperation between Russia, China, North Korea, and Iran whose interests are hardly identical. They may all want an end to US hegemony but that doesn’t make them partners in an alliance acting in unison. The argument does not stand up to scrutiny. Rachman is unable to convincingly establish the “practical and ideological” links he posits between them. In fact, he himself acknowledges the limits of the Russian-Chinese partnership and strains among the four countries. To the question of whether a conflict-ridden world is heading towards a global conflagration, Rachman’s answer is carefully qualified. A world war, he argues, would have two defining characteristics. One, the world’s major powers would have to be in direct conflict as they were in the First and Second World Wars. Two, the conflict would have to span continents. These conditions do not obtain today but they cannot be ruled out in the future.He sees a direct conflict between the US and China as the greatest risk. Rachman devotes a chapter to last year’s conflict between India and Pakistan. It adds little to what has already been written about the military confrontation between the two nuclear neighbours. He sees the four-day conflict in May 2025 as a serious setback to India’s ambitions and efforts to de-hyphenate from Pakistan. Among the lessons he draws from this episode is that “the risks of a very dangerous conflict in South Asia get too little attention compared to other global trouble spots”. He argues that the risk of future conflicts is heightened by the fact that both countries deployed increasingly sophisticated weapons during the crisis. This is an intelligent book well worth reading for its sharp perspectives on war and peace even if one might disagree with some of the author’s assertions. Agriculture: How flawed wheat policy fuelled flour crisis

Wheat flour, once a modest household expense, has become an existential daily struggle for millions of working people. Persistent policy failures, flawed policy experiments, and poor execution have fractured wheat and flour supply chains, threatening household food security. As flour, a basic staple, prices multiply, ordinary families are forced into impossible choices: who gets to eat and who starves, whether to serve a full meal or make-do with less, and whether to put food on the table today or make ends meet tomorrow. Roti has become something to be rationed, negotiated over, and sometimes sacrificed altogether. As a result, what is unfolding is far more than a price crisis; it is a gradual unravelling of the country’s social fabric. Millions are being pushed deeper into poverty as food insecurity tightens its grip and hunger becomes a structural feature of everyday life. At its core, this is a crisis of human dignity – an indictment of a system unable to protect the most fundamental condition of a decent life: access to affordable food. Punjab’s aggregator model turned out to be an utter disaster as nine out of 11 companies could not even start purchasing owing to banks’ reservations. Others managed to procure 160,000 tonnes of grain against 3m tonnes target Let’s see how we got here? Over the past five years, flour prices have surged from Rs50 per kg to current Rs150 to Rs170 nationally - a staggering over 200pc increase. In Punjab, the country’s breadbasket, flour is selling for over Rs200 per kg in deficit districts like Rawalpindi. The price of roti has already surged by more than 212pc – from Rs8 in 2021 to Rs25 in 2026. For the first four of the five years since 2021, government-supplied flour provided a lifeline to the most vulnerable segments of society. This year, Punjab holds barely 25pc of its own requirement, leave alone serving other federating units, because of its failed experiment with procurement, leaving the private sector with a dictatorial share of stocks and power over prices. The price climb has been even steeper this year: from Rs79 to Rs180 per kg. Government-supplied flour, regardless of the quantity released, is officially priced at Rs110 per kg, while the private flour sells at Rs150 per kg – a difference of Rs40, or 36.4pc. This price differential, over and above normal profit margins, opens a window for retailers to reap windfall gains at the expense of consumers. As prices continue to climb and people’s purchasing power erodes, the pattern of purchase is changing accordingly: consumers who can no longer afford a full 10kg bag are turning to loose flour in smaller quantities to meet their daily needs. Retailers, responding to this shift, are opening officially supplied wheat flour bags and selling it by the kilogram at private market rates. Consequently, every purchase is being diverted into retail margins. The roots of the crisis lie in Punjab’s decision earlier this year to abandon conventional wheat procurement under pressure from lenders and replace it with a bureaucratically conceived scheme to engage private-sector aggregators to procure wheat on its behalf. Under the plan, these aggregators were to be helped obtain bank loans and use public warehouses to keep strategic reserves of three million tonnes of wheat. But the scheme ran into trouble from the outset. Disputes over financing terms deterred banks, while the substantial gap between the government’s procurement rate and the open-market price stalled farmers from selling to the aggregators. The result was a spectacular failure of execution: against the ambitious target of 3m tonnes, the aggregators managed only 160,000 tonnes. Nine out of 11 aggregators were not able to even start purchase as banks shied away. Even the Bank of Punjab refused to be part of the financing arrangement. The failure looks even more glaring against the backdrop of the scheme itself: the government had only withdrawn from direct procurement but was shouldering the financial cost and risks of maintaining reserves. The plan neither achieved the targeted stocks nor freed the government from the costs and risks it was supposed to manage. The scale of the failure should have jolted the government into action as soon as the procurement drive ended in May this year. But it did not. Instead of firming up an import plan to bridge the emerging supply deficit, it continued to insist that the entire crop was stocked or hoarded within the country and would eventually find its way into the market. In support of this argument, crop size was grossly exaggerated. This argument was valid, but only up to a point. Hoarders and stockists release their inventories when they anticipate ample supplies from other sources and fear financial losses of keeping stocks. They do not when they know they control a decisive share of supplies and feel no competitive pressure. Punjab’s current release of approximately 3,500 tonnes of wheat daily, compared with the traditional supply of around 25,000 tonnes, appears insufficient to stabilise the market. This represents only 14pc of the traditional daily supply, leaving an 86pc shortfall. Can such a release exert pressure on stockists to release their stocks or bring prices down? The answer is a big no. Wheat would continue pouring in from private stocks but at a price of choice of those who control the supply. That is precisely what is happening.
