LAHORE: Heavy smog pushed air quality in several Punjab cities into the hazardous category on Friday, with Lahore recording an hourly AQI reading of 465 between 7am and 8am, while Gujranwala and Multan registered readings of 661 and 641, respectively, according to real-time air quality data. Lahore’s historical hourly air quality graph classified the reading of 465 as hazardous, indicating extremely polluted conditions and serious health risks for residents. A separate ranking of Pakistani cities placed Gujranwala at the top with an AQI of 661, followed by Multan at 641 and Lahore at 426. Other Punjab cities also reported elevated pollution levels, including Sargodha (289), Sialkot (232), Rahim Yar Khan (228) and Bahawalpur (198). The readings indicate a widespread deterioration in air quality across Punjab, with several cities experiencing hazardous pollution levels that pose serious health risks, particularly to children, elderly people and those suffering from respiratory and cardiovascular diseases. Residents have been advised to minimise outdoor activities, keep windows closed, wear protective masks when going outside and use air purifiers indoors. Prolonged exposure to high concentrations of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) can aggravate respiratory and cardiovascular conditions. The deterioration in air quality comes despite intensified enforcement operations by the Punjab Environment Protection and Climate Change Department against sources of pollution, particularly non-compliant brick kilns. According to departmental figures, enforcement teams conducted 84,370 inspections of brick kilns across the province between Jan 1 and Nov 18, demolishing 1,429 units, sealing 968 and registering 1,248 FIRs against violators. Fines totalling Rs178 million were also imposed. During the critical smog period from Sept 1 to Nov 18, the department conducted 29,999 visits, demolished 519 kilns, sealed 494 and registered 408 FIRs, besides imposing fines totalling Rs101.45m. In the first 18 days of November, enforcement teams conducted 7,509 visits, demolished 101 kilns, sealed 161 and registered 96 FIRs, while imposing fines totalling Rs27.6m. On Nov 18 alone, 552 brick kilns were inspected, 22 demolished and 19 sealed. Fourteen FIRs were registered and fines totalling Rs3.5m were imposed. The department has also introduced repeated inspections to monitor previously inspected kilns and identify units that resume environmentally harmful practices after initial enforcement action. Environment Protection and Climate Change Department Secretary Silwat Saeed has directed sustained field enforcement and coordination to address pollution at its sources, while Director General EPA Punjab Abdullah Nayyar Sheikh has maintained a zero-tolerance approach towards environmental violations. Meanwhile, Pakistan’s average PM2.5 concentration stood at 67.3 micrograms per cubic metre in 2025, nearly 14 times the World Health Organisation’s annual guideline of 5 micrograms per cubic metre, according to the 2025 World Air Quality Report. This concentration corresponded to an average AQI of 156, placing Pakistan among the world’s most polluted countries. The latest readings underline the continuing challenge of controlling air pollution in Punjab despite enforcement measures against industrial and other sources of emissions.

Islamabad province?

OPPOSITION to it has been largely suppressed, but all is not well within the ruling regime. One way or the other, the proposal to break up existing federating units will ultimately shape the regime’s endgame. Stage one has been completed without major resistance, the PTI disarmed through the imposition of emergency in KP, and a neo-administrative experiment comprising 12 districts in the southern and ex-Fata regions operationalised. The ultimate prize for the establishment is the long-desired separation of Karachi from the rest of Sindh. And this is where the Bhutto-Zardari clan, facing an existential threat, will be forced to play its hand. But before this, there are likely to be other moves expected to trigger less resistance, both within the ruling coalition and outside it. One of these is the plan to make Islamabad into a province. The bureaucracy rules Islamabad without impediment. The only proposal on record, finalised by a committee headed by Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal, has advised a 27-member assembly, headed by an elected chief minister/mayor. The elephant in the room is the viceregal institution which dictates the fates of Islamabad’s three million people — the Capital Development Authority (CDA). Pakistani democracy is, at best, an aspiration rather than a functioning reality connected to the will of the people. Elected institutions operate under pressure, while most who claim to be representatives of the people wage class war daily. Nevertheless, the pretence of democracy remains. The federal capital is a glaring exception. The bureaucracy rules here without impediment, the popular will be damned. There has been one citywide experiment with local government since the capital was created in 1960. The 2016 elections brought the Municipal Corporation of Islamabad (MCI) into existence, under the nominal authority of an elected mayor. The mayor and elected representatives down to the level of union councillor had virtually zero legislative and financial power. Elections never took place again, and the MCI was transformed into yet another bureaucratic feeder to the CDA. So if Islamabad is given a legislative assembly, will the CDA be cut down to size? I choose my words carefully — because those who are poised to ‘give’ Islamabad an assembly have, over the past year or so, made their plans for the city and its environs sufficiently clear. A short list of ‘achievements’ reads as follows: thousands of homes in katchi abadis and historical villages bulldozed; suburban sprawl in the form of land-grabbing real-estate schemes extended; thousands of trees razed and the city’s air poisoned by relentless construction. With the CDA’s Master Plan flagrantly violated by its own commercial projects and big proprietors, another post-facto amendment in zoning regulations was recently approved by the government. As for what is to come, entire chunks of Rawal Lake and many villages around Bhara Kahu will be sacrificed for a 350-acre safari park, while the area adjacent to the Bari Imam shrine — where forced evictions took place earlier this year — is being given over to hotel developers. Will Islamabad’s much-celebrated green spaces, already limited public services, and working-class settlements be left by the time the purported legislative assembly comes into place? Will the dominant imaginary of ‘development’ fronted by the state apparatus — of which the CDA is a poster-child — suddenly give way to a political-economic-environmental arrangement that serves the mass of Islamabad’s people? Only the awfully naïve believe that the plans for an Isla­mabad province — which may ex­­tend into adjacent districts like Mu­­rree and Haripur currently part of Punjab and KP respectively — are driven by democ­r­atic impulses. Seen thus, the Islam-abad case provides the most obvious indication of what the new provinces hullabaloo is all about; an attempt to subjugate the political and economic interests of the mass of the population, and future generations to boot, all in the name of efficient administration. Over the course of the past year, mainstream parties like the PPP and PML-N have made no effort to stand with katchi abadi dwellers and villagers whose homes have been bulldozed in Islamabad. They have not resisted the barrage of ‘development’ projects that have forever destroyed local ecologies. Islamabad was always the preserve of the elite, but working people fought for their rights with some success, mainstream politicians paying heed in exchange for votes. Like in the rest of the country, Islamabad’s working masses now have to exclusively wage their own political fight. Helping create paths

ONE child, born in an affluent household, goes to one of the best private, high-fee schools, gets a lot of support from home, appears for international school leaving assessments and then makes her way to the best colleges and universities of the country or of the world. When she enters the job market, she not only has her education to bank on, and the confidence that comes with it and the upbringing she got, she has parental and university networks to support her as well. Another child — and there are millions of children in Pakistan who face these odds — might have struggled to even be in a public or low-fee private school, is likely to drop out well before reaching college, but even if she reaches college, it is likely she will struggle to find a job afterwards. The quality of her education is compromised, she did not have much support from home for her learning, her confidence has not been built up, and she does not have a parental/family network or a university network to support her. The state is supposed to level the playing field amongst children. Education has shown to be an amazing equaliser and the state, in theory at least, can use educational opportunities to level some of the inequalities coming from homes and families. It is a legal and constitutional requirement in the case of basic education (Article 25A of the Constitution as well as many international commitments of our state), but it makes functional sense to do that as well: a merit-based society is likely to bring the best talent forward and that, more than anything else, becomes the engine for economic growth, social well-being and societal harmony. The state of Pakistan has completely failed to deliver the promise of ‘the right to education’. But it has also failed, and quite miserably, to deliver on social outcomes. Twenty-six million-odd children out of school, poor quality of education for most children enrolled in low-fee private and public schools, high dropout rate, small percentage of enrolled children reaching university level (roughly only 10 per cent), poor quality of university education in most private and public universities and very poor opportunities for employment even if you survive in the system and graduate from it with a degree. State policies have, in many instances, helped develop & entrench inequalities. Instead of equalising opportunities and levelling the playing field, state policies have, in many instances, helped develop and entrench inequalities even more. Funding cuts and restrictions for public education, both at school and university level, are a telling example as is the inability and unwillingness of the state to regulate provision of education by private, for-profit providers. Some effort has been made to provide scholarships, on merit, at high school and university level to help students who do well in public examinations, but, compared to the scale of poverty and the escalation in costs of education, all scholarship schemes, together, do not amount to more than a few drops in the ocean. Private not-for-profit initiatives have also been working in the area, but the scale is a big issue here. They have been and can help hundreds of thousands, but they have not been able to reach millions. If experience from other countries is to be of value, the state has to step in and play its role. The Citizen’s Foundation (TCF) is probably the largest non-profit provider of schooling in the country. It has been around for more than three decades and has served hundreds of thousands of students and employed thousands of teachers. But even today, TCF has less than 3,000 odd schools and school units that it runs. Pakistan has some 200,000 schools in operation in the country, and we still have 26m children out of school. How many TCFs do we need? It cannot be done without the state! Equally important is the form of support that is provided. Even when the state has come in it has stepped in to either reduce tuition fees (public schools are free for students and public universities are still subsidised) or provide individual scholarships based on need and/or merit. But a lot more is needed. Again, if you look at examples from other countries young people need support for health and nutrition, academic support (where such support from home is missing), counselling support to ensure better career and occupation choices, scholarships, mentoring support, support for developing non-academic but needed skills in communication, self-presentation, confidence building, and support in making transition to the workplace. These programmes, sadly, are almost completely missing in Pakistan. Even technical education, skills and training providers focus a lot more on specific skills than on issues of confidence, interpersonal skills, self-presentation and so on. But we do need these programmes as well. Here are a couple of examples from small/local initiatives. Amal Academy does fellowships with university students to develop their skills in self-presentation, to improve confidence, to ensure better self-organisation and management. This is done over a few months through mentorship programmes that are quite structured and very interactive. Institute of Lifelong Learning and Development, a new initiative, has a three-pronged strategy. They provide scholarships to ease access, development and mentorship programmes to focus on needed skills and confidence building and a service orientation, right from the start, to ensure benefiting students are ready to pay it forward right from the word go. The point of these examples is to just emphasise that equalising opportunities or levelling the playing field does not get addressed through access issues (tuition fees and subsidies) only; the state needs to do a lot more. We have plenty of examples we could learn from and bodies like TEVTA and PSDF could benefit from scaling some of these programmes. It is hard to see how Pakistan could move on the road to better human development and sustained economic growth without the state stepping in and stepping up to address some of these glaring issues of unequal opportunity sets. In hindsight

JUST as an important partner in the ruling coalition is starting to question the wisdom of the 26th and 27th Amendments, a report issued by the International Commission of Jurists has detailed the damage the two enactments have wrought on Pakistan’s judiciary. Titled Judicial Capture: Independence of the Judiciary in Pakistan after the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments, the report concludes that systemic constitutional and legislative changes have placed the country’s highest courts under the control of the political and executive branches. The ICJ comprises judges and lawyers from around the world working to “develop and strengthen national and international justice systems”. Headed by FCC Chief Justice Aminuddin Khan, a two-judge bench, also comprising Justice Aamer Farooq, had taken up a batch of 568 petitions by private petitioners challenging decisions by the Sindh High Court (SHC) and the Peshawar High Court (PHC) upholding the cesses. The FCC bench examined the constitutional limits of provincial taxation in relation to imports, exports, customs-related transactions and the movement of goods across provincial boundaries. The Sindh infrastructure cess controversy originated in 1994 and was subsequently governed by the Sindh Development and Maintenance of Infrastructure Cess Act, 2017, which provided for taxes on goods entering or leaving Sindh from or for outside the country through air or sea. On June 4, 2021, the SHC upheld the levy. Similarly, the KP Infrastructure Development Cess Act, 2022, imposed cess on the transportation and movement of goods in the province. Section 14 of the KP Finance Act, 2024, amended Section 3 of the 2022 Act to enhance the cess rate from one to two per cent. The PHC, through its Nov 14, 2025 judgement, dismissed 157 petitions challenging the levy and upheld its legality. The judgements of both high courts were subsequently challenged before the FCC. The petitioners argued that the provinces lacked constitutional authority to impose fiscal levies on imports, exports and goods falling within the federal legislative domain. They maintained that the impugned laws encroached upon Parliament’s exclusive legislative competence. Consequently, the provincial provisions and the high court judgements upholding them could not be sustained. Senior counsel Hafiz Ehsaan Ahmad Khokhar, while representing the secretary of the Revenue Division and the Federal Board of Revenue (FBR), submitted that the impugned provincial levies had to be examined in the light of Articles 141 and 142(a) of the Constitution, read with Entries 27, 43 and 59 of Part I of the Federal Legislative List (FLL) in the Fourth Schedule. He explained that Entry 27 covered imports and exports across customs frontiers, inter-provincial trade and commerce and trade with foreign countries, whereas Entry 43 specifically assigned customs duties, including export duties, to the federation. The counsel contended that under Article 142(a), Parliament had exclusive legislative competence over matters enumerated in the FLL. The constitutional character of a levy, he argued, must be determined by its true nature, taxable event, fiscal incidence, charging provisions and actual operation, not merely by its nomenclature as an infrastructure cess. A provincial levy imposing an additional fiscal burden on imported or exported goods and operating through the customs-clearance mechanism directly engaged the federal legislative domain, he said. He also referred to Article 163, explaining that the provincial power to impose taxes on professions, trades, callings and employments could not be extended to customs duties or other subjects constitutionally assigned to the Parliament. The counsel further argued that a provincial legislature could not acquire legislative competence over a federal subject merely by describing a customs-linked fiscal levy as an infrastructure cess. Meanwhile, the Sindh and KP governments defended their respective laws, arguing that the taxes were provincial levies intended to finance infrastructure development and road facilities. They maintained that it was not a customs or export duty and that the provincial assemblies were competent to legislate on such matters under Article 142(c) of the Constitution and the impugned judgements were legal. The additional attorney general supported the federation’s position, submitting that the provincial governments lacked legislative competence to impose levies falling within the FLL. After hearing the arguments, the FCC set aside the two high court judgements and declared that the legislations were beyond the legislative competence of the provinces.
