Sweeping US immigration changes take effect as 250,000 visas revoked

WASHINGTON: Sweeping changes to United States immigration policy take effect on October 1 as the Trump administration tightens scrutiny of foreign nationals while the State Department says it has revoked more than 250,000 visas since President Donald Trump returned to office. The new federal fiscal year brings changes affecting green card applicants, visa holders, some non-citizens receiving government health coverage and people seeking certain US visas. The changes include the reopening of some employment-based green card categories, tighter eligibility requirements for some Medicaid and Children’s Health Insurance Programme (CHIP) recipients, and expanded social-media screening of visa applicants. The State Department said the more than 250,000 visa revocations involved a range of alleged violations, including breaches of visa conditions, criminal activity, fraud, abuse of the immigration system and national security concerns. About 2,300 visas were revoked in cases involving so-called birth tourism, in which pregnant foreign nationals travel to the US specifically to give birth. “Every visa decision is a national security decision,” State Department spokesperson Tommy Pigott said, describing the revocations as part of an expanded vetting effort. The department has also revoked the visas of 27 current or former officials and their family members from Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru over allegations of corruption. In another move, the administration has been preparing to revoke business and tourist visas held by as many as 200,000 foreigners who entered the US on B1 or B2 visas and subsequently applied for asylum. Deputy Secretary of State Christopher Landau has criticised what he described as “bogus asylum claims”, arguing that the asylum system was intended to protect people facing persecution rather than provide a way around immigration restrictions. The administration says a large number of asylum applications have contributed to a substantial backlog in the system, with some cases taking years to resolve. The visa crackdown follows a large number of revocations reported in August, when the State Department said it had cancelled more than 175,000 visas. The department attributed most of those cases to encounters with law enforcement involving offences including assault, driving under the influence, theft and drug-related crimes. Other cited offences included reckless driving, sexual assault, child abuse, fraud and embezzlement. Green cards For immigrants seeking permanent residence, the most closely watched development is the State Department’s October 2026 Visa Bulletin. The monthly bulletin establishes the dates that determine when many family-sponsored and employment-based green-card applicants may proceed with their applications. It contains separate “Final Action Dates” and “Dates for Filing” charts, although applicants adjusting their status inside the US must also follow instructions issued by US Citizenship and Immigration Services. The start of the new fiscal year on October 1 also resets annual employment-based visa limits. The State Department said some employment-based categories had advanced for the new fiscal year, although some categories for applicants from countries other than those subject to special per-country limits had also retrogressed. Indian employment-based applicants are among those closely watching the changes. The October bulletin lists, for example, a February 1, 2023 final-action date for Indian applicants in the EB-1 category, November 1, 2013 for EB-2 and January 1, 2014 for EB-3. The department had exhausted the fiscal 2026 allocation for Indian applicants in the EB-2 category in May, but said the annual limits would reset on October 1, allowing qualified applicants to receive visas under the new fiscal year allocation. Expanded social-media screening From October 1, the State Department is also broadening its online presence review to additional nonimmigrant visa categories. The new screening covers foreign media representatives seeking I visas and Canadian and Mexican professionals and their dependants applying under TN and TD classifications. Applicants in those categories are instructed to make their social media profiles public or open for review. The department says it uses information from available sources during visa screening to determine whether applicants are eligible and whether they pose national security or public safety concerns. Other changes taking effect around the start of the new fiscal year concern access to public health programmes. Some non-citizens receiving Medicaid or CHIP face tighter eligibility requirements, potentially affecting their continued coverage. For immigrants already in the US or waiting for applications to be processed, the consequences will vary according to visa category, country of chargeability, immigration status and the applicant’s priority date. Indonesia haze chokes neighbours, but little legal recourse

Haze from Indonesian wildfires has blanketed neighbouring Malaysia and Singapore for more than a month, closing schools and sending respiratory complaints skyrocketing. It is not the first time Indonesian haze has caused a regional pollution crisis, but neighbouring countries have few options for legal recourse, experts say: Case at the ICJ Suggestions that Malaysia and Singapore take Indonesia to the UN’s highest judicial organ, the International Court of Justice in The Hague, face a basic obstacle: “The main issue is jurisdiction, as the ICJ generally requires the consent of both states,” said Professor Mohd Hazmi Mohd Rusli of Universiti Sains Islam Malaysia. The ICJ can only hear a dispute where states have accepted its jurisdiction, for example through a special agreement, compatible declarations recognising the court’s compulsory jurisdiction, or a treaty clause referring disputes to it. Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore are neither signatories nor parties to the 1979 Convention on Long-range Transboundary Air Pollution, a European-centred treaty framework intended to curb cross-border air pollution. All three countries are parties to the Asean Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution, but the 2002 deal has no clause on litigation before the ICJ. What about Asean? The Asean Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution is a legally binding regional treaty, intended to prevent and monitor cross-border haze from land and forest fires, and to mitigate its effects. But its enforcement provisions are limited. “It is legally binding, but it does not provide a mechanism for one member state to simply sue another,” said Hazmi. Instead, it seeks to solve the problem through “national action and regional cooperation”. Legal analysts say Asean’s emphasis on consensus, sovereignty and non-interference puts the brakes on how much diplomatic pressure can be applied to member states over domestic land management and fire enforcement. Misunderstanding Pakistan

THE foundational basis of Pakistan and the objectives behind the country’s creation remain a subject of debate. One side emphasises religion above all else and argues that the basis for creating Pakistan was Islam itself. On the other hand, some commentators contend that Pakistan was created for the residents of specific regions, the majority of whom were Muslims, so that they could live their lives freely and without subjugation. Although religion was undeniably important, the primary goal was to secure the people’s free will and freedom. Even without debating which worldview is accurate — if any — we can agree on a few truths. First, Pakistan was born through the will and struggle of its people. Second, the decision to create Pakistan was achieved and affirmed through their votes. And finally, Pakistan was brought into being through a process that can, arguably, be considered democratic. In essence, the establishment of Pakistan was people-centric. And when the ‘people’ in question share a single culture, language, history, and geography, it becomes easy to categorise them as a monolithic community with a singular identity. However, when a nation comprises diverse ethnic groups with distinct cultures and histories running parallel to one another, neatly situated in separate regions of a land seeking to unite, the mechanics of political representation necessarily change. Unlike a single monolithic community represented by a unitary voice, multiple distinct communities seeking union often have separate representatives, at least initially, to protect their specific interests within the broader polity. The union is not the merging of a single people, but a voluntary arrangement among distinct communities with shared goals. To achieve this, these communities, through their representatives, discuss, negotiate, and agree on the union’s dynamics, defining which powers are transferred to the central state and which remain local. Commentators frequently overlook the foundational premise of the nation itself. This process transfers sovereignty from individual communities to a united entity that becomes greater than the sum of its parts, while protecting regional rights and identities. The goal of such a union is a better shared future; unity is seen as offering greater benefits than division. This fusion of distinct groups into a singular national framework is the essence of entering into a social contract. Pakistan, which is home to diverse ethnicities and communities, is said to have entered into precisely this type of union right after independence. For many, that social contract was earlier epitomised by the Lahore Resolution of 1940; for others, it was crystallised in the 1973 Constitution. Whichever document one examines, the formal recognition and celebration of diversity are recurring themes. Pakistan was always intended to be pluralistic, with communities coming together to pool their resources and cede certain freedoms for the collective good. Pakistan came into being for them, not despite them. Abdul Wali Khan articulated well the vision of what Pakistan could be, noting that despite their distinct identities, the federating units were akin to “flowers with their distinct colours and fragrance” that “blend into a bouquet, manifesting their unity in diversity”. Ultimately, the country’s founding idea is rooted in protecting its people, their cultures, languages, and rights. That is what it ought to be, and whether anyone likes it or not, this is what it was always meant to be. Why is this discussion so critical today? Because when commentators debate whether creating more provinces is necessary for a prosperous Pakistan, whether local governments need to be strengthened, or whether addressing the missing persons issue is key to winning hearts and minds, they frequently overlook the foundational premise of the nation itself. This premise is intrinsically linked to the people’s will and aspirations. As with any major decision, altering provincial boundaries, whether by creating fewer or more provinces or restructuring existing ones, must remain a choice driven directly by the people whose goals are bound to Pakistan’s genesis. Similarly, the people must decide the role of local governments, and the resolution of the missing persons crisis must be as great a priority as the people make it. If you remove the popular will from this decision-making process, you strike at the core of Pakistan’s founding identity. You not only carve up administrative boundaries and dismiss popularly held convictions, but also dismember the very vision articulated in the 1940 Resolution, codified in the 1973 Constitution, and reaffirmed through the 18th Amendment. Democracy is neither a luxury that Pakistan cannot afford nor is it a failed concept. If anything, when allowed to function, democratic governance is the single thread that has kept this federation together throughout its turbulent history. It maintains the vital link between the state and the populace that gave it birth. If new administrative units are to be carved out or other consequential decisions to be taken, those decisions belong exclusively to the public, not to self-appointed arbiters who claim to know better than the citizens themselves. After all, armies did not create Pakistan; the people’s vote did. War tacticians did not envision Pakistan; politicians, lawyers, and civilians did. A war did not lead the nation to independence; a barrister did. Consequently, the bond between the Pakistani state and its citizens is as intrinsic and consequential as water is to fish, instruments are to musicians, and ‘national interest’ is to our armed forces. If you remove the people from decision-making, you strip away the soul of the country. What remains is merely a land without a people, a people without a voice, and a country without a purpose, with nothing left but despair.
