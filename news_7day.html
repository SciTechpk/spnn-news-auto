Let them eat cake

YET another World Bank report has been published highlighting the unending immiseration of Pakistan’s working people. Pakistan’s economic plight is described as largely on par with war-torn countries in the Arab region like Syria and Yemen. Unsurprisingly, the bank makes only limited mention of its own role in relentlessly enforcing the ‘structural adjustment’ policies which have greatly contributed to mass impoverishment. But the report is damning nonetheless. Almost 20 per cent of Pakistan’s 260 million people now fall into the extreme-poor category, defined as a daily income of less than $3. Extreme poverty has increased three-fold in less than a decade. If the poverty line is simulated at the slightly higher daily income level of $4.20, almost half of Pakistan’s people are poor. It is not all doom and gloom, of course, because Pakistani capitalists, landlords, and state mafias are doing very well. This is reflected in inequality markers. What economists call the Gini coefficient is at its highest level in almost 40 years. The report goes on to name a series of ‘shocks’ which have triggered the huge uptick in poverty; the Covid pandemic and the 2022 floods top the list. Missing is a meaningful political economy analysis in which class exploitation, colonial statecraft, and aid dependency are accorded the centrality that our sordid experience demands. In a nutshell, Pakistan’s masses are the targets of a scandalous class war that becomes more intense with each passing day. The main protagonists of this war are Pakistan’s own militarised ruling class and its external patrons, including the World Bank, IMF and bilateral ‘partners’. Even where the World Bank report notes that rising poverty and inequality is partly the result of a “prolonged period of economic adjustment that weakened real household incomes and employment opportunities”, the mention of the actual players that push the adjustment agenda is conspicuous by its absence. And there is nothing about Pakistan’s own state mafias, capitalists and landed magnates who happily pass on spiralling debt burdens to the people, whilst profiting endlessly through real estate and trade, with no concern for livelihoods and ecologies. Working families are eating less on the whole. There have been donor reports, which go beyond superficially describing social crises and actually name some names. In 2020, the UNDP’s annual Pakistan National Human Development Report explicitly stated that Pakistan’s militarised ruling class benefits from hidden — and sometimes overt — subsidies to the tune of $17.4 billion per annum. Six years later, one can assume that this figure has grown; it is another matter that donors are seemingly unwilling to name the protagonists of this class war as explicitly now as in 2020. The truth deficit extends to our own political classes and intelligentsia. Yes, one could argue that ‘elite capture’ has become a widely used phrase. Petrol bombs and other markers of inflation come in for regular criticism and parties like the Jamaat-i-Islami are given licence to undertake sanitised protests. But the political and intellectual mainstream is least concerned with the banality of everyday class war in comparison to intra-elite palace intrigues and diplomatic ‘successes’ in the geopolitical realm. Indeed, mainstream political conflict in Pakistan features only shades of the right, and the object of politics is to secure the good graces of the establishment, which in turn continues to rely on the age-old policy of securing geostrategic rents from external patrons. It is thus that the mass of Pakistan’s people has been thrust further down the class ladder for at least a decade, and arguably longer. Throughout this period, the actual political-economic order has remained largely unchanged, irr­espective of who has occupied the formal seat of government. The accumulation of crises has made the present moment particularly desperate. The ruling regime proclaims its macroeconomic achievements while promising that the windfalls of its great accomplishments abroad will materialise soon. But the statistics that they do not advertise tell the real story. Working families are eating less on the whole and even less nutritiously than before; kids are pulled out of school and forced to work to supplement meagre household incomes; even those who imagine themselves as white-collar workers, let alone those who are in the category of extreme poor, contemplate killing themselves and their offspring to preserve their dignity. It is thus that one is reminded of the 18th-century ‘French princess’ to whom the reply ‘let them eat cake’ was attributed, when her advisers explained that the mass of her subjects were not able to afford bread. Public good and spaces

NINETY-FOUR per cent of youth had no access to a library, 93pc to a sports facility, and 78pc to a park. Ninety-seven per cent had never been to a live music event, 94pc to a sports event, and 97pc to the cinema. A whopping 71pc had no access to any of the above. These figures are from a survey conducted for UNDP’s Pakistan National Human Development Report 2017: Unleashing the potential of a young Pakistan, of which Dr Adil Najam and I were the lead authors. It was a national survey that highlighted a youth population with few opportunities for positive engagement outside their home. Since 2017, the economy has not been doing well. We have not had any significant positive changes in national human development outcomes, and the impact of Covid and global slowdown has hardly been encouraging. So, one would expect that since conditions have not changed much, a similar survey carried out today would show numbers that are not very different from those reported then. I have been staying in London for the last couple of months. There is a large park about 400 metres away from our place. The park is managed by the local council (local government). It has a walking/ jogging track running along its almost kilometre-long perimeter. The park has two multipurpose fields. I saw them being used as cricket fields in August and they were converted into football and rugby fields in September. There are three hard-surface tennis courts on one side. There is a children’s play area with lots of structures to climb, swing from, and slide on. The play area is full most evenings. The park is utilised well. Hundreds of children, parents, and dogs visit it every day. Walkers, dog-walkers — most of whom pick up their pet’s poop themselves — and joggers are there round the clock. The tennis courts, play area, and fields get busy in the afternoon after school on weekdays. They are occupied throughout the day on weekends. Every afternoon, four to five groups of young people, with their coaches, have training sessions in the park. There are always cricket or football matches going on. On weekends, quite a few groups have picnics there too. The park is, of course, open to all. We have not had any significant positive changes in national human development outcomes. It would not be an overstatement to say that the park defines the neighbourhood and its vibe. It is a very family-oriented area and one sees that parents are comfortable allowing their young children to run around the park or ride their bike or scooters around the jogging track — often unsupervised. A lot of credit for this goes to the local council. The park does require significant upkeep for cleaning and maintaining the track, trees and plants, and the grassy fields. But, since residents can benefit from healthy activities, the council provides the needed services quite diligently. Walking down our area’s high street takes us to the local public library, which is also run by the local council. Since we are a family of readers, and buying books is not cheap, we went in to check if we could be members of the local library. All it took was proof of residence in the area to obtain membership cards free of cost. We then had access to not just the local library but all public libraries around the council (six, I think). One person can borrow up to 20 books at a time for up to three weeks. If a book is not available at our library, we can request it from other libraries in the network that have it. Over the last two weeks, my daughter has read six to seven books. Imagine the saving! And she has had fun learning and managing her own library membership card as well as the activity of borrowing and returning books. I am a little worried that the speed at which she is going, she might not have much left to read at the library in a month or two! Providing access to books is just a small part of the services on offer. The library has very inviting spaces for sitting and reading, and even desks for writing. It has free Wi-Fi and free access to internet-connected desktops for members. It also has at-cost facilities for printing and photocopying, and a decently priced cafeteria. There are also some rooms and spaces that can be booked for group activities. I have seen language-learning, meditation, and book-discussion clubs using these spaces. It has been a bit of a surprise to see a broad cross-section of society using these facilities. The book section for the young is always bustling. And from teenagers to retirees, all age groups are well represented throughout the day at the library. The local council provides all these public spaces. Local accountability for local issues seems to work well here. It is difficult to list all the benefits that accrue to the citizens from these two spaces and the free services that come with them. There are opportunities for physical, mental, and emotional engagement through group activities, social interaction, reading and learning, and hobbies and interests. It is important for all people to have such opportunities for these healthy activities, so that they can interact positively and learn to work together, and, generally, become engaged citizens. The contrast between where we need to be and the findings of the survey of Pakistani youth mentioned earlier could not be starker. Beneath the surface

THE IMF’s staff-level agreement with Pakistan on the fourth balance-of-payments and the third climate financing reviews came with little drama — which itself says something. The mission spent two weeks in Islamabad, wrapped up the 2026 Article IV consultation, and left with a deal that releases $1.2bn once the IMF Executive Board approves it. Nobody expected a different outcome. The Fund is unlikely to push hard against a borrower that is hitting its fiscal targets. Compare this with March, when talks went virtual, ended without an agreement, and dragged on until the Board cleared funds in May. The recent round made fewer headlines because the hard bargaining over the budget had already been done. The current budget is built around a primary surplus of 2pc of GDP, and the lender is now collecting on that commitment. A programme that has settled into a routine reassures markets. It also shows how little room Islamabad has to depart from the script. The lender’s own assessment is generous. It says the authorities have successfully navigated the fallout of the Middle East conflict and that policies have preserved stability. Reserves stand at about $21.5bn and inflation has eased from its May peak. Yet, a closer look shows that, at 10.3pc in September, inflation remains well above the State Bank’s medium-term target range. Last year’s growth of 3.6pc, with an annual population growth of nearly 2pc, leaves per capita gains thin. The World Bank put poverty at about 42.4pc for 2025, and said growth was too weak to prevent an additional 1.9m people from slipping below the line in a single year. Exports tell the same story. Goods exports fell about 6pc to $30.1bn, of which textiles accounted for $17.9bn. Imports rose 8pc to $69.8bn, widening the merchandise trade deficit to $39.6bn. Growth in IT services kept total exports near $40bn, which though welcome, is no substitute for a manufacturing base that sells more abroad. The recommendations deserve more attention than the headline figure. The lender wants the fuel support scheme phased out, calling it costly and too broadly targeted. Unwinding a broad subsidy is politically harder than agreeing to end it on paper. It also wants timely tariff adjustments so that the circular debt does not return, a medium-term tax strategy, private participation in power distribution, better governance of SOEs, and progress on privatisation. Many who have read the earlier review statements will recognise the list. When the same agenda is repeated, the question one must ask is: how much has changed? The Fund’s language of commitment does not answer it. The tranche will arrive. What the government has yet to demonstrate is an economy that grows fast enough to cut poverty and sells more abroad — an issue that cannot be put off much longer. ISLAMABAD: The government on Thursday increased additional customs duty (ACD) on import of tyres and motorcycle parts produced locally and approved first-loss coverage for small and medium enterprises and small farmers. The decisions were taken at a meeting of the Economic Coordination Committee (ECC) of the cabinet, presided over by Finance Minister Muhammad Aurangzeb. The committee also approved investment of Export Development Fund (EDF) resources in government securities and allowed Rs57.85 billion in supplementary grants a day after a staff-level agreement with the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and at the conclusion of the first quarter of the fiscal year. Under the decision, the government increased ACD from 4pc to 11pc on import of radial tyres and motorcycle parts that are manufactured locally. It also increased ACD from 11pc to 31pc on motorbike parts imported by local manufacturers. Both measures are aimed at facilitating local manufacturers of radial tyres and motorcycle parts. “On a summary submitted by the Revenue Division, the ECC approved an amendment to SRO 693(I)/2006 concerning the levy of additional customs duty on import of tyres being manufactured locally. The measure is aimed at promoting domestic manufacturing,” an official statement said. The ECC also approved a financing framework developed by the State Bank of Pakistan to facilitate the inclusion of Agency Financial Institutions (AFIs) under the government’s existing Risk Coverage Schemes for Small Enterprises and Small Farmers through wholesale and agency arrangements. The framework provides for first-loss coverage to loans up to 20pc for SMEs and 10pc each to small farmers and medium size industries. “The framework aims to broaden access to finance by leveraging the outreach of eligible microfinance and non-banking financial institutions,” the statement added. The ECC also approved an Addendum to the Second Supplemental Trust Deed of the Credit Guarantee Trust Fund (CGTF), originally meant for Prime Minister’s Mera Pakistan Mera Ghar scheme, expanding its scope to facilitate more effective utilisation of the existing credit guarantee facility to support affordable housing finance. The meeting approved 11 supplementary grants totalling Rs57.85bn. These included Rs2bn for the Small and Medium Enterprises Development Authority to facilitate implementation of its approved business plan in line with the prime minister’s vision. It also approved another Rs11.329bn for the Industries and Production Division to meet the immediate funding requirements of the Utility Stores Corporation and facilitate completion of its closure process. On the request of the Privatisation Division, the ECC approved Rs8bn for the Public Private Partnership Authority (P3A) to support the development and implementation of infrastructure projects through public-private partnerships.
