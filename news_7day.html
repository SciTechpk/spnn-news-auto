The restriction, however, would not apply to scholarships offered by international development partners, or to training and courses arranged through the Economic Affairs Division or under institutional agreements of the Government of Pakistan. According to the notification, Pakistan’s ambassador or high commissioner concerned would represent the country at obligatory and important events during the period. However, in case of “unavoidable visits” all ministers, advisors, ministers of state, special assistants to the premier, parliamentary and government functionaries shall “travel only in economy class”. The government further directed that official meetings will be preferably held through teleconferencing, except intra-city meetings. “No official dinners shall be hosted, except in the case of visiting foreign delegations,” the notification added. The notification also prohibited all government-funded official seminars, trainings and conferences. However, where such events were deemed unavoidable, government facilities, including auditoriums and committee rooms, would be used to host them. It further said requests for exemptions from any of the measures could be considered on a case-by-case basis by the Committee for Monitoring and Implementation of Fuel Conservation and Additional Austerity Measures. The committee would then submit its recommendations to the prime minister for approval. Earlier this week, Information Minister Attaullah Tarar said that the government was considering reviving austerity measures introduced during a previous fuel conservation drive amid renewed hostilities in the Middle East. The government had earlier imposed austerity measures on March 9 to mitigate the impact of the ongoing US-Iran war. These included a 50 per cent cut in fuel allowances for official vehicles, salary cuts for lawmakers, and a partial work-from-home policy in the public sector. The government ended the measures on June 19, except for market timings. The move comes amid renewed hostilities in the Middle East, which have disrupted major oil supply routes and led to a rise in fuel prices. In addition to the crisis in the Strait of Hormuz stemming from the US-Iran war, trade through Bab al-Mandab — which has become a vital route for Saudi oil exports in recent months — is also facing a growing threat from Houthi rebels.
