Pak-US reset?

SINCE early 2025, Pakistan’s relations with the US have taken an upturn after years of mutual disenchantment. Why did the US opt for this reset? And will this bonhomie last? Historically, Pakistan and the US have come close only when their interests converged. America has preferred a transactional relationship with Pakistan based on specific issues of its geopolitical agenda. The recent thaw in ties can, therefore, be better understood in terms of how global geopolitics is evolving. It is no longer a unipolar world led by the US. Since the dawn of this century, several centres of power have arisen while the US remains entangled in long, distant wars. The US reliance on its military might to subdue adversaries has taken a heavy toll on America’s world standing. Into this evolving geometry of global power politics stepped Donald Trump, who has ruptured the trans-Atlantic alliance, pulled back support for Ukraine in its war against Russia, and sought to stabilise America’s ties with China. However, nothing has compromised America’s domestic and international standing more than the recent US-Iran war. As for Pakistan-US ties, three developments seem to have contributed to the recent thaw. First is Pakistan’s role in countering terrorism. In March 2025, President Trump thanked Pakistan in his State of the Union address to Congress for apprehending the mastermind of a bombing in Kabul on the eve of the US exit from Afghanistan. Later, Centcom chief Gen Michael Kurilla described Pakistan to Congress as a “phenomenal partner in the world of counterterrorism”. In June 2025, Trump hosted Pakistan’s army chief for lunch at the White House. And in August, BLA was designated as a terrorist organisation by the US. Three developments seem to have contributed to the recent thaw. Second, the US, like the rest of the world, saw Pakistan robustly counter Indian aggression in May 2025. The US mediated a ceasefire on May 10. Pakistan acknowledged America’s positive role. New Delhi, on the other hand, was reluctant to acknowledge Trump’s role, even though India itself had asked the US to secure a ceasefire. Third, Pakistan’s mediatory role in the recent US-Iran conflict has convinced Washington of Pakistan’s important role as a net stability provider in the region. Meanwhile, America’s distrust of India has deepened because the latter has bought Russian oil while ignoring US sanctions, and has maintained continued economic ties with China while pretending to be a US partner to counterbalance China. America is no longer convinced that India can be a trustworthy counterweight to China. How will this reset impact Pakistan’s other key relationship? Islamabad’s close ties with Beijing are not unknown to Washington. The Chinese leadership is also well aware of the strategic nature of Sino-Pak relations. In an era where cooperation and confrontation coexist, Pakistan has ample space to pursue mutually beneficial relations with both China and the US based on its own national interest. India is upset at the uptick in Pakistan’s ties with the US, and its own failure to isolate Pakistan diplomatically. As for Afghanistan, the US has not shown any interest in it, even as the country is on the cusp of becoming a safe haven for world terrorists. The US is also aware of the fine balance that Pakistan has always maintained in its ties with both Iran and Saudi Arabia. How should Pakistan use this reset in its relationship with the US, even if it was transactional, for mutual benefit? Several promising areas can be identified: Pakistan must ensure that its share of flourishing trade with the US is maintained and enhanced. The US offers bright prospects for Pakistan’s yo­­ung, tech-savvy entrepreneurs, IT exports, startups, software de­­velopment, growing digital adoption, and e-commerce. American companies shou­­ld be encouraged to invest in Pak­istan’s shale oil potential and minerals sector, especially lithium, rare earth elements, and copper — crucial for the semiconductor industry, defence technology and robotics, among others. Other promising areas of cooperation include defence, security, and counterterrorism. Agriculture has been a legacy area of cooperation since the 1950s, as have education and public health. However, all this will only be possible if Pakistan addresses its policy and regulatory hurdles, cost of doing business, security risks, and infrastructure issues. Today’s Pakistan stands tall as a competent, self-confident power that can contribute to regional peace, stability, and development. However, this window of opportunity may be limited because global geopolitics is constantly evolving. What matters most is making the best use of the recent thaw in Pakistan-US ties while preserving our other key relationships, including with China. The nuclear danger at sea

Dawn’s October 23 editorial rightly warned about India’s nuclear-armed submarine patrols. The danger is larger than a new vessel at sea. It is a sea-based nuclear force growing alongside precision-strike capabilities, missile defence and a false confidence in initiating military action. Imagining that these developments confer a first-strike advantage or create a safe space for war is imprudent. Pakistan has the capability and resolve to preserve the strategic balance. Retired Lt-Gen Mazhar Jamil, Adviser to the National Command Authority, warned at the International Institute for Strategic Studies on October 13 that continuous at-sea deterrence further weakens the credibility of India’s declared no-first-use policy and points towards an offensive strike posture. He added that unannounced submarine-launched ballistic-missile tests, with operationalised weapons aboard submarines, raise the risks of misperception and escalation. Pakistan reserves the right to take all appropriate measures to protect its security and the regional balance. At the Islamabad Non-Proliferation Conference a week later, retired Lt-Gen Khalid Kidwai, also an adviser to the National Command Authority, reinforced the warning, saying Pakistan would review its options if India sought to impose a strategic imbalance. In June 2026, the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (Sipri) estimated that India had a stockpile of 190 nuclear warheads and 12 of these deployed on a nuclear-powered ballistic-missile submarine for occasional deterrence patrols. That estimate is too low and arrives seven years late. Prime Minister Modi announced the INS Arihant’s first deterrence patrol in 2018 and the Indian Navy again patrolled it again during the 2019 crisis. India has three operational ballistic-missile submarines (SBBN), with a fourth expected in 2027. Next year, the four SSBNs will have a combined capacity to launch missiles carrying up to 72 warheads from 24 tubes. A larger fleet of 12 nuclear ballistic and attack submarines is envisaged in the 2030s. Aggressive posturing India’s professed no-first-use pledge cannot be judged without taking into account its capabilities and political conduct. A ready, concealed launch platform gains a different meaning when combined with counterforce ambitions, missile defence and talk of pre-emption. General Kidwai’s assessment about a possible strike at ‘zero notice’ should be read in that setting. Pakistan has never allowed its deterrence capability to be neutralised by India’s posture that is taken as routine modernisation. Strategic stability rests on a credible balance and Pakistan will maintain it under all circumstances. General Zubair Hayat, former Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff Committee, argued at the Islamabad Non-proliferation Conference that the old international order is giving way without an equitable replacement. His strategic point applies here: strategic exemptions for a favoured state, followed by alarm at its neighbour’s balancing measures, do not produce equal security. The nuclearisation of the Indian Ocean did not begin when Pakistan raised its concerns. It began with India’s risky choices, enabled and fuelled by an international tendency to treat its ambitions as benign. May 2025 demonstrated the danger of that assumption on land and in the air. On the first anniversary of Marka-i-Haq, President Asif Ali Zardari reiterated that India used Pahalgam’s false flag as a guise for premeditated strikes. India ignored Pakistan’s offer of a neutral investigation and had to endure a defeat and loss of face. Pakistan’s calibrated conventional response, under political authority, restored conventional deterrence, while Full Spectrum Deterrence preserved the strategic ceiling. The Indian Air Force grounded itself, and the Indian Navy’s carrier strike group cut a hasty retreat to safety. The ceasefire followed Islamabad’s response and New Delhi’s call for third-party mediation. There was no safe band of war then, and there is none at sea now. A lesson for India Retired Lt-Gen Sarfraz Sattar, who served as Director General of the Strategic Plans Division, has also warned that emerging technologies and great-power competition threaten nuclear stability. The March 2022 BrahMos launch into Pakistan showed why. A supersonic nuclear-capable missile crossed the border in peacetime; India waited nearly two days before acknowledging it and did not accept Pakistan’s call for a joint inquiry and preserved the risk of escalation. Three years on, India carried that risk into wartime by employing BrahMos against another nuclear power — the first such instance. The sea-leg of India’s nuclear triad and dual-capable missiles are sinews of a Hindutva-inflected policy of hegemony and coercion. Pakistan’s declared Full Spectrum Deterrence covers the range of threats it faces, and it clearly signals that aggression at any level or in any domain risks a response that will deny objectives and impose costs; an adversary cannot dictate the level at which a confrontation will remain. Political authority, prepared forces and credible options give that warning its weight. As India’s posture changes, Pakistan will take the measures necessary to safeguard its sovereignty and strategic balance. Risk reduction has value, but it cannot substitute for deterrence or responsible political choices. Dialogue is worthwhile when it reduces the incentives and opportunities for unilateral force, not when it is offered as a substitute for Pakistan’s defence. Pakistan always stands ready to impose greater costs for on any misadventure that India may undertake, including the maritime domain. In May 2025, Islamabad demonstrated that its calibrated notch-up response should not be read to mean that every aggression will meet the same ceiling. General Kidwai also recommended adding a “plus” to Pakistan’s quid-pro-quo plus response strategy. The lesson for India is direct. Neither an expanding submarine fleet nor other developments give it an edge over Pakistan’s response to aggression. Pakistan has demonstrated both resolve and the means to defend itself. It will preserve the balance on which peace depends. In the Subcontinent, the true enemy is the delusion that war can be kept safely on a lower rung. That Indian aspiration makes the unthinkable look controllable. It’s an illusion that a war can be started low, kept low, and stopped on command. A political choice driven by Hindutva and Akhand Bharat may begin the escalation; interaction decides where it goes. Field Marshal Asim Munir expressed the governing reality most aptly: there is no space for war in a nuclearised environment.

Political radicalism sits uneasily within the paradigm of mainstream politics. Interestingly, this phenomenon has increasingly manifested among the middle classes. The lineage of both left and right ideological extremes can be traced to two historical junctures. Left-wing radicalism finds its genesis in the 18th century French Revolution and right-wing extremism in the ascent of Nazism in 1930s Germany. In both instances, the working classes served primarily as foot soldiers. They were mobilised by middle class concepts and aspirations. The French Revolution was animated by the theories of ‘enlightened’ middle-class intellectuals and Nazism was built upon convoluted race theories conceived by middle-class thinkers. In 18th century France, radical philosophies emerged from an ascending and fledgling middle class that felt its economic and social progress impeded by the remnants of the ancien régime, such as decadent monarchs, landed nobility and an exploitative clergy. These were overthrown through bloody upheaval, driven largely by downtrodden mobs. However, when this triumph’s middle-class architects attempted to translate their radical philosophies into governance, they plunged the nation into further turmoil. This instability was checked by the rise of Napoleon Bonaparte, who established a dictatorship. The resultant political trajectory finally matured into the secular democratic system that endures in France till today. The maturation was a synthesis of clashing ideas, crystallised by an overwhelming need for moderation and stability in a country that had been turned on its head. From revolutionary France to contemporary Pakistan, radical movements have repeatedly demonstrated how the pursuit of political absolutism can become an instrument of self-destruction By the 1930s, Europe’s middle class was expanding its influence within this evolving arrangement. But the arrangement was suddenly unsettled by the expansion of socialist movements following the 1917 Bolshevik Revolution in Russia. As socialist ideologies gained political traction among the growing working classes, the middle class began to experience severe insecurity. They felt they were being squeezed between entrenched elites and increasingly active working class constituencies. In Germany, this middle class anxiety manifested as hostility toward democracy. It began being blamed for Germany’s economic ruin, military humiliation and the perceived infiltration of communism. To the middle classes, communism threatened the socio-economic hierarchy through which their own aspirations were sustained. Levitsky and Ziblatt suggest that the main problem with bringing radical ideas into mainstream politics is that they simply do not work with normal governance or the art of working together. Governing effectively requires making deals and knowing when to compromise. Radical political figures who refuse to compromise or work within institutional rules eventually undermine their own ability to govern. They view political rules and traditions not as guidelines, but as traps set by their ‘corrupt’ rivals. Their strict ‘no-compromise’ attitude makes it nearly impossible to build stable alliances. Moderate voices inside the party are slowly pushed out or silenced. On a social level, political radicalism creates deep divisions in society and leaves the public exhausted. Indeed, tapping into anti-establishment anger can be an effective way to turn economic frustration into massive street rallies, but keeping that radical momentum alive requires the constant sustaining of the outrage. This leads to protest fatigue. According to the Dutch political scientist Cas Mudde, radical populism splits society into hostile camps, turning normal debates over policy into dramatic ‘us versus them’ battles. Over time, average citizens, the more thoughtful voters, and the business community grow weary of the continuous turmoil. The country becomes politically exhausted and economically fragile. The researchers Erica Chenoweth and Maria J. Stephan concluded that when society begins to crave stability, radical politics rapidly loses its broad electoral appeal. Its practitioners become isolated. When radical leaders urge their followers to confront state authorities directly, public sentiment gradually begins to move toward the desire for order and security, legitimising the state’s countermeasures. In 1972, the British psychologist Irving Janis demonstrated that radical movements are driven by collective arrogance and a charismatic leader’s illusion of invincibility. Such leaders often develop “hubris syndrome”, mistaking mass rallies for absolute mandates. They believe they are immune to legal or institutional limits and begin to couple their personal political survival with the destiny of the nation itself.
