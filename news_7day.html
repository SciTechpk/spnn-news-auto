Parliament’s place

MUCH has changed in just a little over a decade. In 2015, when the question of Pakistan’s involvement in the conflict between Saudi Arabia and Yemeni rebels last arose, the then defence minister, Khawaja Asif, opened the issue for debate before a joint sitting of parliament. Days later, after a marathon session, it was the then finance minister, Ishaq Dar, who presented the parliamentary resolution affirming Pakistan’s support for Saudi Arabia’s territorial integrity, while conveying a desire to “maintain neutrality in the Yemen conflict so as to be able to play a proactive diplomatic role to end the crisis”. In 2026, the Foreign Office tells us that the recent signing of the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement and the commitment of military capabilities under it are executive functions that do not necessarily require parliamentary approval. That is a rather large departure. Ironically, in his Friday briefing, the FO spokesperson, while rejecting criticism of the apparent lack of political consultation on the matter, pointed to the participation of Defence Minister Khawaja Asif and Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar in the Strategic Political and Defence Committee. He mentioned it in support of his assertion that members of parliament and the federal cabinet had not been completely cut off from the decision-making process. But the two ministers ought to be asked: is the risk calculus of the conflict any different from before? And if the policy is the same as in 2015, why not say so in parliament, as they did then? One hopes they have satisfactory answers to these questions. The secrecy surrounding the matter gives the impression that, in 2026, parliament is being deliberately sidelined in a matter in which it was deliberately involved in 2015. Meanwhile, the third party in the trilateral Makkah defence agreement, Turkiye, is taking the agreement to its parliament. Regardless of what the law allows, it would be better if Pakistan took the same route. The fact that our troops are already on Saudi soil, and have been so for decades, is no answer, especially since the terms of the new joint defence agreement have not been made public, and, therefore, their consequences cannot be judged. A handful of ministers in a committee deliberating behind closed doors cannot be claimed as a substitute for a parliamentary debate. Where Pakistan’s involvement in a foreign conflict is concerned, the public’s representatives should have been taken into confidence. Pakistan has always been unequivocal about its commitment to protect Saudi Arabia’s territorial integrity and the holy sites — that is not in question here. Broken record: Friendship with Britain and Hindustan

With the advantage of hindsight, one has the opportunity to study the historical cycles in a nation’s life. Thanks to this newspaper’s unique, extensive archive of back issues, one can examine the major political, economic and social trends that have shaped Pakistan and the world over the last seven decades and counting. Cliched as it may sound, history repeats itself, and we — as a state and as a polity — largely refuse to learn from it. Through this platform, we will look back at the ‘big’ stories that made it to the front pages of Dawn, as well as significant developments tucked away in the inside pages, from the birth of Pakistan onwards, and try to put them in perspective. We will study the critical issues of the week, starting from Aug 14, 1947 and examine the major themes and issues that made it to the paper. Though Dawn’s archive goes back further, we have chosen to start from the day Pakistan was born. The news items have been selected for their relevance — in the eyes of this writer — to the modern day as well as their historical impact. ‘Friendship with Britain and Hindustan’ The front page of the Dawn Delhi edition of Aug 14, 1947 captured the zeitgeist of the time: a fond farewell to empire, and hope for the future. Quaid-i-Azam Mohammad Ali Jinnah, while delivering a speech at a state dinner in honour of Lord Louis Mountbatten, the last viceroy of India, spoke of the British warmly, saying Pakistan would “endeavour to create and maintain goodwill and friendship with Britain and our neighbouring dominion — Hindustan. …” Though relations with Britain saw ups and downs, they have remained generally cordial. The same, obviously, cannot be said for India. Mere months after the Quaid would express his desire for good relations with India, both nations went to war over Kashmir, as Maharaja Hari Singh signed the Instrument of Accession to India, a process that would spark a dispute that remains unsolved to this day; despite how New Delhi may spin it, Jammu and Kashmir remains a disputed region. Moreover, Pakistan and India have fought numerous major and minor wars, with the last conflict breaking out in May 2025 after the Pahalgam incident. The unfinished business of Partition remains unfinished, and has resulted in a permanent state of hostility between Pakistan and India. A single-column news item on the front page reflected another, forgotten territorial dispute that has now become a part of history. It mentioned that the princely State of Junagadh, in the Kathiawar region of what is now India’s Gujarat state, had decided to join Pakistan. Mr. Ismail Abramani, Secretary for Constitutional Affairs to Junagadh State, “met Mr. Jinnah … and communicated to him the decision of the State.” Interestingly, when the Muslim nawab of Junagadh decided to accede to Pakistan, India did not accept it and occupied the state in November 1947. Yet the Kashmiri Hindu maharaja’s accession to India was perfectly acceptable to New Delhi. The Indian rulers of the time clearly shifted the goalposts when it suited them. Trouble in Lahore But with great hope there was also trepidation. The inside pages carried reports of communal disturbances which reflected the frenzy that accompanied Partition. A grim report from Lahore told the reader that “most of the sectors in the Walled City are on fire and burning unchecked”. It added that the military and police had been “firing incessantly in several places, where arson and looting was rampant”. So volatile was the situation that the district magistrate clamped an 11-hour curfew on many parts of the Punjab capital; three military brigades had to be moved in to control the situation. Disturbances had also been reported from Amritsar (East Punjab) and Moradabad (UP). Though Independence held great promise for the people of the subcontinent, freed as they were from colonial subjugation, as the record shows, the bitter rivalry between the erstwhile dominions of Pakistan and Hindustan has persisted, while communalism remains entrenched on both sides of the border. The pages of Dawn would over the decades tell these — and other — stories that shaped the future of Pakistan, and made it what it is today. It is these stories we will seek to examine every week.

The pushed river

THE Indus River is moving east in central Pakistan. Along an 85-kilometre stretch from upstream of Layyah city down to Taunsa Barrage, it is pressing hard against its eastern bank. In this year’s flood season, it destroyed Basti Kanjal, a riverine settlement of more than 500 houses some 15km northwest of Layyah. It did not stop there, washing away several more settlements. Since June, close to 5,000 acres of cultivated land have been lost; according to news reports, 100,000 people have been displaced. A close analysis of 38 years of satellite imagery shows that the river is not wandering. It is being pushed. Ask any elder in the riverside villages of Churkin or Basira which face the Vehowa hill torrent’s mouth 30km upstream of Layyah about the river of his childhood, and he will tell you the Indus was 10 miles (16km) wide. He will also tell you that the river gave as much as it took: land drowned on one bank surfaced on the other, and the patwari’s records followed the water back and forth. The satellites agree with him on the width. Between 1988 and 1997, the active belt below Churkin measured 16km to 18km, and above Taunsa Barrage nearly 24km. Today, it is reduced to a third. A river squeezed into a narrow gut runs deeper and faster, and expends its vigour on eroding vulnerable banks. Three engineering structures, built with public money and international loans, have together hemmed in the river on three sides. The first of these is the Chashma Right Bank Canal, financed by the Asian Development Bank. The canal runs through the Suleiman hill torrents. These are no small streams: the Vehowa alone drains a catchment of over 2,600 square kilometres and has recorded flood peaks above 35,000 cusecs. Before the canal, such floods spread across the Daman plain under the old rod-kohi system, watering the land and dropping their silt on the way. After 2003, the canal’s 68 cross-drainage structures began delivering every flood, with its full load of mountain sediment, to fixed points on the river’s right bank. Between 2011 and 2016, two great fans of new land rose out of the riverbed — one at the Vehowa’s mouth opposite Layyah, and one at the mouth of the Sanghar torrent near Taunsa city. Together, they hold some 37sq km of new permanent land, a metre and more above the old riverbed, and about 100 million cubic metres of sediment. Where the trained torrents deliver their load, walls have risen; in the intervening stretches, the river’s western edge hasn’t moved in 38 years. The canal itself has been closed since the 2022 floods, but its silt walls remain standing in the river. A close analysis of 38 years of satellite imagery shows that the Indus is not wandering. It is being pushed. The second factor is Taunsa Barrage. Built in 1958 to pass a million cusecs, its capacity fell by half within five decades as sediment accumulated. A wedge of silt today stands nearly 2m high near the pond and stretches 40km upstream, flattening the river’s slope and promoting further deposition. The World Bank’s 2005-08 rehabilitation project raised the crest and built a secondary weir downstream. But the embankments were never raised proportionately. Local communities protested and wrote to the Bank that the remodelling ignored the sedimentation, causing flood protection for structures to fail. In 2010, a flood peak of 960,000 cusecs breached the eastern marginal embankment upstream, allowing the river to outflank the structure and carve a new channel. Their warning proved correct. The third factor relates to the embankments. The marginal ones along the left bank, running some 40km from Daira Din Pannah up towards Layyah, froze the river’s eastern edge decades ago, and the floodplain strip between the river and bund has since been almost entirely converted into farms and settlements. In the 1990s, the river still reclaimed that strip in every high flood; today, 95 per cent of it has turned into permanent land. Meanwhile, the restricted riverbed is level with the protected villages outside — all that protects them now is the crest of an earthen bund. This is the fuller picture of the narrative that those affected recounted outside the Layyah Press Club this September, as they blamed two decades of Chashma Canal silt for their plight: sediment walls advancing from the west, a wedge of silt backed up behind the barrage, and, on the east, the embankments holding the door shut. A river so enclosed has nowhere to go but against the weakest point. In 2010, the weakest point was the marginal bund above Taunsa; in 2026, it was the training works at the Layyah bridge. The river has now cut to within 200m of the Lala, its old channel passing along the western edge of Layyah city. The city’s protection bund stops 3.5km short of that point. What happens if the river completes its eastward shift? The low floodplain east of the river holds hundreds of villages built on the assumption that the river would keep to its corridor. A river breaching the Lala line would place Layyah on the front line, and behind it Jaman Shah, Kot Sultan, and Paharpur. An eastward-shifted Indus would progressively outflank Taunsa Barrage itself. A barrage commands only the water that arrives at its gates; the canals of Dera Ghazi Khan and Muzaffargarh, irrigating millions of acres, depend on that command. The river would leave the barrage presiding over a dying channel. The communities of this region warned both lending institutions years ago, the ADB over the canal and the World Bank over the barrage, through formal complaints, inspection claims, and hunger strikes. I was among those who warned. I remember how patiently they were heard and how completely they were ignored. Both institutions noted lessons and closed their files. The river has now reopened them, writing its supplement in drowned villages and 5,000 acres of lost land. Reconstruction without the benefit of an honest analysis of these structural challenges would be tantamount to allowing a similar situation, or even worse, in the future. The analysis now exists, measured from orbit and verifiable by anyone. What remains is whether anyone with authority is willing to read it. The lost civilian CT body

THIS is an obituary for the National Counter Terrorism Authority, once conceived as a civilian counterterrorism body that combined policymaking, coordination among law-enforcement agencies, and data collection and processing. Since its inception in December 2008, and even after gaining legal cover under the Nacta Act, 2013, it has struggled to fully realise its role and mandate, while facing resistance from LEAs reluctant to coordinate with a police-led body. Appointing a senior military officer as its national coordinator was not the first deviation from the Nacta Act. A bigger departure from the statute had come in 2025 with the establishment of the National Intelligence Fusion and Threat Assessment Centre. Niftac was created as a parallel body, apparently under Nacta but in effect superior to it, even though Nacta is the statutory body. This gave a superior security institution the leading role. Nacta’s new form seems to have little to do with developing a counterterrorism strategy, building a coordination mechanism, or taking on a CT-related operational role. Its real purpose is to synchronise all civilian-led LEAs with the superior security institution, and, at the very least, to bring them under one roof. This synchronisation of internal security policy with the security doctrine conceived by the establishment’s top brass has been underway for a long time, taking various forms. Bureaucrats see it in the National Security and War Course (NSWC) at the National Defence University (NDU), which is now equivalent to the National Management Course, a mandatory course for promotion from Grade 20 to 21. The NSWC gives senior bureaucrats more opportunities to align with evolving thinking at the top levels of the security institutions. At the narrative level, NDU and many other military-led institutions run short courses for opinion-makers, politicians, and business and community leaders to align them with the new thought process. Interestingly, all these courses and discourses happen at the expense of civilian governments, whether provincial or federal. Nacta, the only civilian-led CT body, struggled to find its role and eventually lost. One may argue that synchronisation within the internal security structure will improve coordination, the very dream for which Nacta was conceived. However, the new changes appear to be aimed at control, and the CT body is being used as an instrument of that. The structures of other LEAs, such as the police and FIA, leave little room for intervention by other institutions — unlike Nacta. Admittedly, Nacta had a few good leaders who clearly understood the terrorism challenge facing the country and how to respond. But it is safe to say that most of its heads, and even others who served in the authority, had little idea of the CT body’s objectives. Many came on deputation, or to rest before landing a field posting. As mentioned earlier, Nacta faced pressure and challenges from superior security institutions, which were reluctant to work under or with a civilian-led body. Still, the police cadre is undoubtedly responsible for destroying the institution and providing an excuse for its takeover. This did not happen overnight. The process began much earlier. When former prime minister Imran Khan chaired the Nacta board of governors meeting in September 2018, as required by law, two key realities emerged. One, he was the first prime minister to convene a board meeting since the Act was passed, even though quarterly meetings were required. Two, the national coordinator at the time recommended that the prime minister dissolve the body, as it had no use or role in countering terrorism in the country. This was the first and last board meeting held under the original Act. In July 2019, parliament amended it through an ordinance, which it later passed in March 2020. The amendment made the interior minister the board chairman and the national coordinator its secretary. Several references to the “prime minister” in the Act were also replaced, shifting Nacta’s reporting line to the interior ministry. The 2019-2020 amendments thus downgraded Nacta from a prime minister-led federal-provincial body to one under the interior minister. Interestingly, one of the few board meetings held since then, and chaired by Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi, approved the establishment of Niftac. Just before Niftac was introduced, Nacta inducted around 130 permanent officers. Since Niftac’s establishment, most have struggled to find a role. Niftac was described as a national platform connecting more than 50 federal and provincial departments and agencies — supported by provincial fusion centres — to improve intelligence coordination, speed up threat assessment, and strengthen information-sharing across institutional boundaries. That was Nacta’s purpose. A military or intelligence institution can possess information that a civilian authority cannot access. It can conduct operations a civilian institution cannot. It can assess threats from a classified perspective. But the state also needs an institution that can sit openly with provincial police, civil administration, prosecutors, courts, parliamentarians, researchers and international partners. It needs an institution whose authority is intelligible within the civilian legal and administrative system. Against this backdrop, Niftac overshadowed Nacta.
