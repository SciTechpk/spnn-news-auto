July deadliest month of 2026 as 205 lives lost, 401 terrorists killed: PICSS

The Pakistan Institute for Conflict and Security Studies (PICSS), an Islamabad-based think tank, said in its monthly security assessment that fatalities among security personnel and terrorists rose substantially during the month as terrorist violence increased and security forces intensified counter-terrorism operations. According to the report, at least 112 security personnel lost their lives during July, the highest monthly toll since January 2023 and the second-highest recorded in more than a decade. In January 2023, 114 security personnel were martyred, including around 102 in the suicide bombing at Peshawar Police Lines, the report recalled. It said 401 terrorists were killed during July, the highest number recorded in a single month in more than a decade. On the other hand, 74 civilians and 19 members of peace committees lost their lives in July. The death toll among peace committee members was also the highest recorded in a single month during the past decade. According to PICSS, the 232 injuries during July included 101 security personnel, 103 civilians, 14 peace committee members and 14 terrorists. The data showed that, compared with June, security personnel deaths increased by 195pc, rising from 38 to 112. Terrorist deaths increased by 109pc, from 192 to 401, while civilian deaths rose by 21pc, from 61 to 74. The institute recorded at least eight suicide attacks during July, including five vehicle-borne suicide attacks. The think tank noted that this was the highest number of suicide attacks recorded in a single month during the past decade. Kidnapping incidents also doubled during the month, rising by 100pc from 33 in June to 66 in July. Province-wise breakdown It stated that the security situation deteriorated most significantly in Balochistan, where the overall death toll increased by 241pc, from 109 in June to 372 in July. Security personnel deaths in Balochistan increased from six in June to 62 in July, representing a rise of 933pc. Terrorist deaths rose from 75 to 238, while civilian deaths increased by 93pc, from 28 to 54. Eighteen members of peace committees were also killed in the province. According to the report, mainland Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, excluding the merged districts, recorded 154 deaths in July, compared with 75 in June, representing an increase of 105pc. The number of people injured rose from 60 to 98. Security personnel deaths in mainland KP increased from 12 to 38, while terrorist deaths rose by 192pc, from 37 to 108. Civilian deaths, however, declined by 73pc, falling from 26 in June to seven in July. One member of a peace committee was also killed in the province. Data showed a slight decline in overall deaths in the merged districts of KP, formerly known as Fata. The region recorded 79 deaths in July, compared with 85 in June. The number of people injured in the merged districts declined by 37pc, from 60 to 38. Security personnel deaths increased from six to 11, while terrorist deaths declined by 24pc, from 72 to 55. Civilian deaths rose by 86pc, increasing from seven to 13. In Sindh, it recorded two terrorist attacks in which one security personnel member was killed. The province had recorded 12 deaths in June. Security forces also arrested six suspected terrorists during counter-terrorism operations in Sindh. Punjab, Islamabad, Azad Jammu and Kashmir, and Gilgit-Baltistan witnessed no terrorist violence during July. However, security forces arrested three suspected terrorists during operations conducted in Punjab. Overall, the data showed that the first seven months of 2026 witnessed 2,786 deaths linked to terrorist violence and counter-terrorism operations. Those killed included 1,857 terrorists, 463 civilians, 434 security forces personnel and 32 members of peace committees. The average martyrdoms per month among security forces increased from 56 in 2025 to 62 in 2026, while average deaths of civilians increased from 48 to 66, and average deaths among the terrorists increased from 178 to 265 per month.

The Federal Register notice applies to B1 and B2 visas, which are issued for business and tourism travel. “Consular officers may require covered nonimmigrant visa applicants to post a bond of up to $20,000 as a condition of visa issuance, as determined by the consular officers,” the notice said. “The 2025 visa bond pilot, which provided a framework for the Department of State, the Department of Homeland Security, and the Department of the Treasury to assess the feasibility of administering a visa bond programme, has provided sufficient data to suggest that a visa bond programme is an effective tool for enforcing compliance among bonded visa holders.” In the pilot programme, consular officers could require bonds of $5,000, $10,000 or up to $15,000. The final rule eliminates the $5,000 option and raises the maximum bond to $20,000. The final rule goes into effect on August 3 when it is scheduled for publication in the Federal Register. In the list of 50 countries for whose citizens this programme applies, 30 are from Africa. US officials say the policy is intended to reduce visa overstays. Immigration advocates argue it will deter legitimate travel to the United States. Rights advocates say President Donald Trump’s aggressive immigration crackdown violates free speech and due process, while creating an unsafe environment for ethnic minorities and encouraging racial profiling. The first time I stood beneath the ceiling of the Sadequain Gallery at Frere Hall, I could not look away. Arz-o-Samawat [The Earth and the Heavens] was Sadequain’s most ambitious mural and possibly his largest. His demise in 1986 left the mural permanently unfinished, and the empty blue space that remains gives the work a strange, mystic appeal, holding in itself the same intrigue that one might feel for the promised idea of heaven. Sadequain is said to have called it his own Sistine Chapel. I came across the same mural again recently, in miniature, on a postage stamp. On June 30, 2026, Sadequain’s 96th birthday, Pakistan Post unveiled three record-length stamps in the artist’s honour at the Pakistan National Council of the Arts (PNCA), Islamabad. Each stamp measures 280mm by 40mm, and a set of three stamps — billed as the longest in the world — are available on one perforated sheet. The stamp sheet itself features two panels from the Frere hall ceiling, alongside the entire length of another Sadequain mural, Treasures of Time. Sadequain’s gift for scale and precision showed early. As a boy, he could reproduce entire schoolbooks by hand and taught himself to draw the world map from memory. He would fill the neighbourhood walls with charcoal birds and animals, much to his neighbours’ bewilderment and annoyance. A reflection of that instinct for line and geography can still be traced in the details of the Frere Hall ceiling. These early creative habits, alongside the history of his 1961 State Bank mural, Treasures of Time, were documented by his cousin Saiyid Ali Naqvi in his book Sadequain: Artist and Poet. Four decades after his death, Sadequain’s monumental murals have found a new public canvas on Pakistan Post’s record-length stamps The Treasures of Time mural is 62 feet long and eight feet wide, tracing the history of human thought from the fourth century BC to the 20th century CE across six panels: the Greeks, the mediaeval Italians, the Islamic world, the post-Renaissance Enlightenment, South Asia and, finally, Einstein, painted alone. Forty-six figures appear along its length, among them Socrates, Leonardo da Vinci, Al-Biruni, Rumi and Rabindranath Tagore, each rendered as an impressionistic portrait identified by the tools or symbols of their calling. Walking its length feels like walking through centuries. Sadequain placed himself in the mural too, a frail, sickly self-portrait, taking his own pulse before the ninth-century physician Al-Razi, reflecting his own appreciation and deep interest in the history of ideas he was painting. Across his career, Sadequain is said to have produced more than 15,000 works. He gave many away freely, to friends, family and strangers alike, and chose to paint many of his murals inside public institutions rather than private galleries. That instinct for accessibility shaped his legacy, even as, after his death, rising collector interest pulled much of his work into private hands and into the reach of forgers. Sadequain was not just Pakistan’s foremost painter but a pioneer of modern South Asian art, and it is fitting that, even shrunk to a few centimetres on a postage stamp, the scale of his murals still comes through — although it does demand a record-length to hold them. Stamps are made for public dissemination. Issuing one carries forward Sadequain’s belief in accessible art a step further, letting anyone possess a piece of his work that is otherwise locked inside a gallery or private collection. At a moment when national narratives are so often shaped from above, the choice of what represents a country’s visual identity matters. The recent digitisation of Sadequain’s artworks at the Lahore Museum is another attempt at increasing the broad appeal of his work. In choosing Sadequain, the state has honoured a national icon and set a quiet example for what Pakistani art can still claim as its own.
