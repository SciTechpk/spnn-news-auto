Short-term inflation surges to 11.38pc due to costly food

ISLAMABAD: Short-term inflation rose to 11.38 per cent year-on-year in the week ending October 15, driven largely by higher food prices, according to official data released by the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics (PBS) on Friday. Of the 51 essential items monitored during the week, prices of 23 commodities (45.10pc) increased, while those of seven items (13.72pc) declined, the data showed. Meanwhile, the prices of 21 items (41.18pc) remained unchanged from the previous week. Short-term inflation has remained in double digits for several consecutive weeks, largely on the back of persistent increases in petroleum prices. The Sensitive Price Index (SPI), which tracks short-term inflation, slightly fell 0.04pc week-on-week, reflecting price fluctuations in both food and non-food items. Unwilling to ease the petroleum levy, the government has continued to raise petrol and diesel prices on a daily basis. The surge in energy costs is not only fuelling inflationary pressures but also indirectly boosting sales tax collections through higher prices of goods and services. This is cost-push inflation, in which rising energy costs are followed by increases in food prices. As a result, households are under pressure from higher consumer prices. The index, which comprises 51 items whose prices are collected from 50 markets in 17 cities, is calculated weekly to track changes in the prices of essential commodities and services. The items whose prices increased the most over the previous week included pulse gram (4.97pc), wheat flour (3.36pc), chicken (3.31pc), eggs (0.99pc), pulse mash (0.98pc), pulse masoor (0.95pc), firewood (0.69pc), shirting (0.58pc), cooked daal (0.55pc), diesel (0.47pc), LPG (0.42pc) and tea prepared (0.28pc). The items whose prices declined week-on-week included tomatoes (23.05pc), onions (4.90pc), bananas (2.59pc), petrol (0.70pc), rice basmati broken (0.65pc), sugar (0.62pc) and gur (0.36pc). On an annual basis, the items that recorded the highest price increases included onions (75.19pc), LPG (68.95pc), electricity for Q1 (58.59pc), petrol (49.60pc), diesel (43.98pc), wheat flour (35.13pc), chicken (20.77pc), chilies powder (15.55pc), mutton (14.90pc), beef (12.27pc), bread plain (9.02pc) and tea prepared (8.36pc). In contrast, tomatoes prices fell by 63.65pc, followed by potatoes (39.93pc), sugar (23.20pc), eggs (19.83pc), salt powder (14.86pc), pulse masoor (8.97pc), gur (8.92pc) and pulse moong (7.83pc). PHC to hear PTI chairman’s plea against emergency, governor’s rule in KP on Oct 20

PESHAWAR: The Peshawar High Court on Friday fixed October 20 for the preliminary hearing of a petition filed by PTI Chairman Barrister Gohar Ali Khan challenging the imposition of emergency and governor’s rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and seeking to have the move declared unconstitutional. The federal government on October 11 imposed emergency in KP, and placed the province under governor’s rule, taking note of the “provincial government’s deliberate disengagement from the fight against terrorism”. The move followed weeks of political tensions between the Centre and the PTI — as well as negotiations to bridge their differences — after the latter announced a long march to Islamabad to demand the release of its jailed founder, Imran Khan. The opposition had criticised the move, vowing to raise its voice at every forum, including the judiciary. The instant petition was filed on October 14 by the PTI chairman. He will be represented by a panel of lawyers, including Barrister Sameer Khosa, Salman Akram Raja, Ali Zaman, Sardar Haseeb Ahmad and Zainab Shahid. The members of the bench that will hear the petition will be announced later. In his petition, Gohar urged the PHC to declare the federal government’s decision to impose emergency and governor’s rule in KP “unconstitutional, ultra vires, coram non judice, tainted with malice in law and fact, devoid of objective material, and of no legal effect” and to set aside the proclamation. Filed under Article 199 of the Constitution, the petition named the federation of Pakistan (through the secretaries of the Cabinet Division, law, interior, and defence ministries); the province of KP; KP Governor Faisal Karim Kundi; the KP Assembly speaker; and the secretary of the KP Home and Tribal Affairs Department as respondents. Sohail Afridi, who is no longer functioning as the province’s chief minister following the imposition of governor’s rule, was also named as a pro forma respondent. Gohar challenged the “constitutionality, legality and vires” of the October 11 decision, the order deploying army troops to the province, and “all consequential and derivative executive orders, notifications, and bodies” constituted as a result. These included the establishment of the KP Advisory Council and the “arbitrary” detention and removal of Afridi as the lawful chief minister of KP, along with his provincial cabinet, which had been vested with full executive powers under Article 130 of the Constitution. Following the imposition of emergency and governor’s rule in the province, the federal government formed an 11-member technocratic KP Advisory Council. The council will assist Governor Kundi, who is now in charge of running the province, in exercising the functions of the KP government on behalf of the federal government. The PHC was further requested to declare that Paragraph 2 of the proclamation — which transferred the legislative powers of the KP Assembly to Parliament in the absence of a prior resolution passed by the assembly, as required under Article 232(1) — was “void ab initio and an unconstitutional usurpation of provincial autonomy”. The petition also urged the PHC to restrain the federation from “tabling, entertaining, or enacting any provincial legislative business concerning Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in Parliament”. The petitioner asked the court to declare the proclamation illegal and unconstitutional and to set aside, dissolve or permanently restrain all executive orders, notifications, actions and bodies created “pursuant to or under the colour of” the proclamation. These included the constitution of the 11-member KP Advisory Council, headed by Governor Kundi; the establishment of the Area Development Secretariat (ADS) across 12 districts of the province; and the removal of the KP advocate general and the purported appointment of a replacement. The ADS was established to “restore law and order, safeguard the security of the citizens of Pakistan and ensure good governance and development” in Dera Ismail Khan, Tank, Lakki Marwat, Bannu, Bajaur, Mohmand, Khyber, Kurram, Orakzai, North Waziristan, South Waziristan (Upper) and South Waziristan (Lower). The petitioner also challenged the October 11 order deploying army troops under Article 245, asking the court to declare it unconstitutional on the grounds that it could not “supplant or bypass the civilian executive and constitutional administration” of the province and could not be invoked in the absence of a lawful requisition by the provincial government headed by Afridi. Gohar sought directives requiring the respondents, including the federal government and the KP governor, to immediately disclose, produce and place before the PHC “the entire record and so-called ‘objective material’”, including Governor Kundi’s report and the federal cabinet’s summary, on which the emergency proclamation and subsequent orders were allegedly based. He also sought directives for the immediate release of Afridi and members of the KP cabinet and assembly from “unlawful executive or preventive detention”. The petition further urged the court to direct the respondents to immediately produce Afridi before the PHC and restrain the state from placing him or any cabinet members in preventive detention or transferring them outside the court’s jurisdiction. The petition claimed that, by unilaterally bypassing the elected provincial assembly over an alleged question of internal disturbance, the impugned proclamation violated “the federal structure guaranteed under Article 1, the principles of democracy and provincial autonomy enshrined in the Objectives Resolution (Article 2A), and the legislative domain of the province recognised by Article 142 of the Constitution”. On these grounds, the proclamation was “ultra vires the proviso to Article 232(1) read with Article 1, 2A, and 142 of the Constitution” and liable to be set aside, it added. Gohar further contended that the proclamation made no mention of any “grave emergency” arising from a war or other external aggression, as required by the text of Article 232(1) — nor would such an assertion be supported by facts. It added that the federal government’s claim was also belied by the fact that even at the height of terrorism between 2007 and 2015, “no emergency was ever imposed anywhere inside Pakistan”. Gohar also argued that the invocation of Article 234 was “without lawful authority, tainted with malice in law, and devoid of jurisdictional basis”.
