Sindh High Court restores dissolution of marriage provision in Christian divorce law

KARACHI: The Sindh High Court on Wednesday revived a provision of the Divorce Act of 1869 that had been omitted in 1981, enabling Christian couples in the province to approach courts for dissolution of marriage on ordinary grounds. A two-judge constitutional bench headed by Justice Yousuf Ali Sayeed ruled that the omission of such provision was inconsistent with various provisions of the Constitution. Former military dictator Ziaul Haq had omitted Section 7 of the Divorce Act through an ordinance in July 1981, leaving Christian citizens with no ground to divorce their spouses except on charges of adultery. Section 7 of Divorce Act 1869 was omitted by Gen Zia in 1981 through an ordinance; LHC had revived the provision in 2017 only in Punjab An NGO, said to be providing free legal representation to marginalised persons including religious minorities, along with two Christian citizens, had petitioned the SHC in 2023 and pleaded to declare the impugned omission unconstitutional and without any legal effect, and to direct the district and family courts of Sindh to disregard it. The petitioners argued that the impugned omission had frozen the law in its 19th-century form, compelling a spouse to remain bound for life in a marriage that had irretrievably broken down unless he or she could prove adultery, with conversion to another faith or a false accusation of adultery remaining the only practical means of exit. They also contended that such action had violated fundamental rights, including the right to liberty and dignity, and amounted to discrimination against Christian citizens in comparison with citizens of other faiths, as well as against Christian women in comparison with Christian men. The federal and provincial law officers could not controvert the submissions advanced by the counsel for the petitioners as to the effect of the impugned omission, and instead fell back on Article 270A to argue that the ordinance was protected thereunder and that fresh legislation on the subject remained under consideration in both the federal and provincial domains. The Archbishop of Karachi strongly opposed the petition and contended that it was not maintainable, as the petitioners lacked locus standi to institute proceedings on behalf of the Christian community, and that any reform must be preceded by meaningful consultation with all Christian denominations and representative bodies. On his behalf, it was also argued that the Roman Catholic Church regarded marriage as a holy sacrament governed by Canon Law, and that any reform which directly or indirectly interfered with sacramental doctrine or ecclesiastical jurisdiction would offend Article 20. The bench noted in its judgement that, apparently, the divorce law had recognised cruelty and desertion for two years or more as conduct sufficiently grave to warrant a decree of judicial separation, but not on their own as grounds for dissolution of the marriage. It said a spouse who establishes such conduct may live apart from the other under the sanction of a decree, but the bond itself subsists, with the consequence that neither party is free to marry again. “For a husband, dissolution is available only upon proof of adultery. For a wife, it is available only upon proof of adultery coupled with an aggravating circumstance, or of conversion coupled with a further marriage, or of rape, sodomy or bestiality. The disparity between the grounds available to each spouse is evident on the face of the provision,” it added. Referring to the decision of the LHC, the bench observed that it was a lucidly reasoned judgement where the vires of the impugned omission was examined on the touchstone of constitutional guarantees of dignity, equality and freedom of religion with great sensitivity. After the 18th Amendment, it noted that the issues of marriage and divorce came within the residual legislative competence of provinces and by virtue of Article 270AA(6), every law with respect to a matter in the omitted list that was in force immediately before commencement of the amendment continued to remain in force until altered, repealed or amended by the competent authority. “The Act, in the form in which the impugned omission left it, is therefore an existing law that continues to operate in this province, the competent authority to alter it now being the Provincial Assembly of Sindh, which has not done so,” the bench remarked. “Equally, a husband and wife who have lived apart for many years and who both desire to bring their marriage to an end have no lawful means of doing so, unless one of them is prepared to accuse the other of adultery. Curtains on COP31

THE 31st Conference of the Parties (COP31) took place in the backdrop of geopolitical fragmentation, a rising cost-of-living crisis, tightening of national and international budgets and the imminent threat of a 1.5ºC overshoot. Despite high stakes, the talks — instead of propelling the agenda forward — highlighted significant gaps in international cooperation and revealed critical areas where progress was stalled, resulting in an underwhelming outcome. At its core, COP31 faced a leadership vacuum, leaving parties to navigate the negotiations with limited political direction. Turkiye’s presidency for hosting and Australia’s presidency of negotiations were unable to shepherd political compromises for the highest possible ambition. The result was a watered-down draft cover decision with the lowest common denominator that failed to secure consensus and was therefore not adopted. The lack of ambition was visible across all critical workstreams. The negotiations failed to get a tangible commitment from major emitting countries to ratchet up their 2030 greenhouse gas emission reduction targets in line with the Paris Agreement. COP31 reaffirmed that multilateralism must endure as the backbone of climate action. Many nations maintained vague promises without concrete policies or mechanisms to accelerate carbon cuts. This failure underscores the persistent divide between developed and developing countries over responsibility and capacity. Financial pledges for climate adaptation and loss and damage remained grossly insufficient, especially for vulnerable countries grappling with the worst climate impacts. COP31 did not substantially increase the promised climate finance commitment, nor did it establish clearer mechanisms for operationalising this support. This shortfall deepened the trust deficit between richer and poorer nations and hampered purposeful progress. COP31 made little headway in solidifying a comprehensive loss and damage framework that would provide timely financial assistance to countries suffering from climate-induced disasters. Despite long-standing calls for compensatory mechanisms, negotiations were bogged down in political disagreements, reflecting the complexities and sensitivities surrounding liability and responsibility. Although adaptation is a critical pillar of climate action, COP31 showcased a yawning gap between rhetoric and reality. Concrete and enforceable commitments for adaptation planning, funding, and technology transfers failed to materialise. Perhaps the most glaring omission at COP31 was the absence of decisive action on phasing out fossil fuels. Despite growing scientific consensus on the need to rapidly transition to renewable energy, the conference sidestepped a clear, actionable roadmap away from fossil fuel dependency, highlighting the influence of entrenched economic interests. While these shortcomings are disappointing, they offer a valuable opportunity for reflection and reform. The failure at COP31 should not be viewed as the collapse of the global climate effort but as an urgent signal that the status quo in multilateral climate negotiations is insufficient to meet the scale and immediacy of the crisis. This moment calls for re-energising international cooperation, urging countries to bridge their divergent interests and work with renewed transparency and solidarity. It also brings to light that the current voluntary pledging model needs firmer accountability mechanisms for enforcing compliance and incentivising larger, more actionable and ambitious commitments. The inability to get an overarching political consensus also demonstrates that countries are no longer willing to go along without assurance of financial, technical and policy support needed to adapt and recover. The setbacks also highlight the need to examine new approaches that include civil society, indigenous voices and private-sector leadership for fresh perspectives and urgency into a traditionally slow-moving process. The weak outcome at COP31 should not cause despair but create a renewed sense of purpose. Sometimes a failure in negotiations is less damaging than weak agreements (like previous COPs) that create a false sense of security. COP31 has forced a reset and serves as a crucial pivot point for rejecting unfair agreements and harnessing the moment to strengthen the spirit of the Paris Agreement, keeping its promise alive and escalating its ambition. The failure of COP31 is in fact a triumph of COP21. COP31 may have faltered but it reaffirmed the essential truth that multilateralism must endure as the backbone of climate action. The diplomatic disagreements at COP15 in Copenhagen in 2009 resulted in the milestone triumph of COP21 in 2015 in Paris. Choking the provinces

LET’S be very clear about one thing: this business of cutting the provinces up into “administrative units” has nothing to do with good or bad governance. It is mainly about recentralising control over the state’s resources. A paper written by three eminent Pakistani academics in 2005 made a related point that is worth revisiting in the present debate. The authors were Ali Cheema, Asim Ijaz Khwaja and Adnan Qadir and the document was published in 2005 as a working paper for Harvard’s Kennedy School of Government. They took a longer view of all attempts to bring in local governments in the country’s history, and came to the following conclusion: “local government empowerment has always been combined with centralisation of political power in the hands of the non-representative centre. The centralisation of political power has undermined representative institutions not only at the level of the centre but also at the level of the provinces.” Their analysis is especially relevant all over again, as the same gambit to centralise power is being rolled out for the fourth time in our history. They noted that in all such experiments in the past, “[o]ur interpretation is that these reforms have been used as a means for a non-representative centre to gain legitimacy by by-passing the political agents at the provincial and national levels”. Today almost 30pc of the federal deficit is being financed by provincial resources, and the demand from the centre for more is continuing to grow. The seventh NFC award agreed to in 2009 and made effective in July 2010 reversed the centralisation of powers and resources that the federal authorities had grown accustomed to over the decades. It was the first time in Pakistan’s history that powers and, more importantly, resources, flowed down from the federal government towards lower tiers of government in such large quantities. And almost immediately, from its very first year of operation, a thrust emerged from the federal authorities to try and undo this. It began slowly at first. Then in the middle of the decade it achieved its first written form. Here is a brief timeline of how small, subtle changes in how fiscal resources are measured and counted turned into a full-blown programme for reversing the flow of these resources to the provincial governments. In FY2011, the first year after the seventh NFC award, the federal government “successfully pressed” the provincial governments to run a surplus and not spend all the money being devolved down to them. Then the next year they asked for these surpluses again, but with no Fund programme in force, the provincial governments did not comply. This changed in 2013 when the IMF made it a prior action for provinces to post a surplus, and provincial deposits with the State Bank leapt from Rs42 billion in June 2013 to Rs257bn by March 2014. This was ratified in the Council of Common Interests, and provincial finance secretaries agreed to continue the practice into 2015. By June 2015 the IMF programme called for written agreement from the provinces to raise their surpluses, as a prior action and a few months later, in September, provincial spending was capped at 6.5 per cent of GDP. By June 2016, as the programme was winding up with only a few months left in it, the government set up the Fiscal Coordination Committee to ensure that centre and provinces coordinate their spending and surpluses. The very next year, as the Fund programme ran out, provincial governments ramped up their spending, running a deficit of Rs163bn in FY17. In July 2019 another IMF programme was signed, after replacing finance ministers. This one again made it a prior action to get provinces to run surpluses, and raised the surplus targets from one to 2.7pc of GDP. That agreement was renewed in the two years that followed, and provincial surpluses leapt from Rs77bn in FY20 to Rs314bn in FY21 and Rs351bn in FY22. That was the most troubled programme of Pakistan’s history, with more false starts than any other programme Pakistan had ever signed. The blowout came in 2024. As part of the bailout in July 2023, the provinces now had to agree not only to post a surplus, but also submit spending plans. Provincial deposits were already netted against government borrowing in the IMF’s targets for the budget deficit and SBP’s net domestic assets, thereby helping the federal government meet its targets under the Fund programme. Most importantly, the targets for provincial surpluses agreed to in that programme rose to Rs1.2 trillion in the first year, rising to Rs1.9tn by FY27. So in 2011 the provinces left unspent 13.5pc of what they received as a transfer under the NFC award. This figure fell as soon as the Fund programme lapsed, then rose again every time Pakistan entered a Fund programme. Today it stands at 19pc, skyrocketing in the last three years. Today almost 30pc of the federal deficit is being financed by provincial resources, and the demand from the centre for more is continuing to grow. In May 2025, for example, provinces were required to put surplus cash into T-bills. Their holdings of federal government debt rose from Rs19bn in June 2025 to Rs1.8tn as of October 2026, though this figure fluctuates a lot with maturities every few months. And then in June 2026, under Article 164 grants, the provinces were made to hand over Rs1.0tn to the federal authorities, or about 12.66pc of each province’s NFC share. What began as a plea to restrain spending in 2011 slowly grew into a codified demand to run a surplus. Then the targets for these surpluses were raised. Then came requirements to buy government debt paper followed by a reverse flow from province to centre as a “grant” under Article 164. And now they want to dismantle the whole set-up altogether, in the name of governance. The irony here is that it is the governance failures of the federal authorities themselves that have fuelled their hunger for resources from the provincial authorities. Red lines

INTERIOR Minister Mohsin Naqvi is right to say that freedom of expression cannot be a licence to promote terrorism, hate speech, incitement to violence or child sexual exploitation. No responsible media outlet or citizen would argue otherwise. And few would object to curbing such content on social media. Those who spread such material should expect to answer for it under the law. But drawing red lines is only half the job. The state must also show that it respects them. This is where its record raises questions. There are visible red lines against hate and violence. But there are also invisible ones that journalists and citizens encounter every day when they question powerful quarters or report matters considered inconvenient. Journalists have been picked up, threatened and intimidated for publishing stories that upset those in power. Media organisations have faced pressure over their editorial choices. Reporters often find themselves working out what they can safely publish and which subjects are best avoided. This has created a vacuum that social media is rapidly filling. So when Mr Naqvi tells journalists they are free to report, and that citizens are free to ask questions, it rings hollow. Terms such as “anti-state activity”, “misinformation” and “hate speech” are elastic. And when officials interpret them without checks, they become tools for silencing critics. Having a law or a regulator does not make its use legitimate. Enforcement agencies should operate within defined legal limits, give valid and legitimate reasons for their decisions, act in proportion to the harm and be answerable to the courts. The rules should apply equally to everyone, whatever their politics or standing. Otherwise a measure meant to protect citizens ends up policing public debate. The minister’s assurance that dissent, including criticism of the state, will be heard is welcome. But assurances count for little while media organisations and journalists are still being intimidated. The state must guarantee complete press freedom, subject only to clearly defined constitutional and legal restrictions. A sensible first step would be to bring journalists and their representative bodies, media owners, civil society and relevant state institutions together to agree on what responsible expression means in practice. Such consultations should produce a clear commitment to protect journalists, safeguard editorial independence and prevent the misuse of regulatory powers. Freedom of expression carries responsibilities. But those responsibilities cannot be imposed on the media alone. The state must also accept its own obligations and allow uncomfortable questions to be asked. Rules against violence and hatred are fair enough.
