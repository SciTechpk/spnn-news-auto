RETHINKING PAKISTAN’S AUTO POLICY

For one woman interviewed for the 2019 social assessment of Karachi’s planned Yellow Line Bus Rapid Transit (BRT), getting to her job destination meant walking 20 minutes to a road crossing, paying for a rickshaw and then paying again for a bus. Other women interviewed for the assessment relied on shared three-wheelers that waited to fill up with passengers before leaving. For some, the journey could take two hours each way. Monthly transport costs ranged from Rs3,000 to Rs6,000 in households where incomes of Rs20,000 to Rs30,000 were common. Fares can eat into an already modest wage. An unreliable connection can make a worker late. A second fare at each end of the journey can make studying at a college or a job across town too expensive a proposition to take up. The draft for Pakistan’s Auto Policy 2026-31 revolves around car prices, manufacturers and tariffs. Yet, most households in the country own no car and struggle with the daily cost of commuting. Mobility for work, education and healthcare remains restricted due to unavailability of affordable and safe public transportation choices. How can Pakistan’s auto policy serve the people at large and not just the top decile of households in the country? THE FAULT IN OUR DRAFTS In April, when petrol rose by more than Rs137 a litre, governments briefly treated the price of a bus ticket as a cost-of-living issue. Islamabad announced 30 days of free public transport. Punjab’s relief ran for 69 days, with authorities estimating that hundreds of thousands of passengers a day used the free services. The relief ended, but the daily cost of getting to work remained. Pakistan now has a chance to address some of that cost through its next auto policy. The draft Auto Policy 2026-31 has been okayed by Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif but is still awaiting final approval as the Ministry of Industries and Production is set to forward the draft policy to the Ministry of Finance, which aims to share it with the International Monetary Fund (IMF) for review. This policy would reportedly reduce tariffs, open a formal route for commercial used-car imports and expand the role of electric vehicles. These changes could give car buyers more choice. The draft also shapes how buses and two-wheelers are taxed, which vehicles get incentives and where the proceeds of a proposed automobile levy go. The problem, in my view, is that the debate remains organised around the vehicle and its producer. The household experiences it as a sequence of fares, waits and transfers. An auto policy cannot run provincial bus services, but it can reduce fleet costs and make public support conditional on affordable service. That connects industrial policy to the ability to earn a living. Those decisions should be judged by what they do for the journeys people make every day. For most Pakistani households, the price of a bus ride is a more immediate concern than the price of a new car. WHO’S BUYING THE ALTO? Pakistan’s assemblers sold 155,631 passenger cars in 2025-26. Including separately reported jeeps and pick-ups takes four-wheeler sales to about 206,000. Motorcycle and rickshaw sales totalled roughly 1.97 million. My analysis of the 2024-25 Household Integrated Economic Survey (HIES) shows how differently Pakistanis get around. About 6.6 percent of households own a car and 57 percent own a motorcycle. Roughly 41 percent own neither. Among the poorest fifth of urban households, nearly two-thirds have neither vehicle and only about one in a thousand own a car. Among the richest urban fifth, more than one in five own a car. A Suzuki Alto VXR lists at about Rs2.99 million. The survey puts average household income at Rs82,179 a month, so the Alto costs more than three years of total income, before a household pays for food, rent or anything else. One reported estimate puts the likely reduction in small-car prices under the Auto Policy draft at around nine percent over five years. Applied to the Alto, that would still leave it costing about 33 months of average household income. There is a case for bringing those prices down faster. In 2006-07, local assemblers sold 180,834 cars, whereas passenger car sales reached just 155,631 in 2025-26. The figures are imperfectly comparable because classifications have changed and SUVs have become a bigger part of the market. Still, Indus Motors estimates that the industry can assemble about 600,000 vehicles a year and uses roughly 30 percent of that capacity. New manufacturers have entered the market, but factories still have far more capacity than the market can absorb. The entry of more assemblers does not by itself resolve this. If each operates a small plant behind similar tariff protection, the market can acquire more brands without acquiring the scale or price competition consumers need. Lower duties on finished cars would strengthen the pressure to compete, but duties on imported components also matter. A sensible transition should expose producers to competition while allowing them to obtain inputs at competitive prices. For some smaller imported cars, one reported tariff schedule keeps customs duty at 50 percent for the first two years and then brings it down in stages to 15 percent by 2030-31. Bringing those cuts forward would put earlier pressure on local producers to compete on price and quality. If the government also wants to discourage fuel-hungry vehicles, it can tax size or fuel use directly. A cut in customs duty does not translate directly into an equal cut in the showroom price. Exchange rates, other taxes and producer margins impact what the buyer pays. I would judge success by actual prices and product quality, rather than the number of assembly licences issued. A tariff timetable that can repeatedly be deferred offers consumers little assurance. The distribution of electric vehicle incentives deserves the same scrutiny. Some reported proposals for electric cars include a favourable sales tax rate and a financing ceiling of Rs10 million. If a 17 percentage-point tax preference (the difference between tax rates on electric vehicles as compared to on an internal combustion engine vehicle) is applied to an Rs8 million pre-tax car, the buyer’s tax advantage would be Rs1.36 million. That is roughly the value of 21 electric-motorcycle subsidies of Rs65,000 each under the Pakistan Accelerated Vehicle Electrification (PAVE) programme. The actual benefit would depend on how the tax is applied and what the government ultimately approves. The comparison shows how much support can go to a single purchase. Equivalent fiscal support does not mean equal benefits from different vehicles. A higher loan ceiling does little for someone without a down payment or an income that a lender will accept. Eligibility matters as much as the advertised concession. Electric cars can cut petrol use, especially for people who drive long distances. But a household that cannot finance any private vehicle needs a different kind of help. A bus fare can determine whether someone in that household works at all. Car ownership remains heavily concentrated among wealthier households, while most urban Pakistanis in the lower income brackets have no personal vehicle | Author’s analysis of HIES 2024-25 data THE HIDDEN COST OF BEING UNABLE TO TRAVEL The poorest fifth of Pakistani households spent 4.8 percent of their budgets on transport, compared with 6.7 percent for the richest fifth. In cities, the respective figures are 4.2 and 6.5 percent. If spending were the measure of need, the figures would suggest transport was a bigger problem for the rich. But consider: a household survey counts the fare someone paid. It has no fare to record when a worker walks to save money, a woman turns down a job she cannot reach safely or a student stays home because college is too far away. The poorest fifth also spends almost half its budget on food, compared with about 29 percent for the richest fifth. Once food and the estimated rental value of housing are set aside, transport takes up a more similar share of what remains. The estimated rent includes the value of living in an owner-occupied home, even though no rent changes hands. Removing it from the comparison does not create cash for a bus ticket — it changes the denominator. I read the low transport share as a reason to investigate journeys people cannot make. The expenditure survey alone cannot tell us how many jobs were declined or classes missed. My analysis puts transport spending for the poorest fifth at about Rs254 per person a month. At Rs20 a ride, that pays for six complete return trips. A World Bank benchmark used in urban transport affordability studies allows for roughly 60 one-way trips a month. Even if someone in the poorest fifth put their entire transport budget towards a bus, they would still be well short of a month’s travel to work or school. Among poor urban households with at least one earner and neither a car nor a motorcycle, the survey implies about Rs9 in reported in-city fares per earner per working day. People may forget small fares when asked to recall spending over a year. The figures also cannot separate journeys made on foot from those abandoned altogether. Project documents for Karachi’s Red Line BRT estimate that about 40 percent of trips in the city are made on foot or by bicycle. Some people choose to walk or cycle. For others, it may be the only way to avoid paying for several rides on a single journey. The cost is easier to see against a wage. My analysis of the survey puts median monthly earnings for an urban paid worker at about Rs32,000. At Rs80 each way over 26 working days, the commute costs Rs4,160, or 13 percent of pay. A combination of wagons and rickshaws costing Rs200 a day takes Rs5,200, or more than 16 percent of pay. At the household level, two earners, each paying Rs20 in both directions over 26 working days, would spend Rs2,080 a month. Against the roughly Rs40,146 monthly budget for the poorest urban households in my analysis, that is just over five percent. At Rs80, the same journeys cost Rs8,320, or about 21 percent. These comparisons use survey-period budgets and illustrative fares, not today’s measured burden. They show why a fare that looks modest per trip can be unaffordable when paid repeatedly. For women in paid urban work, reported median monthly earnings were about Rs10,000, partly reflecting fewer hours of paid work. A 26-day commute at Rs80 each way would take about 42 percent of that amount. This is an illustration, since a woman earning Rs10,000 may work fewer days. It shows what fares can do to the gains from taking on more work. Distance can also put education out of reach. In the survey, about 1.3 million girls and young women and 900,000 boys and young men aged five to 24 had never attended school and cited distance as the reason. Where there is a school to go to, a safe, affordable journey can remove one of the barriers to getting there. Motorcycle ownership only partly solves this problem. A bus reaches people a motorcycle subsidy cannot readily serve, including students, older passengers and households that cannot buy a vehicle. That makes a case for supporting both. The journey being replaced matters. A bus carrying former wagon passengers has a different effect on imports from one attracting motorists. An electric motorcycle replacing a petrol motorcycle is different from an additional vehicle bought for convenience. My calculations assume those substitutions — they are not a forecast that every subsidised purchase will deliver the same saving. High utilisation helps recover the higher purchase price only if charging arrangements allow buses to run the promised service. Electric buses eliminate exhaust at the roadside, but overall emissions also depend on electricity generation. A national pollution claim needs a separate estimate. At 11 percent, the contribution falls by about Rs5.5 million, to roughly Rs16 million. At that lower cost, the entire annual levy could support close to 2,900 buses. Using only a share would pay for proportionately fewer. The levy still needs approval and the figures depend on what buses cost, how much operators collect in fares and how many people ride. The assumptions also deserve scrutiny before anyone commits public money. Eight hundred boardings are not 800 different people: a commuter travelling both ways boards twice. At Rs25 a ride and 312 operating days, 800 daily boardings produce Rs6.24 million in annual fare revenue. If boardings halve, that revenue falls by Rs3.12 million, while much of the cost of owning the fleet remains. The model also assumes non-fare revenue at 37 percent of fares, which requires testing against actual advertising receipts. The 11 percent financing rate is an assumption rather than a financing commitment. Any interest subsidy must be funded and a guarantee can leave the state liable if repayments fail. Supporting 15,000 buses on those terms would cost around Rs240 billion a year. That is a recurring public expense. Pakistani households already spend an estimated Rs2.37 trillion a year on transport, according to the household survey. The question for the government is how much of that spending on a better service could save households and which trips people could make if the fare came down or the bus turned up regularly. Household savings cannot simply be netted against the government’s subsidy bill. A lower fare partly shifts costs from passengers to taxpayers — it does not eliminate them. Nor is all of the Rs2.37 trillion available to finance urban buses. The economic case must rest on savings in fuel and time, safer journeys and better access to work, alongside a credible plan for meeting the recurring budget commitment. It can give affordable electric motorcycles and rickshaws greater priority in vehicle incentives. And it can make bus funding conditional on the service that provincial authorities and operators deliver. Localisation targets also need care. A rule designed around a petrol engine can penalise an electric motorcycle because its battery is imported. I would reward the value actually created in Pakistan, with a transition that allows battery and component suppliers to develop. The aim should be competitive suppliers that can sell beyond one protected domestic market. For bus funding, that means setting routes, fares and safety standards, then paying operators for running the scheduled service and maintaining the fleet. Provinces would have to pay on time and operators would have to run the promised service. Passengers should be able to see how often buses ran, how long they waited, and how many services were cancelled. Cities should also track who uses the system and ask people which journeys they still cannot make. A contract can make those obligations enforceable. The province could retain fare revenue and pay an operator for service delivered, with deductions for missed trips and safety failures. That reduces the incentive to overcrowd buses but leaves the province carrying the risk of weak ridership. Payment reserves and dependable budget releases would be necessary — private finance does not remove that obligation. Depots and power connections must be ready when the fleet arrives. I would begin with routes where demand can be tested, publish the results and expand where the service earns continued support. I would also want a travel survey to test the arguments made here. If low reported spending chiefly reflects short, affordable journeys and incomplete recall, the case for suppressed travel (when individuals do not undertake trips or activities due to barriers or constraints) would weaken. If well-run buses attract few riders, routes and spending should be reconsidered. A policy should allow those findings to change where the next rupee goes. Pakistan can keep a tally of cars coming off assembly lines. Over the next five years, it should also count how many more people can get to a job, a classroom or a clinic without buying a vehicle. For a woman making that journey, it comes down to what happens after the 20-minute walk: how long she waits, whether there’s room to get on and how much of her pay she has left when she gets home. The correct position of the law now is this: allegations of workplace harassment prior to 2022 (which is when the Protection Against Harassment of Women in the Workplace Act was amended) must involve conduct of an overtly sexual nature to fall within the scope of the law. Actions that take place after the 2022 amendment are covered by the law not only if they are overtly sexual in nature, but also if they are rooted in gender-based discrimination. Let’s see why. First, let’s look at the definition of ‘harassment’. Prior to January 2022, it was defined to cover “any unwelcome sexual advance, request for sexual favours or other verbal or written communication or physical conduct of a sexual nature or sexually demeaning attitudes, causing interference with work performance or creating an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment, or the attempt to punish the complainant for refusal to comply to such a request or is made a condition for employment”. This definition was amended in January 2022 by adding the following: “discrimination on basis of gender, which may or may not be sexual in nature, but which may embody a discriminatory and prejudicial mindset or notion, resulting in discriminatory behaviour on basis of gender against the complainant” (Section 2[h][ii] of the Act). The court interpreted the original definition restrictively, following the reasoning of the first Nadia Naz judgement. The amendment was made after the 2021 Supreme Court decision in Nadia Naz vs President of Pakistan. This case, which analysed and applied the original, pre-2022 definition of harassment, concluded that the law only covers harassment of an overtly sexual nature. The 2022 amendment, passed a few months after the judgement, rectified the limitation in the original definition as interpreted by the Supreme Court in the Nadia Naz judgement of 2021. The amended definition explicitly includes gender-based discrimination within the meaning of harassment. The amended definition would not apply retrospectively. The expanded definition, which broadens the scope of the actionable wrong, would only cover actions that take place after the amendment. But in 2023 the Supreme Court, reviewing the Nadia Naz judgement, held that the original definition was broad enough to include gender-based discrimination. Harassment as originally defined should be interpreted to include conduct that is not overtly sexual in nature but amounts to gender-based discrimination. The Supreme Court declared that the bench’s interpretation of the original definition was too restrictive. The interpretation in the Nadia Naz judgement on review would be brilliant were it not for the 2022 amendment. By interpreting the original definition to have essentially the same meaning and effect as the 2022 insertion, the Supreme Court implies that the 2022 amendment was redundant. This contradicts a basic principle of statutory interpretation, which requires that each statutory provision should be interpreted to give it a distinct meaning. It may well be that the 2022 amendment was not needed because the original definition should be interpreted as broadly and purposively as the Supreme Court did in the Nadia Naz judgement on review. But when the legislature amended the definition in 2022 to explicitly cover gender-based discrimination, it also implied that the earlier definition was restrictive, which is why the amendment was needed. By explicitly expanding the de­­finition, it endorsed the view that the original definition did not cover gender-based discrimination. The FCC in its recent judgement did the same. The FCC interpreted the original definition restrictively, following the reasoning of the first Nadia Naz judgement. It did not refer to the 2022 amendment, and it did not need to because the incidents involved in the case predated the amendment. The amended definition would not apply to these facts. Let’s say, as many have argued, that the FCC should have interpreted the original definition more broadly to include gender-based discrimination. What implication would that have for the current definition of harassment? Such an interpretation would entail that the second part of the definition is redundant because the first part covers conduct embodying gender-based discrimination. Such reasoning would again contradict the principle of statutory interpretation, which says that the legislature does not waste its words. The FCC decision is deeply flawed, but for another reason. The FCC has not applied its own interpretation properly. The harassment at issue in this case was overtly sexual in nature. It is disturbing that the FCC did not consider this conduct to fall within the scope of the original definition. Flawed as it is, the FCC has not overruled the 2022 amendment to the law. The expanded definition (Section 2(h)(ii)) still stands, but it will only apply to incidents that took place or are alleged to have taken place after the date of the amendment. Prior incidents will be covered by the original definition, which will be interpreted restrictively. This clarification is important because after the FCC ruling and the outrage against it, many potential claimants believe that the pervasive gender-based discrimination they currently face at the workplace is not covered by the law. People sitting on workplace harassment committees have told me that their mandate is now restricted to only incidents that are overtly sexual in nature. We must remind them that it is not. Smaller pie, firmer grip

WHEN railways and factories began transforming 19th-century Europe, the rulers of the Habsburg Empire understood their economic potential. Yet they resisted industrialisation. Friedrich von Gentz, Metternich’s adviser, explained why: “We do not desire at all that the great masses shall become well-off and independent.” His reasoning ended with a revealing question: “How could we otherwise rule over them?” The Habsburg rulers were not necessarily ignorant of the economic benefits of technological progress. They feared its political consequences. A more prosperous society might also become more independent, weakening their grip on power. This historical episode helps explain a persistent puzzle in Pakistan: why do those in power repeatedly choose institutions (rules of the game) and policies that constrain economic progress, even when they too benefit from better alternatives? The answer may lie in the distinction between economic and political returns. Elites do not necessarily choose arrangements that maximise national income, or even their own immediate economic gains. They also consider how a change might affect their ability to retain power and capture future returns — what we call the political replacement effect in economics. Technological and institutional changes can expand economic opportunities while simultaneously creating new centres of political power. Incumbents may, therefore, resist changes that threaten their hold on power. The calculation is straightforward. A reform may make the economy larger and even increase the income of existing elites. But if it also raises the probability of their political displacement, or reduces the share of future gains they can command, their expected returns may decline. Under these conditions, elites may rationally prefer an inferior arrangement that preserves their political dominance. In other words, a smaller pie under a secure scenario may be more attractive than a larger pie with uncertain future distribution. An economically promising change can redistribute political power. Pakistan’s recent history offers three institutional and technological choices that are especially revealing. First is local government. The Constitution requires provinces to devolve political, administrative and financial authority to elected local representatives. Yet successive governments have either delayed local elections or weakened the institutions they create. The largest province, Punjab, last held LG elections a decade ago. Other provinces have also adopted various approaches to limit devolution. KP, for example, recently increased the population threshold for village and neighbourhood councils to reduce the number of councils, potentially weakening representation. In Sindh, transfers to LGs declined from 15.7 per cent of provincial general revenue receipts to just 5.8pc in the 2025-26 budget. What makes these choices economically questionable? Elected LGs with genuine fiscal and administrative authority can better respond to local needs, improve service delivery, and strengthen accountability. Why, then, resist them? Because empowered LGs also create political competitors. A mayor controlling development funds and public services can build an independent political constituency. Legislators lose influence over resources that help sustain their electoral networks. Even if improved local governance generates greater prosperity, its political dividends may accrue to someone else. The second example is fiscal federalism. The seventh NFC award raised provinces’ share of the divisible pool to 57.5pc, with constitutional protection against subsequent reductions. But since then, the federal government has built a parallel fiscal system undermining the very principle of federalism. Petroleum levy collections, which are not shared with provinces, reached Rs1.567 trillion in 2025-26. Provinces were also asked to generate surpluses and, lately, to provide grants to the federal government. The parallel system may be responding to genuine fiscal pressures, but it preserves fiscal discretion at the centre, despite the constitutional commitment to provincial autonomy. In essence, after the seventh NFC, the provincial share fell without anyone touching the award. The political economy consideration here is that meaningful fiscal autonomy changes the distribution of power. Provinces with independent revenue sources and predictable resources can pursue their own priorities and strengthen their political standing. But the provinces are no different. While demanding autonomy from Islamabad, they resist transferring meaningful authority and resources to elected LGs. At each tier, those holding resources resist passing them down. The third example concerns technology: rooftop solar. Faced with high electricity tariffs, the public began investing in their own power generation. Solar offered consumers cheaper electricity, greater reliability, and reduced dependence on the national grid. Yet policy increasingly moved towards discouraging its adoption. The government not only imposed a 10pc sales tax on solar panels but, in February, it replaced net metering with net billing for new connections, substantially lowering the compensation for surplus electricity supplied to the grid. A more economically sustainable response would have restructured costly contractual obligations, modernised the grid, and established fair charges for network use. Why has such adjustment proved so difficult? One explanation is that decentralised electricity generation threatens more than power producers’ revenues. It also weakens an established system through which economic resources, contracts, subsidies, and regulatory discretion are concentrated. These instruments carry financial and political value. Reforming them could improve overall economic performance while reducing the influence of those who currently control them. These three cases are not identical, but they share a key feature: an economically promising change can redistribute political power, creating incentives for incumbents to resist or dilute it. This is what makes the political replacement argument more powerful than the familiar explanation that elites protect their economic rents. Sometimes they may sacrifice economic gains, including their own, to preserve the political position from which future gains can be secured. The implication is that Pakistan’s development problem cannot be resolved through better policy advice alone. The harder challenge is creating institutions that reduce the ability of those in power to obstruct changes that might eventually displace them. The stakes extend beyond the current examples. AI is creating new opportunities while challenging existing economic and political hierarchies. Whether or not Pakistan embraces those opportunities will depend largely on how it responds to a technology most capable of redistributing power. Until the existing calculations change, the country may continue choosing a smaller pie for the sake of a firmer grip.
