As an example, Tarar highlighted the closure of Pakistan Public Works Department (PWD), which he said faced allegations of corruption and “wastage” of money. “It was closed and a package finalised for its employees. Several factors are responsible for a situation in which disputing parties often find themselves waiting for decades before their cases are settled. A shortage of judges, unclear rules, frequent court adjournments, and corruption at all tiers are just a few of the underlying causes. It is no wonder then that informal forums of ‘justice’, based more on centuries-old, often barbaric customs than on modern legal principles, persist in the country. Seen in this light, a panchayat system that prioritises community decision-making but with some state regulation, appears, at least theoretically, to be the right move. However, as with any initiative, it is important to closely study similar local justice schemes elsewhere before embarking on the proposed project. In fact, Pakistan is no stranger to alternative dispute resolution laws intended to reduce the burden on the courts. The Punjab Alternative Dispute Resolution Act, 2019 — recently repealed by the Punjab Alternative Dispute Resolution Ordinance, 2026 — is a case in point. The ‘success’ of the repealed law can justifiably be questioned. How will the proposed panchayat framework ensure better results? It is too early to be ambitious.
