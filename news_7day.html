Two-Nation Theory and genesis of Pakistan

IN South Asia, the rise of Muslim political consciousness and the creation of Pakistan reflected a prolonged constitutional and political struggle led by Quaid-i-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah. The demand for Pakistan was rooted in the belief that the Muslims of British India constituted a distinct political community whose cultural, religious, and political interests required constitutional protection and effective political representation. Pakistan’s national identity has consequently remained closely associated with the ideas that formed the raison d’être for the Pakistan Movement. Its history offers lessons that can be learnt from significant events, most notably the political crisis of 1971, which culminated in the separation of East Pakistan. The post-1971 period also witnessed continuing debates over the nature of the federation, provincial autonomy, and national identity. These debates underline the importance of learning from history rather than using it to promote narrow political narratives. Pakistan’s political experience demonstrates that national cohesion depends not only on shared historical ideals but also on constitutional governance, political inclusion, social justice, and respect for diversity. The enduring challenge, therefore, is to reconcile Pakistan’s historical foundations with the requirements of a modern, democratic, and inclusive state to dispel the spread of unsubstantiated narratives against the Two-Nation Theory as an ideological basis of Pakistan. Pakistan’s leadership must follow the Quaid’s democratic vision and understand the primacy of Jinnah’s thoughts in tackling constitutional, political, economic and security challenges. The dilemma that crystallised after the 1857 War of Independence was the result of wilful distortions and misconceptions about the Muslims of British India. The British believed that Muslims were the real ‘troublemakers’ responsible for the uprising. Their hostile attitude towards Muslims caused exploitation and generated feelings of deprivation among them. They lost social status and suffered cruel punishments. During this dark phase of the community’s life Sir Syed Ahmad Khan dispelled the idea that Muslims were responsible for the war. The Aligarh movement he launched was based on a trinity of ideas: loyalty to the British, distance from politics, and devotion to education. His educational movement started to bring lasting change in the outlook of Muslim community. In the beginning Sir Syed was in favour of Hindu-Muslim unity; he considered India as a bride and Hindus and Muslims as the two eyes of the bride. Nevertheless, in 1867, after the Hindi-Urdu controversy, he realised that both communities were separate. Thus, Muslims became aware of their own distinctive political identity in the subcontinent. They acted on the advice of Sir Syed and cooperated with the British. They did not join the Indian National Congress (INC), which was established in 1885 to provide a common platform for Indians and to introduce the concept of one nation. It was clear that the organisation based on Western type of democracy would not be able to protect Muslim interests due to dominance of Hindus. Crystallisation of Sir Syed’s thoughts formed the Two-Nation Theory. It was not only the religious and social aspect of life where there were divergences between the two communities. There were little or no common customs and traditions shared by both communities. The structure, function and shape of houses and places of worship belonging to both communities were different. The variance was much wider in cuisine. There were no common festivals and celebrations among Hindus and Muslims. The language spoken by Hindus and Muslims was Hindustani, used for the purpose of communication. However, for literary purposes Muslims used Urdu, which absorbed many Arabic, Persian and Turkish words. Hindus used Hindi as a source of literature, which consisted of high percentage of Sanskrit words. A nation between adversaries

The logic behind Pakistan’s mediating role The primary strategic motive behind Pakistan’s mediation diplomacy is the need to keep regional disputes from spilling over onto its own borders. Unlike great powers, which may approach mediation largely for geopolitical or ideological reasons, Pakistan’s security is immediately influenced by conflicts in its near neighbourhood. These efforts were primarily motivated by Islamabad’s desire to construct a relatively stable Afghanistan capable of denying space to transnational terrorist organisations and minimising fears of long-term instability along the Pakistan-Afghan border. Thus, mediation is a strategic investment in Pakistan’s domestic security. Theoretically, for mediation efforts to be successful, all parties must believe that the mediator is impartial and trustworthy. However, Pakistan’s deep strategic ties with Saudi Arabia and other Arab Gulf states often cause concern among opposing parties. For example, Pakistan’s longstanding defence and security partnership with Saudi Arabia has historically hampered its ability to be perceived as completely neutral by Iran, despite Islamabad’s consistent efforts to maintain cordial relations with both nations. Similarly, Pakistan’s extensive engagement with the Afghan Taliban has occasionally prompted segments of the former Afghan political elite and some Western policymakers to question Islamabad’s neutrality, even though Pakistan actively encouraged negotiations and political settlements in Afghanistan prior to the fall of Kabul in 2021. These perceptions, whether accurate or not, frequently undermine the warring parties’ willingness to fully embrace Pakistani mediation. The geopolitical climate further limits Pakistan’s effectiveness as a mediator. Many recent conflicts are increasingly impacted by great power competition, and leave little diplomatic space for middle powers to independently secure comprehensive settlements. The Afghan peace process, for example, brought together the strategic interests of the US, China, Russia, Iran, India, Qatar, and numerous other Gulf states. Similarly, the ongoing conflict between Iran and the US is driven by larger issues such as nuclear proliferation, sanctions, Israel’s security, insecurity of GCC states and regional proxy conflict. In such cases, Pakistan can encourage dialogue and propose confidence-building measures, but it lacks the ability to address the underlying strategic calculations that drive the conflict. Another rationale for Pakistan’s relatively low success rate is the nature of the mediation role Islamabad normally adopts. Unlike well-established and well-known mediators such as Qatar, the UAE, and Norway, who aim to reach binding agreements, Pakistan has frequently served as a facilitator, host, bridge-builder, or provider of good offices. These roles are less apparent yet nevertheless diplomatically significant. Facilitators generate opportunities for dialogue, provide conducive environment for dialogue, establish communication channels and decrease misperceptions. Although these measures may not result in landmark peace treaties, they frequently keep preventing crises from escalating into broader armed conflict. Hence, measuring Pakistan’s mediation record exclusively based on the number of signed agreements understates its overall contribution to regional stability. Finally, Pakistan’s mediation activities have occasionally suffered from a lack of follow-up by the opposing parties themselves. Several times negotiations brokered by Pakistan began with hope but later disintegrated due to one or more sides withdrawing due to their domestic political factors or altered external or regional developments. Pakistan’s mediatory experience in intra-Afghan dialogue exemplifies this challenge. Although Pakistan played a crucial role in supporting the Doha talks, the subsequent political trajectory in Afghanistan, combined with deteriorating Pakistan-Afghanistan relations after 2021, proved that successful mediation does not guarantee long-term peace. Similarly, Pakistan’s efforts to de-escalate tensions between Saudi Arabia and Iran in 2016 and 2019 were overshadowed by broader regional forces before China helped broker the 2023 diplomatic reconciliation. Nevertheless, Pakistan’s moderate success rate as a mediator should not be regarded as diplomatic failure. International mediation is inherently risky, and even the most experienced mediators have struggled to reach long-term settlements in prolonged conflicts. Pakistan’s comparative advantage is less in imposing solutions than preserving dialogue when contact between adversaries has broken down. Despite Islamabad’s structural limitations, its geographic proximity, long-standing regional relationships, understanding of local political dynamics of Iran and Afghanistan, and ability to maintain engagement with mutually hostile actors such as Saudi Arabia and Iran are valuable diplomatic assets.
