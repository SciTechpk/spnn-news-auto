MPC noted the recent escalation in the Middle East conflict, which it said led to an increase in global commodity prices and persistent supply chain disruptions. “However, recent domestic macroeconomic data turned out broadly in line with the MPC’s expectations,” the statement said, adding that headline inflation increased by 11.1pc year on year in August from the previous 9.2pc in July and “core inflation was slightly lower than expectations”. “External account pressures remained contained, supported by robust workers’ remittances and higher financial inflows.” MPC noted that economic activity picked up “as reflected by recent high-frequency indicators”. MPC said that the current monetary policy rate was “appropriate to guide inflation towards target range of 5-7pc over the medium term”. “However, uncertainty regarding the outlook has increased, particularly from the worsening geopolitical environment.” Listing key developments since its last meeting, MPC noted that Pakistan’s sovereign credit rating was upgraded to B3 by Moody’s with a stable outlook. It also noted that the country had tapped international capital markets to “raise $3bn through Eurobonds,” leading to an increase in foreign reserves to over $21bn “Third, inflation expectations of both businesses and consumers increased in September, while their confidence weakened,” the statement read. It also observed an output decline of 3.5pc in June in large-scale manufacturing, which it said brought the “cumulative FY26 growth to 5pc”. “Fifth, fiscal consolidation turned out higher than the budgetary target during FY26,” it said, further noting that Federal Board of Revenue (FBR) collection had remained “on-target” during July to August in FY27. It continued: “While SBP transferred higher profit of Rs1.9 trillion than the budgeted amount of Rs1.4tr to the government. “Lastly, central banks have become more cautious amidst challenging global economic conditions.” Within the context of the ongoing developments, the MPC said that it was committed to “achieving price stability with close monitoring of incoming data and the ongoing situation in the Middle East”. It also observed that adverse geopolitical events as well as weather disruptions had increased in frequency, posing risks to the macroeconomic outlook. In this regard, the MPC stressed the need for maintaining a “prudent monetary and fiscal policy mix and further buildup of buffers to absorb supply shocks”. The petition argued that a person subject to constitutional disqualification was legally incapacitated from exercising control over state functionaries or issuing binding political directives. The petition requested the FCC to quash and set aside all consequential actions, including the Oct 15, 2025 notification regarding the installation and continuation of Afridi as the chief minister. It further requested the court to restore Gandapur as the CM, declaring his resignation invalid and his de-notification legally ineffective. The petition also sought to restrain all respondents from acting on any political or constitutional directives issued by any disqualified or de-recognised political entity regarding KP’s governance. Is it possible to slow down the development of AI?

He received public support from his counterpart at OpenAI, Sam Altman, as well as from Elon Musk and Google DeepMind chief Demis Hassabis. Why now? In recent months, OpenAI and Anthropic have reported several incidents in which AI systems spontaneously broke out of their confined environments to access the internet and attack websites and platforms. These AI systems demonstrated the ability to coordinate, establish hierarchies among themselves, cheat and erase traces of their actions. Meanwhile, on Tuesday, Jacob Coxon — who resigned from Anthropic and previously worked at OpenAI — publicly accused both startups of lax security standards and “gambling with our lives.” Will they slow down on their own? Competition among the industry’s leading players is so fierce, with hundreds of billions of dollars in investment at stake, that none of them is willing to risk easing off the accelerator alone. Amodei has expressed a willingness to coordinate with rivals, excluding China. When asked by AFP about potential discussions to this end, Anthropic, OpenAI and Google did not immediately respond. For now, the only concrete measure adopted by both Anthropic and OpenAI is the inclusion of independent observers who will internally verify the companies’ work regarding AI safety. Is this a PR stunt? While many in the industry welcomed Amodei’s message, others questioned his motives. For Brian Roemmele, an entrepreneur and figure in the AI sector, it amounts to a sales pitch delivered just weeks before Anthropic is likely to take shares public. “That is the IPO story in essay form: we are the responsible firm; we are the cure; buy the safety premium,” he posted on X, referring to the widely expected initial public offering. On Sunday, Trump dismissed the warnings from AI industry leaders as “negative forces.” Several Republicans have expressed reluctance to actively regulate the technology, fearing it could stifle innovation and risk letting China win the AI race, according to House Speaker Mike Johnson. On Sunday, Amodei reiterated the need for government oversight of AI. Like Altman and Hassabis, he believes that rules set by the industry itself would be insufficient. Can the brakes be applied without China? Anthropic’s Amodei suggests limiting coordination to democratic countries, excluding China. However, the AI race and China’s advances in the field create “the toughest dilemma” regarding whether or not to slow down AI development, he noted on CBS on Sunday. The United States plans to discuss AI safety on the sidelines of Chinese leader Xi Jinping’s late September visit to Washington. But China is wary of any US attempt to impose its own regulatory framework, according to several media outlets. This divergence is particularly pronounced given that China encourages the emergence of so-called “open” AI systems that are freely accessible and modifiable — in contrast to the “closed” models favoured by major US companies. Open AI models are, by definition, harder to control, as users can alter their parameters — potentially for malicious purposes. As American firepower ousted the Taliban’s first regime and shattered the Al Qaeda network in Afghanistan, the war spilled across the border. It didn’t stop in Pakistan’s rugged tribal belt; it flowed south into the sprawling, chaotic metropolis of Karachi — quietly at first and then with devastating force. The city was becoming something its law enforcement agencies had never been trained to confront: a staging ground for “a transnational jihadist war”, says Khattab, a highly decorated officer who recently retired after spending the bulk of his 35-year police career on the CTD frontlines. While Pakistan’s tribal areas offered sanctuary, Karachi offered reach. They increasingly established themselves in its urban periphery, particularly in Pakhtun-majority neighbourhoods. Militants established control over parts of the city’s peripheral localities, running extortion networks, imposing their own rudimentary forms of justice and targeting polio vaccination teams, educationists and political activists, particularly those associated with the Awami National Party (ANP). Factions were organised largely along regional lines, reflecting where militants had originated, including Swat, South Waziristan and Mohmand. These semi-autonomous groups controlled their own areas and revenue streams. During this period, the TTP’s central chief, Mufti Noor Wali, also served as the group’s Karachi chief. Local militants brought something equally valuable: knowledge of Karachi. “Born, raised or deeply embedded in Karachi, these local operatives understood the city’s complex urban geography,” Khattab says. As foreign Al Qaeda operatives moved towards the Afghan border, or were killed or captured through raids, the organisation’s influence did not simply disappear. A key faction was Harkat-ul-Mujahideen al-Alami, which had split from the mainly Kashmir-focused Harkat-ul-Mujahideen (HuM) during the early 2000s. HuM al-Alami had orchestrated several high-profile attacks in Karachi, including the 2002 assault on French engineers and the 2004 bomb blast near a concert featuring Indian singer Sonu Nigam. While AQIS signalled its ambitions through high-profile actions, such as the attempted 2014 hijacking of a Pakistani Navy frigate, its true impact was structural rather than tactical. Unlike foreign fighters, local operatives possessed deep knowledge of the urban terrain, allowing them to blend seamlessly into the city. Investigations into individual attacks routinely exposed complex, interconnected networks rather than isolated cells. “An investigation into a single bombing would lead to a completely different organisation or cell,” Khattab explains. Instead of operating in isolation, militant factions built fluid alliances, borrowing personnel, tactics and specialised expertise. The declaration of the Islamic State (IS) caliphate in Iraq and Syria introduced a competing model of jihadism. Frustrated by Al Qaeda’s perceived caution, some disgruntled TTP leaders, including Daud Mehsud (a TTP militant and a former Karachi policeman), shifted their allegiance to IS. He explains, “Even though Al Qaeda was also Salafi-leaning, it chose not to emphasize its Salafi identity when dealing with local Deobandi groups in the Pakistan-Afghanistan region.” But the significance of this period lay less in which organisation gained the upper hand than in how militancy itself was changing. The old, hierarchical structures were giving way to looser networks, in which people, money, skills and ideology could move across organisational lines.
