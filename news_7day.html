India’s water blackmail

SINCE April 2025, when India unilaterally suspended the Indus Waters Treaty (IWT), most scholarship has treated it as a political decision by the BJP-led NDA government. While it is true that the BJP has been developing a narrative that Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi was ‘rectifying’ the ‘mistakes’ of the Nehruvian era, the decisions and developments during the past 18 months suggest that New Delhi is developing a long-term strategy to strangle Pakistan economically. India’s primary target in this strategic campaign is the Chenab river, which it is systematically converting into a long-term instrument of leverage to dictate Pakistan’s foreign policy, weaken its stance on Kashmir, and dominate bilateral relations. In the 18 months since it decided to keep the IWT in abeyance, India has aggressively leveraged this treaty vacuum to build a massive network of storage, regulation, and diversion infrastructure on the Chenab. New and accelerated hydroelectric power projects, primarily concentrated in the Chenab basin, have ballooned from a baseline capacity of 3,263 MW to 7,620.5 MW, supported by a massive financial outlay of approximately Indian rupees 414.04 billion. No single dam or diversion canal can permanently dry up a river. However, a highly synchronised, connected network of mega structures, coupled with diversion canals, can fundamentally determine who controls the timing and volume of river flow. India is trying to apply a two-pronged strategy, ie, increase storage capacity and enhance the ability to divert the Chenab’s water. India’s strategic roadmap involves five megaprojects alongside extensive local diversions. This includes Sawalkot (1,856 MW), Kirthai I & II (1,320 MW), Pakal Dul (1,000 MW), Ratle (850 MW), and Bursar Storage (800 MW). India’s infrastructure push began in May 2025, with the announcement of the tender for the Uri-II project (240 MW), followed quickly in June 2025 by the tender announcement for the Dulhasti-II project (260 MW). Activity accelerated in July 2025 with two major announcements — first, a tender for the removal of deposited sediments from the Salal Dam Reservoir, and second, the Sawalkot project (1,856 MW) tender under international competitive bidding. In March 2026, civil contracts were officially awarded for both the Uri-I stage II and Dulhasti stage-II projects, alongside the official tender announcement for the highly controversial Chenab-Beas Link Tunnel Project (Link-3). These aforementioned projects command a combined potential diversion and storage capacity of roughly 7.287 MAF. For years, New Delhi has strategically used international platforms and institutional levers to weaken Pakistan’s economic foundations. The Chenab-Beas Link Project is particularly consequential. With a reported budget of Indian rupees 23.52bn, it proposes to transfer water from the Chandra river, a major Chenab tributary, through an 8.7-kilometre tunnel into the Beas basin and onward through a 113km canal network to eastern Indian states. The proposed diversion of 1.9 MAF annually from the Chenab basin raises serious questions about compliance with Article III of the IWT. The threat of manipulation, or the Chenab’s vulnerabilities, poses a direct risk to Pakistan’s economic survival, social stability, and national security. The Chenab directly irrigates agricultural lands across more than 11 districts of central Punjab, the country’s undisputed food basket. These districts form the densest core of the famous rice-wheat cropping zone. This 11-district area accounts for about 22 to 25 per cent of Pakistan’s national food grain output. If India controls water flows during October to December or May to July, it may affect seasonal yields in this zone. In such a scenario, it could cause a direct blow of $9bn to $11bn to Pakistan’s economy. The broader impact would ripple through the national economy in several ways. Pakistan would be forced to spend billions of scarce foreign exchange reserves to import basic food commodities. Instead of generating $3.3bn to $4bn from booming rice exports, the country would face a multibillion-dollar trade deficit just trying to secure alternative grain imports. Second, any sustained restriction on, or unpredictable disruption to, these water flows would threaten the livelihoods of millions of farming households, while endangering crops and compromising national food security. Therefore, Islamabad must view India’s decision to place the IWT in abeyance as another dimension of New Delhi’s long-standing and coordinated policy of economic strangulation of Pakistan. For years, New Delhi has strategically used international platforms and institutional levers to weaken Pakistan’s economic foundations. New Delhi has been consistently trying to restrict Islamabad’s access to global capital and limit its trade capacity. A primary example is India’s aggressive lobbying within the Financial Action Task Force, where it has consistently tried to politicise technical compliance and block Pakistan’s removal from the grey list to discourage foreign direct investment and cripple its banking sector. Beyond regulatory warfare, India has systematically disrupted regional trade platforms, such as Saarc, effectively stalling economic integration across South Asia to keep Pakistan regionally isolated. Faced with this asymmetric threat, Islamabad’s response has been largely concentrated on the external front. Pakistan’s state apparatus has consistently pursued international legal and diplomatic channels. However, a dangerous policy gap exists on the domestic front. Ground-level preparedness is profoundly lacking. The agrarian communities, provincial irrigation departments, and economic sectors that will bear the immediate impact of India’s upstream infrastructure remain highly vulnerable. Pakistan lacks the modern water-storage infrastructure, optimised canal lining, crop-switching strategies, and real-time early warning systems needed to survive sudden changes in river flows. Hence, Islamabad should complement its legal and diplomatic efforts with urgent domestic reforms. Pakistan must declare a national water emergency, educate vulnerable communities in 11 districts of central Punjab that depend on the Chenab, expedite dam construction, and modernise irrigation. Uncertain local governments

THE battle between the Election Commission of Pakistan and the provincial and federal governments has been an almost permanent feature of Pakistan’s electoral scene since 2010, when the responsibility for holding local government elections was entrusted to the Commission under the 18th Amendment. Currently, the ECP is fighting a four-way battle — with the provincial governments of Punjab and KP, the federal government, and the cantonment boards — for LG elections in the two provinces, Islamabad Capital Territory (ICT), and the cantonment boards. The LGs in Sindh and Balochistan (except in the largest district of Quetta, where the entire term passed without election) have yet to complete their terms. The ICT LG election is the most disappointing case. The last LG election in ICT was held in November 2015. Its five-year term ended on Feb 14, 2021. Under the Elections Act, 2017, the new election was to take place within 120 days after the previous LGs’ term ended, making June 15, 2021, the due date for the next election. For more than five years, the ECP and the federal government have been haggling over legal points and the possible date of the next election. Since 2021, the ECP has issued the election schedule four times and carried out fresh delimitation five times. Each time it has had to eat humble pie by withdrawing the schedule as the federal government changed the goalposts and amended the LG election law. The latest in the series was the ICT Local Government (Amendment) Ordinance, 2026, promulgated on Jan 10, 2026, which drastically changed the capital’s LG system. However, the ordinance lapsed in September 2026. While parliament is going through the motions of converting the lapsed ordinance into an act of parliament, the ECP maintains that the old ICTLGA, 2015, stands resurrected after the ordinance lapsed and, therefore, issued a polling schedule on Oct 25, fixing Dec 20 as polling day. Looking back at the federal government’s track record, the new act is likely to pass in the next few days, after which the ECP would have no choice but to withdraw the schedule and begin preparations anew. The case of KP is not very different; the terms of the LGs expired on June 19, 2026, after which new elections should have taken place by Oct 20, but the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Local Government (Amendment) Act, 2026,was passedand became effective on July 16 this year. It changed the size of the village and neighbourhood councils. The ECP strongly objected to the amendment, termed it a delaying tactic, and asked the provincial government to review the law. In the meantime, the ECP is proceeding with preparations to hold the LG polls, except in 12 districts where the law and order situation doesn’t permit polls. The ECP has yet to issue an election schedule. Still, it remains to be seen whether the National Assembly, which has the legislative powers of the KP Assembly after the proclamation of emergency, will amend the LG law or whether the elections will be postponed yet again on some pretext or the other. The ECP is battling both the federal and provincial governments that are reluctant to hold LG polls. All formalities for holding the LG election in Punjab have been completed by the ECP and the Punjab government under the Punjab Local Government Act, 2025. If finally held, this will be the first LG election in roughly 11 years. The election was supposed to be held in May 2022, but a series of new laws and amendments kept postponing it. Although the Punjab government ministerial committee and the ECP agreed to hold the election in three phases between Dec 15 and Jan 15, the Punjab government changed its mind and asked the ECP to defer the election until April 2027 because of dense fog and school exams in December. The ECP immediately rejected the new dates and asked its secretariat to prepare an election schedule based on the earlier agreed dates. The election schedule has not yet been issued; one should wait to see which side blinks first. The election to 43 cantonment boards in the country was due on Aug 8 as the four-year term of the previously elected boards expired on April 11, 2026. The cantonment boards’ election has an additional prerequisite: the ECP should receive the federal government’s request before holding the election. Reportedly, no such request has been received, and the ECP, given the legal requirement to hold elections within 120 days of the expiry of the term, is trying to reach out to the federal government. Regardless of the outcome of these efforts, the election to the cantonment boards is also overdue. These four stories lead to a single conclusion: both the federal and provincial governments are reluctant to hold LG elections on one pretext or another. The key flaw in the system is that while the ECP is responsible for holding LG elections, the governments and their legislatures alone write the LG laws. There is no bar on legislatures amending LG laws. As demonstrated repeatedly, the law can be amended after the announcement of the election schedule and even a few days before polling day, undoing all the preparation made by the ECP such as delimiting LG boundaries. The solution is for parliament to amend the Elections Act, 2017, and bar any amendment to the LG laws after the expiry of the LG term, so the ECP can prepare unimpeded and hold timely elections. As if these difficulties were not enough to make the future of LGs uncertain, another layer of unpredictability has been added by the possibility of carving out new provinces and, in the case of ICT, by the announcement that Islamabad may soon have provincial status. This may give the government yet another pretext to slow-walk LG elections. Islamabad High Court bars FIR mention in character certificates after acquittal

ISLAMABAD: The Islamabad High Court (IHC) has ruled that police cannot mention an FIR in the character certificate of a citizen who has been acquitted in the case, declaring any such certificate unlawful and invalid. Justice Ayyaz Shaukat, while deciding a public interest petition, directed the Inspector General of Police, Islamabad, to establish a formal mechanism to ensure that references to criminal cases ending in acquittal are not included in character certificates issued to citizens. The court observed that citizens should not be compelled to approach courts to exercise rights already guaranteed under the law. It held that following an acquittal, there was no legal justification for portraying an individual as an accused or criminal through a police-issued character certificate. Rules such mention violates constitutional rights, including dignity and freedom of movement Allowing the petition, the court ordered the removal of a reference to an FIR registered at Shalimar Police Station from the petitioner’s character certificate. The judgement clarified that although police could maintain internal records of criminal cases for official purposes, such information could not be disclosed in character certificates issued to citizens, particularly when these were required for travelling abroad. It further observed that there was no legal basis for mentioning an FIR in a character certificate after the proceedings had culminated in a final acquittal. Any certificate containing such a reference would be legally invalid. Justice Ayyaz Shaukat also rejected the police’s description of the petitioner as having been “discharged” during the investigation, observing that such a characterisation undermined the legal effect and status of the acquittal. The court held that including an FIR in a character certificate despite an individual’s acquittal violated Articles 4, 9, 14 and 15 of the Constitution, which protect citizens’ rights, liberty, dignity and freedom of movement. It emphasised that a person acquitted through due process of law could not continue to face adverse consequences through a character certificate suggesting involvement in criminal proceedings. The court directed the Islamabad police chief to introduce a proper procedure to ensure that acquitted citizens did not have to initiate fresh litigation merely to obtain character certificates reflecting their actual legal status. Cost of provinces

THE cost would be steep. We all knew that. An Islamabad-based think tank has now put figures to those costs. That gives a fiscal dimension to a debate that has largely been driven by political demands and promises of better administration. The Policy Research Institute of Market Economy estimates that creating two more provinces in Punjab could add Rs47bn to Rs57bn to annual recurring expenditure. The bill could rise to Rs610bn-Rs730bn if all the country’s 30 divisional units were turned into provinces. Every new province would require a permanent administrative and political structure, including a governor, chief minister, assembly, secretariat and high court. Their salaries, operating expenses and overheads would have to be met year after year, leaving less money for development. The figures expose a major weakness in the argument for creating more provinces: political and administrative restructuring is often presented as an inexpensive way to make government more responsive. It is not. Smaller administrative units create another layer of government whose costs would be hard to justify. If a substantial portion of their allocations goes towards maintaining political and administrative establishments, little would remain for schools, hospitals and other essential services. Citizens promised better governance could end up paying for an expensive new tier without seeing much improvement. There is, of course, a real case for reorganising provincial boundaries. Punjab’s size and population, regional disparities and the sense of political and economic neglect in its southern districts cannot be dismissed. The demand for a separate South Punjab province reflects genuine grievances over unequal development. But these grievances cannot be resolved simply by creating new capitals, assemblies and secretariats. Nor should the fiscal argument be used to brush aside legitimate demands for political representation and administrative autonomy. Can the same objectives be achieved through empowered local governments? Political parties’ record of repeatedly suspending, delaying or weakening elected LGs suggests that the problem is less the size of provinces than the reluctance of political leadership to devolve power and resources. Functional LGs, with predictable funding, elected representatives and clearly defined responsibilities, could take decision-making nearer to citizens without multiplying provincial overheads. Any proposal for provincial restructuring must show how it would improve public services. It must also establish how new units would finance recurring expenditure. The creation of provinces is a long-term fiscal and constitutional commitment. Nuclear science boosts Pakistan’s ability to track groundwater

ISLAMABAD: Pakistan has strengthened its ability to track groundwater resources using nuclear science, enabling scientists to identify recharge sources, determine groundwater age and trace contamination pathways, according to an International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) report released on Saturday. The Regional Cooperative Agree­ment (RCA) for Research, Deve­lopment and Training Related to Nuclear Science and Technology for Asia and the Pacific, Hydrology and Groundwater programme has significantly enhanced Pakistan’s capacity for continuous groundwater monitoring through the application of isotope-based techniques. This advancement has considerably strengthened the connection between scientific research and water governance, allowing for more precise and systematic observation of groundwater dynamics across diverse hydrogeological settings, the IAEA report said. Studies undertaken through RCA-supported projects have generated reliable estimates of groundwater recharge rates, aquifer interconnections and pollution pathways in key regions such as the Indus Plain, the Quetta Valley and the Cholistan Desert. IAEA report says RCA-backed isotope techniques help country trace water sources, age, pollution pathways These findings have deepened the scientific understanding of Pakistan’s groundwater systems while providing essential inputs for developing long-term strategies for water reso­urce management, the agency noted. Prior to joining the programme, groundwater monitoring was not a primary focus of national hydrological investigations. Several studies were conducted using conventional methods, such as hydro-chemical analyses and physical measurements, but these approaches provided only basic information on water table depth, water quality and aquifer dynamics. Through its participation in the programme, Pakistan has significantly strengthened its laboratory capacity for isotope hydrology. The Pakistan Institute of Nuclear Science and Technology (Pinstech) now hosts one of the region’s most advanced isotope analysis facilities, complemented by 12 laboratories nationwide. Currently, Pinstech and the Pakistan Council of Research in Water Resources work in close collaboration to incorporate isotopic data into national groundwater management models. “This integration supports the early detection of over-extraction, salinity intrusion and other forms of aquifer stress, thereby contributing to more sustainable groundwater utilisation,” the report said. The focus on data-driven management has fostered a stronger culture of evidence-based decision-making within national and provincial water institutions. Policymakers are increasingly rel­ying on isotope hydrology data to inf­orm regulatory frameworks, resource allocation and long-term planning, reflecting a significant shift from reactive to preventive and science-informed groundwater management.
