Economic ills

DESPITE an oversold stabilisation, many of our economy’s vital signs continue to falter. The economy resembles a patient out of the ICU but still in hospital. Since 2024, our per capita GDP growth has been near zero — sadly, South Asia’s slowest recorded pace. Even our recent growth spurts, brief and import-led, have given way to longer busts, draining reserves and sending us back to the IMF’s tough terms that extend low growth. Once near the region’s top performers in per capita GDP, we now risk falling to the bottom. As incomes stall, poverty rises. Our extreme poverty rate (23 per cent) is near the bottom regionally. It’s about 10 times India’s, against near parity in 2011. Our GDP’s composition tells a bleak tale too. It’s consumption-heavy (82pc) but investment-light (14pc) — on both counts among the region’s worst. India and China invest over 30pc of GDP, creating jobs and capacity for today and later — much better than our consumption binges. Limiting job, technology, and export growth, industry’s share has been nearly a fifth for over 50 years versus a quarter in India and Sri Lanka and a third in Bangladesh. Low investment causes low growth, but its origins and destinations matter too. We court foreign capital while ignoring local capital. Foreign capital must earn and save more dollars than it repatriates and create jobs and local capacity. But we get it mostly in domestic sectors, which later depletes reserves. Unlike our peers, we chase a few rich states for capital rather than prioritising diversified private capital. We shun creative ways to boost local savings and capital. So, we remain capital-starved — yet we quickly drain reserves and risk the haughty whims of rich states. Capital droughts in export sectors cause dollar and job droughts. From a peak of 17pc in 1992, exports now barely reach a tenth of our GDP — South Asia’s second-lowest share, below even Afghanistan’s. Low-quality compounds low quantity. Harvard’s Economic Complexity Index ranks us 89th among 145 states. So our exports face intense competition but return little. Exports rarely cover imports, creating large trade gaps. Dreary metrics mirror a sinking economy. Remittances partly plug the gap. But exports keep workers at home and families intact, create jobs, earn dollars, and pay taxes. Remittances separate families and often don’t create jobs or pay taxes directly. They frequently fuel consumption, while exports are fuelled by investment. Remittances now almost equal our exports, as it is easier to send workers abroad than goods (India earns six times as much from exports as from remittances). Even so, we often run large external deficits that lead to a crisis as booms turn into busts. Low growth and weak efforts leave our coffers lean. Tax revenues barely reach 11pc of GDP — which is among South Asia’s lowest. Indirect anti-poor taxes provide about half our tax target, down from about two-thirds in 2021-22. New direct taxes further burden the wallets of salaried persons and formal industry, while favoured sectors pay low taxes. Spending prioritises upkeep over uplift. Expenditure hogs over 90 paisas out of every federal budget rupee; development consoles itself with just five. These twin deficits force us to keep our borrowing bowl out. Our public debt-to-GDP ratio of 73pc is lower than the Japanese and Singaporean levels of over 150pc. But our repayment muscle better depicts our debt overhang. On the share of federal outlays spent on interest alone, we rank among the worst globally. We use debt not only to invest or meet recurrent expenses but also to beef up rese­rves. IMF debt and deposits by rich states, which impose odious economic and political conditions on us, now exceed even our highest-ever reserves. These dre­ary metrics mirror a sinking economy. Low investment and import-led growth widen the twin deficits to swell debt. Interest outlays then leave little money for growth, creating a vicious circle. A weak economy reflects perverse politics. Poor countries need both a developmental and a welfare state. Ours is a parasitic and apathetic state run by rapacious rulers. This isn’t a tragedy of ineptitude alone: willing rulers can easily hire merit. It’s a deliberate design by kleptocrats who may lose their wealth and power if there is real progress. Nor is this the tale of a few eras. Our autocracies, too, delivered short-term growth, leading to conflict and undercutting future progress. Ideas abound on how to pursue sound progress, but minds that will champion and implement them well do not. Only democracy and civilian sway will bring such minds to the helm of affairs. Served by the court

LAST week, the newly established Federal Constitutional Court (FCC) gave two noteworthy judgements. Both concerned legal issues of a political nature — the kind that perhaps would have taken up considerable time and energy in the Supreme Court of the olden days, along with detailed coverage by the media. But those were different times; at present, the cases progressed quietly and the decisions came without much bang or thunder. In one case, the court settled an election dispute that has been going on for the past two years. Back in 2024, the JUI-F had disputed the notification of a woman to one of its reserved seats. It claimed that the woman who had been notified simply shared the first name — Sadaf — with the woman mentioned on the party’s list. The woman on the JUI-F list, Sadaf Yasmin, had decided against submitting her papers due to personal reasons; however, a woman called Sadaf Ihsan (with whom the party says it has no connection) submitted her papers and got nominated by the ECP for the seat. The JUI-F claims Ihsan impersonated Sadaf Yasmin despite having a different name and was somehow identified as the latter. When this was pointed out to the Election Commission of Pakistan, not only did it withdraw the announcement, but also suspended the official who issued the notification. This was reported in Dawn. But the matter didn’t end there. Sadaf Ihsan approached the Peshawar High Court (PHC) and was reinstated. The party then challenged the decision in the Supreme Court, winning reprieve for itself. Ihsan was suspended. But the relief was simply an interim order, and once the FCC was established, the file was transferred to it. The FCC sent the case back to the ECP, which in January this year reinstated Sadaf Ihsan in the seat. This past week, the JUI-F’s appeal against this decision was dismissed by the FCC. Ihsan is now a JUI-F MNA, while the party continues to insist that she is not a member and was never on the list submitted to the ECP. Ihsan, according to a report on the Fafen website, claims she and her family are associated with the party. Three cases, three judgements. But they reveal quite a lot of what the court appears to stand for. It now appears the decision is final and no appellate forums remain. Another interesting aspect of this case is that this seat was given to the JUI-F after the decision that deprived the PTI of reserved seats. In a second case, the FCC has apparently stopped the PHC from hearing cases regarding the custody or arrest of PTI workers. With the imposition of the emergency in KP and a crackdown against the party in the province, multiple cases concerning the whereabouts or bail of PTI members ended up in the PHC, which then passed an interim order preventing such detentions. As in many cases, bail had also been granted; the provincial government challenged them in the FCC. According to one report in Dawn, more than 60 cases in which bail had been given were challenged by the KP government in the FCC. After hearing the cases, the FCC stopped the high court from hearing these matters; its argument was that since the army has been called in, in aid of civil power, the court could no longer hear matters under Article 199, which, in layperson’s terms, is equivalent to what we once saw as the suo motu powers of the Supreme Court. According to one lawyer’s comment, which he posted on social media, this order means the high court can no longer entertain any writ petitions, which, according to him, is a power the courts continued to enjoy even under martial law regimes. In other words, the order limits the PHC in matters far wider than simply the arrests of and bail for PTI workers. It means the high court cannot hear any matters related to fundamental rights. While these two cases date back to just the past week, a third judgement is a little older. It had already been discussed in quite a bit of detail in Dawn and elsewhere. But it is still worth mentioning. In a recent case, the FCC seems to have provided a restricted interpretation of workplace harassment. Ignoring past precedents, which had interpreted workplace harassment more liberally, the court has now ruled that harassment is to be limited to actions of an overtly sexual nature; previously, judgements had interpreted the law to include what is defined as gender-based discrimination. A piece by Barrister Rida Hosain pointed out that in a previous decision, Justice Ayesha Malik had said that “reducing harassment to conduct that is sexual in nature ‘takes away the essence’ of the harassment law, and ignores that harassment is often less about sexual interest and more about reinforcing existing power dynamics”. Hosain quoted Justice Ayesha Malik as saying that the very “purpose of harassment laws is to address gender-based discrimination at the workplace and not to limit it to sexual forms of harassment”. But the FCC has now seen it differently. And while the decision drew criticism, it is fortunate that amendments to the law itself have provided a more wide-ranging definition. However, the ruling of the FCC does mean that complainants in incidents or cases that predate the amendment may have to prove harassment according to the FCC’s interpretation. Women lawyers in particular have discussed this issue in detail in articles and commentary that have appeared in Dawn. Three cases, three judgements. But they reveal quite a lot of what the FCC appears to stand for — to those of us who have little understanding of the legalese but who do keep an eye on the politics of the country — be it fundamental rights, women’s rights, or perhaps those of political parties. Restructuring plans

NO matter how strongly the PPP, a key coalition partner, wishes to see it buried, the push for new administrative units and provinces seems to be picking up steam once again. Defence Minister Khawaja Asif brought the issue into the limelight on Sunday, telling an interviewer that the country could have as many as 12 new provinces if the exercise were driven by administrative needs rather than ethnic, linguistic, or political considerations. Yet in Pakistan, redrawn boundaries always carry ethnic and political weight. Speculation about a possible administrative restructuring has intensified of late, with talk of it soon being accomplished through a 28th amendment to the Constitution. It is also suggested that while the amendment may be fast-tracked, perhaps wrapped up within the year, its actual implementation will be deferred until after the next general election because of the formidable legal and administrative challenges involved in redistributing the state’s resources equitably. But if the implementation can wait, why not the legislation? The government may be tempted to push ahead while it can still count on a majority in parliament, but a majority alone is not a consensus. A proposal of such immense national import demands nothing less. The Election Commission’s botched conduct of the 2024 general election has also left serious, lingering doubts about how far the current legislature represents the public will — doubts that might taint any constitutional change of this magnitude that it passes. It would, therefore, be a much wiser move, politically speaking, to take up any debate on administrative restructuring again after the next general election. In the interim, parliament should pursue electoral reforms with the opposition to ensure that the commission does not repeat its 2024 mistakes, so that the next election earns the public’s trust and yields a legislature whose mandate is beyond doubt. Until then, the federal and provincial governments should at least make an effort to revive the local government structure and give it a fair chance to prove its efficacy as a means of public service delivery. Most arguments in favour of new administrative units circle back to a few central ideas, chief among which is the failure of provincial governments to ensure that resources allocated to them reach the people. The LG system was designed to be that last-mile tier of governance that would make this possible, but, sadly, successive civilian governments have failed to operationalise it effectively, preferring instead to concentrate administrative, political and financial power in a few provincial centres. Population growth and internal migration have sharply increased pressures on existing administrative centres in recent decades, but empowered LGs can redistribute that burden without redrawing provincial boundaries.
