Welfarism is dead

PARADED as amongst the greatest achievements of ‘advanced’ capitalist countries in the post-World War II period, the welfare state epitomised an era of widespread social prosperity. The mass of people in Western Europe, North America, and Australasia was largely guaranteed public education, healthcare, water, electricity, and other material needs. Working-age populations either found work in bustling manufacturing industries or received significant unemployment benefits. The anti-communist US ideologue, Walt Rostow, boasted that the ‘West’ had successfully entered the age of high mass consumption. Today, as mass protests rock France, the welfare state can officially be pronounced dead. The convulsions were triggered by high school and university students demanding accountability for the rapidly deteriorating and underfunded education sector. The demise extends to all spheres of life, and to all parts of the so-called developed world. The welfare state itself was not a gift from above but a compulsion forced upon ruling classes of Western countries petrified of working-class populations at home and colonised people demanding self-rule in Asia and Africa. The fear was that the masses would be taken in by the irrepressible appeal of Soviet, and later, Chinese socialism. Seen thus, the welfare state represented a historic compromise between capital and labour. The former accepted regulation of profits, progressive taxation, and limits on its political power; the latter took on the role of an empowered citizenry that would eschew revolutionary politics. It is no surprise that the unravelling of this capital-labour compact coincided to a significant extent with the collapse of the Soviet Union and the end of the Cold War. The balance of class forces within Western societies had already started to shift with the emergence of what later became known as neoliberalism in the late 1970s, but the die was cast by the end of actually existing socialism. The demise extends to all spheres. It has been a slow death, because welfare apparatuses do not disappear overnight; neither do people’s expectations of the state. Indeed, what is happening in France today is itself a reflection that the mass of the people deeply imbibed ideas of citizenship during the era of welfarism. Compared to our society, students, workers, and other social forces in countries like France are still well-organised and possess the ability and will to rock the system. Nevertheless, the unleashing of neoliberal capital and the attendant rollback of public funding in health, education, and other public infrastructure have had devastating effects. Stagnating real incomes, growing inequality, and an overall decline in social well-being have been feasted on by hate-mongering right-wing forces, while entities like the British Labour Party and French Socialist Party have totally abandoned the left project. The shameful retreat of social democratic parties aside, the welfare state will not return because the historical conjuncture that spawned it is over. The reconstruction of war-ravaged Britain and France from the late 1940s was funded by Washington through the so-called Marshall Plan; post-Nazi West Germany was also given an economic foothold in world trade because of US largesse. The US empire is no longer willing to prop up Europe; it is also drastically cutting aid to the so-called developing world. It’s worth being reminded that the zenith of the welfare state in ‘advanced’ capitalist countries was coeval with either aid dependency, violent coups, or imperialist wars in the erstwhile Third Wo­­rld. Think Pakistan under Ayub Khan, or Vietnam, the Congo and Chile. A few Third World countries broke with the dominant trend, notably the East Asian ‘developmental state’. Some, like South Korea, benefited from being central to Washington’s Cold War designs, their infant industries given preferential access to the US market. But state apparatuses were authoritarian, and there were fewer concessions made to labouring classes by capitalist interests in comparison to the Western prototype. We in Pakistan have never really had a welfarist interregnum. When socialism was a mass ideology in the 1960s and 1970s, governing regimes adopted a few tangible policies like land reforms and nationalisation. But the state remained colonial in its orientation and was never fully subdued to the public interest. Today, like many of its counterparts, the Pakistani state couldn’t care less about the masses’ welfare. Profit and repression are its only concerns. When mass protests do erupt, structural change remains elusive. A new era of mass prosperity to transcend the historical inheritance of the capitalist world system requires both ideology and organisation. Only then can we force the state and capital into retreat. How to sustain research?

IN terms of urgent and important problems requiring resolution, Pakistan’s education sector is the real frontier. With some 26 million five- to 16-year-olds out of school, a lack of universal enrolment, high dropout rates, and the poor quality of education provided to most of those lucky enough to be enrolled, could any issue be more important? Pakistan has a very young population; we are still growing at 2.4 per cent every year. This means that approximately 6m children are added to the population annually. In more developed societies, the frontiers are about ageing populations and health. In Pakistan, they are about children, their schooling and health; young people and their education and skills; and early employment opportunities. All this implies that Pakistan provides a research frontier for those interested in education. And we need a lot of research. We face big questions and need good answers: how can we provide education to millions; how do we improve the quality of education; how do we manage private versus public schools; how do we recruit, train, and support teachers; how do we govern and manage schools and systems better; how do we ensure good curricula, books, assessments, teaching, etc? The list is long; we need good research to answer all these questions and more. But, historically — and even today — education research has not been a priority area for funding. First, the education sector, as a whole, has always been underfunded. Even in better days, the state never spent more than 2pc of GDP on education; currently, we are at less than 1pc. Within this, one can imagine what funding for research would be and what, within research funding, funding for education research must have been. Second, there are strong biases in research funding agencies, such as the Higher Education Commission, for research in science and technology as against research in the social sciences, humanities and the arts. The general thinking is that Pakistan needs engineers and technologists — and thus research in science and technology for development. Research in the social sciences, humanities, and the arts is not seen as important or needed, nor is it viewed as providing the returns that society needs. Historically, research done on education in Pakistan was conducted either locally — with smaller budgets and samples — by academics and students in university education programmes, or by foreign academics and academics based abroad. The latter had higher budgets. A lot of the research was funded by multilateral and bilateral agencies such as the World Bank, DFID (now FCDO — the Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office), USAID, GIZ, etc. A lot of the local research was not published internationally, while international work did little or nothing to develop local capacity. Some good partnerships developed between local and international academics, but these were rare and tended not to last beyond the life of the project. Education research has not been a priority area for funding. It has been interesting to follow the Data and Research in Education — Research Consortium (DARE-RC) in this context. This FCDO-funded programme, due to end by December, tried to change the existing equilibrium. The main ingredients of change were the following: a) it encouraged local researchers to apply for competitive funding; b) it encouraged collaborations across local institutions; c) it encouraged collaborations across local and international academics and institutions as well; d) it committed to making all data public; and e) it insisted on quality outputs that should be published internationally. More than 40 different studies were commissioned; many of them have been completed and their reports and policy briefs are available. Some of the early studies have already been slated for publication in international journals, while many are in the process of being submitted. Some will be appearing in a special issue of an international journal. Many interesting partnerships have been created among local universities, between universities and research institutions, between local and international researchers, and between local and international universities. This has led to quite a bit of capacity building, and has provided each project with junior research assistants. Quite a few people have been trained and mentored over the last few years. There has been considerable interaction with policymakers and the wider public, with one or two DARE-RC events held every couple of weeks this year. DARE-RC has even started a yearly conference on education. The second edition is at the end of the month in Lahore. But there are issues too. As DARE-RC winds down and the funding ends, it appears that, given the new global funding realities, FCDO is not going to support such programmes anymore. What happens to the gains made? Will the collaborations be sustained? Will someone take over the yearly conference? Will the fora created for capacity building and sharing research continue beyond DARE-RC funding? Will the sharing of data for research continue? Will cooperation between researchers and policymakers continue? Or will it lapse as it has on previous occasions? Will the government, federal or provincial education departments, or HEC take on the role of research funder? The research has been very policy-focused and has helped in critical areas such as teacher recruitment and licensing, inclusive education, improved teaching and learning in classrooms, education and support for children with disabilities, and curricula and textbook improvements. Do departments see value in such research, and will they provide support for sustaining and expanding the networks that have been created? Research is the backbone of progress, but quality research is expensive, hard to do, and even harder to sustain. We urgently need a lot of high-quality research in education. Recent investments, through DARE-RC in particular, have provided insights and lessons on how networks capable of producing the high-quality research needed to improve education outcomes can be created. But will these gains be sustainable beyond the life of the project? What will continue and what will not? A lot depends on whether the HEC or the education departments come forward to support and expand the current networks. Nuclear uncertainty

CONSIDERING the poor state of bilateral relations between Pakistan and India, there is always a possibility of miscalculating a situation, especially in the absence of regular contact. These concerns take on greater importance in the nuclear domain. In this regard, officials associated with Pakistan’s nuclear programme have rightly warned that India’s reported attempt to alter the strategic balance has heightened risks of escalation in South Asia. Speaking at a conference in Islamabad, retired Lt-Gen Khalid Kidwai, adviser to the National Command Authority, highlighted a report by the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, which said that India had deployed nuclear warheads on a submarine as part of “deterrence patrols”. He also cited New Delhi’s lack of advance notification for ballistic missile tests. The official observed that these developments cast doubt on India’s no-first-use policy, and were seen as an “irresponsible escalatory transition” towards nuclear asymmetry. Lack of coordination between nuclear states creates great unknowns, and such a situation is untenable in a neighbourhood as volatile as South Asia. It should be remembered that it was only last year that Pakistan and India confronted each other on the battlefield, illustrating how quickly matters can escalate. In such an atmosphere, India’s apparent attempts to alter the strategic balance are cause for serious concern. As Lt-Gen Kidwai pointed out, Pakistan may “review and reconsider” its deployment options, while reminding New Delhi of this country’s full spectrum deterrence policy. With matters frozen in the political and diplomatic spheres, it will take military-to-military engagement to prevent miscalculation in the strategic sphere. This can be carried out through the directors general military operations hotline, or via other appropriate channels. India’s political class — as well as military officials — has issued jingoistic statements targeting Pakistan. There is certainly no room for such bluster where the issue of nuclear weapons is concerned. To ward off any potential catastrophe, the situation must be handled in a rational manner and lines of communication need to be kept open to ensure conventional or nuclear signals are not misconstrued. India should halt its highly provocative nuclear-armed submarine patrols and engage with Pakistan to ensure there are no misunderstandings, particularly in the strategic sphere. Sound macroeconomic policies in recent years have helped stabilise the economy and preserve growth despite the impact of the Middle East conflict. These improvements were recognised through upgrades by two major international rating agencies, S&P and Moody’s, and enabled Pakistan to return to international debt markets with the issuance of a $3 billion Eurobond. “Pakistan has made real progress in restoring stability, and markets have taken notice,” World Bank Country Dir­ector Bolormaa Amgaa­baz­­ar said. “But real per capita growth — the rate at which the income of the average Pakistani increases — remains stuck at around 1.6pc.
