What happens when a bank ducks rules in Pakistan? For Deutsche Bank, perhaps nothing

In 2018, the world turned its microscope on Pakistan’s financial system. Placed on the Financial Action Task Force’s (FATF) “gray list”, the country was thrust into a frantic race to prove it could secure its financial system against the flow of illicit capital. It was a moment of national urgency, defined by rigorous audits and a desperate need to reclaim international legitimacy in a landscape shadowed by allegations of money laundering and terror financing. The decision sent shockwaves through the country’s financial system and the resulting restrictions battered the economy. Yet, even as policymakers were scrambling to bolster the defences of the nation’s financial system, vulnerabilities in the internal systems of Deutsche Bank’s Pakistan operations were complicating the fight against illicit money flows. The global banking giant’s own assessment had classified Pakistan as a “restricted” high-risk country, subjecting businesses connected to it to additional checks and limitations. An internal report cited Pakistan’s “terrorism and Taliban links” and proximity to Iran as key reasons for the classification. Bank documents, however, show a history of overlooked alerts and bypassed regulations, and in some instances, an inability to identify who was truly moving the money in this “restricted country”. These internal records are part of over 15 million internal Deutsche Bank files leaked to the journalists Josy Joseph and Khadija Sharife. The records span many of the bank’s most sensitive departments: financial crime, sanctions monitoring, internal auditing, and the units responsible for deciding whether risky clients stay or go. This report is part of Inside Deutsche Bank, a months-long investigation of the leak involving over 200 journalists from more than 50 countries. The records reviewed by Dawn do not necessarily establish that suspicious or illicit transactions passed through the bank. What they reveal, however, is that while Pakistan was under intense international scrutiny over its financial controls, one of the world’s largest banks, while operating within the territory, struggled to distinguish customers from non-customers, monitor financial transactions and implement safeguards against financial crime. The Pakistan operation Deutsche Bank has held a commercial banking license in Pakistan since 1962. It processes hundreds of millions of dollars’ worth of transactions into, out of and within the country every year. It currently employs close to 100 staff in its Karachi and Lahore branches, offering Pakistan-based clients a “wide range of corporate and investment bank products and services, including foreign exchange, fixed income sales and trading, cash management, trade finance and treasury solutions for corporates”. Over the years, the bank’s internal controls against financial crime have been found wanting in multiple jurisdictions across the world. It has paid billions of dollars in fines and settlements over interest-rate manipulation, failures to prevent money laundering and other misconduct. In Pakistan, however, its small yet significant operation has maintained an image of respectability, not once being named in any scandal or accused of involvement in financial irregularities. According to internal documents, Pakistan has been one of the most active countries in Deutsche Bank’s global trade finance business. In 2016, it ranked third by the number of letters of credit handled by the bank, behind only China and India. That year, Deutsche Bank processed an estimated €872 million in incoming funds and €255 million in outgoing funds for Pakistan. Across its global network, the bank exchanged around 187,000 incoming and 174,000 outgoing cross-border payment instructions involving Pakistan in the same period. About six in 10 were in US dollars. Locally, the bank’s operations were relatively small and largely hands-on. An internal Deutsche Bank Pakistan spreadsheet dated December 2019 shows its local teams dealt with an average of about 4,560 items a day across cash counters, cheque clearing, payment orders and electronic payments. None of these was fully automated, according to the document. The spreadsheet also recorded more than 14,000 daily reviews of transactions flagged by sanctions screening, illustrating the substantial compliance workload the bank faced alongside its routine operations. Local compliance challenges In October 2019, the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) introduced key requirements under its Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML) framework and ordered banks to implement them with immediate effect. However, after feedback from some banks on implementation challenges, the regulator extended the deadline to September 30, 2020. The framework, seen by Dawn, came on the heels of pressure from the FATF and aimed to strengthen “trade-related Anti-Money Laundering/Combating Financing of Terrorism (AML/CFT) regime and restrict possible misuse of banking channels”. It was part of a broader overhaul of processes relating to financial institutions. Yet, internal records show Deutsche Bank Pakistan failed to implement two essential safeguards months after the September 2020 deadline: a price due diligence mechanism to identify artificially inflated or undervalued trade transactions; and an automated Management Information System (MIS) to monitor suspicious activity across its vast transaction volume. “DB Pakistan Trade Finance has not fulfilled the SBP’s Trade Based Money Laundering (TBML) Framework requirements on implementation of a price due diligence mechanism and an automated MIS tool,” Khurram Amanullah, the bank’s principal auditor for Pakistan, wrote in an email on July 30, 2021, almost 10 months after the SBP’s revised deadline. The email was addressed to Markus Weick, who was part of the bank’s Trade Finance Product Management team in Germany. At least a dozen other bank staff were copied. According to Amanullah, “DB Pakistan Trade Finance spent excessive time in deciding whether to implement the relevant system in-house or outsourced to external vendor. (Lack of Diligence).” Amanullah and Weick did not respond to requests for comment. This wasn’t the first time the bank had flagged shortcomings in its trade finance processes in Pakistan. An earlier group audit, published in 2017, into Deutsche Bank Pakistan’s operations in 2016 had flagged similar concerns, stating: “Trade Finance Operations (TFO) does not always check whether underlying goods (HS Codes) for letter of credit (LC) transactions meet local requirements. In addition, Trade Product Management do not fully document expected client trade flows required to monitor [anti-money laundering] risk and identify potential red flags (GTB Due Diligence policy requirement).” This was almost three years before the SBP ordered banks to address such risks. Yet it seems nothing had been done to mitigate them even by July 2021. Whose money? Who knows Beyond trade finance protocols, the 2017 audit report flagged another discovery: “DB Pakistan does not perform transaction filtering (sanction screening) for manual domestic cheque payments (€4.6bn in 2016) and a subset of local bank payments made through DBDC (global app).” All banks in Pakistan are required to cross-check their clients against sanctions lists — such as the United Nations Security Council Consolidated Sanctions List and the National Counter Terrorism Authority Proscribed Persons List — and report any irregularities to the Financial Monitoring Unit, Pakistan’s central financial intelligence unit. A banker with over two decades of experience in the compliance departments of various Pakistani banks told Dawn that the SBP requires banks to screen all active clients against these lists every month. “The lists are frequently updated, sometimes even within hours, so it’s common practice to perform the screening regularly,” he explained. The 2017 audit report identified the risk that “DB Pakistan may process transactions with sanctioned/embargo counterparties or jurisdictions without detection.” The bank was clearly wary that some suspicious transactions may have escaped detection, especially considering the number of transactions and the quantum of funds involved. This was against the Deutsche Bank Group’s own Embargo Policy, which mandates that “all transactions resulting in the transfer of assets or which instruct the transfer of assets are to be filtered against the applicable embargo lists prior to leaving DB Group, or subject to further processing within DB Group, to ensure DB Group does not process transactions with sanctioned designations”. In 2016, the report noted, the matter had been escalated by the Pakistan operations staff to senior management, but the former were advised ”not to do anything further” because “… a system flag was due to be implemented across the group”. This wasn’t the first time the bank had faced such a situation in the region. In 2015, according to the audit report, Deutsche Bank had paid a $258 million fine “as a result of sanction breaches for transactions in embargoed Middle East territories”. The report detailed that Deutsche Bank Pakistan had processed 76,821 unfiltered cheques worth €4.6 billion — €3.5 billion in deposits and €1.1 billion in payments — in 2016 alone. “The majority (circa €2.9bn of deposits) relates to one high-risk client, which is in the process of being off-boarded,” said the report. The audit report was silent on who this high-risk client was and why they were being off-boarded. Another audit report from 2020 on Deutsche Bank Pakistan’s anti-financial crime functions noted that not a single Suspicious Activity Report or Suspicious Transaction Report was generated by the bank between July 2019 and June 2020 — a highly unusual occurrence given the volume of transactions the bank was handling. Banks must file such reports to legal and regulatory authorities whenever their internal compliance checks detect potential money laundering, terror financing or other financial crime. Confusion over KYC function In September 2019, Chiara Monaco from the anti-financial crime unit at Deutsche Bank’s headquarters in Frankfurt emailed bank staff in Pakistan to flag a concern regarding “certain sub-accounts in Pakistan belonging to customers not KYC’ed”. KYC stands for “Know Your Customer” or “Know Your Client”. This is the information-gathering process by which a bank checks who you are, works out what you use your account for, and decides how risky you are as a customer. Banks need it to meet anti-money laundering (AML) and Combating the Financing of Terrorism (CFT) rules. In another email the next day, Monaco wrote: “We found 1000s of subaccounts in Pakistan without KYC, opened for custody purposes and belonging to clients of State Street Bank and Bank of NY Mellon.” “Can you please help [us] to fully understand the business model, the setup and background behind these accounts. They have not been KYC’ed,” she added. The emails triggered a debate among the bank’s personnel regarding the nature of these accounts and whether they were subject to KYC regulations. Custody is a banking service where a bank holds and protects financial assets — stocks, bonds, securities and the like — on behalf of clients. In this instance, the “sub-accounts” referred to Special Convertible Rupee Accounts (SCRA) maintained by Deutsche Bank Pakistan for two major international financial institutions, both based in the United States. An SCRA account, introduced by the Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP) in 2008, is a kind of specialised, repatriable bank account that allows non-resident investors, foreign nationals and overseas Pakistanis to trade freely in local capital markets. The sub-accounts in question held money in the form of securities or treasury bonds belonging to other investors, many of whom never dealt with Deutsche Bank or its Pakistan operations directly. “The local AML/CFT Regulations of State Bank of Pakistan require due diligence/KYC to be performed on the customers’ of the bank, where such underlying accounts do not fall under the definition of customers as per the regulations,” Zeeshan Waheed, the head of the compliance and anti-financial crime unit in Pakistan, wrote back. He added, “very recently we had a review being conducted by the Central Bank’s Inspection team and they had gone through the business model of DB Pakistan for Security Services and its operations, where they had not highlighted any concern/observation from an ML/TF risk point of view or overall controls with respect to compliance of local AML/CFT Regulations”. A year earlier, the SBP had issued an update to its AML/CFT Regulations explicitly defining a “customer” as “any person having a relationship with the bank, such as holding deposits, placing money with the bank/DFI, availing financial services, locker facilities, safe deposit facilities, or custodial services”. Under these regulations (Paras 7-9), banks are required to apply customer due diligence measures for all customers, including “identifying and verifying beneficial owners through reliable sources; understanding ownership/control structures for legal persons; and verifying senior managing officials where no controlling owner is identified”. Moreover, according to the Anti-Money Laundering Act, 2010, a beneficial owner is defined as a “natural person who ultimately owns or controls a customer or the natural person on whose behalf a transaction is being conducted”. The same law also provides a framework for due diligence as follows: identify the customer and verify the customer’s identity on the basis of documents, data or information obtained from reliable and independent sources; identify the beneficial owner and take reasonable measures to verify the beneficial owner’s identity on the basis of documents, data or information obtained from reliable sources and be satisfied that it knows who the beneficial owner is; understand and, as appropriate, obtain information on the purpose and intended nature of the business relationship; and monitor the business relationship on an ongoing basis. Dawn reached out to Monaco and Waheed for comment, but did not hear back. Three bankers, who spoke to Dawn on condition of anonymity, said that the SBP requires banks to conduct customer due diligence (CDD) in these cases. “KYC/CDD requirements apply, although the precise extent of due diligence on each underlying investor depends on the account structure, customer relationship and applicable regulatory requirements,” one banker with over two decades of experience in compliance explained to Dawn via email. “Under Regulation R-2 of the SBP AML/CFT/CPF Regulations, banks must conduct CDD in accordance with the Anti-Money Laundering Act, 2010.” Deutsche Bank Pakistan, the emails show, vacillated on whether the beneficiaries of custodial accounts were in fact its customers, despite the fact that the SBP and other banks did apply KYC requirements to such accounts. The debate lasted more than two months before the issue was closed. As of last year, Deutsche Bank Pakistan held Rs 143.63 billion in government securities and shares, as per its published financial statement. According to the statement, the bank does not treat these as its own assets. Until 2019 at least, it apparently did not know who the actual owners of these assets were. The documents reviewed by Dawn do not make clear if the 2019 Trade-Based Money Laundering compliance framework and KYC disputes were ultimately resolved, whether any corrective measures addressed the concerns raised by senior compliance officials, or whether the deficiencies identified by auditors in 2021 were ever fully remedied. Each of the audit reports reviewed by Dawn recommended short-term and long-term measures to be adopted by Deutsche Bank Pakistan to mitigate the risks flagged. It remains unclear whether these were implemented. It also remains unclear whether any of these issues were taken up by the SBP as part of its annual audits. Despite repeated reminders, the SBP did not respond to a detailed questionnaire sent by Dawn. In a summary response to over a dozen questions from Dawn, Deutsche Bank stated: “We do not comment on potential, former or existing client relationships. “Deutsche Bank takes its obligations to prevent financial crime seriously and maintains controls designed to comply with applicable anti-money laundering, sanctions and other regulatory requirements.” Editing by Roman Gautam/Himal Southasian; Header art by Mohsin Alam/Dawn The Inside Deutsche Bank investigation was coordinated by Barracuda Media in South Africa, with technical support from the Data and Research Center (DARC) in Germany.

Cabinet approves deregulation of sugar sector to meet vital IMF benchmark

ISLAMABAD: To meet a long-postponed structural benchmark of the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the federal cabinet on Tuesday approved the deregulation of the sugar sector, an end to its export subsidies and free movement among the provinces. The cabinet approved the ‘National Policy for Deregulation of Sugar Sector’ through circulation while Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif “recused himself from the sugar sector related decisions” owing to his immediate family’s sugar business, informed sources told Dawn. The decision came after the government allowed the export of more than 400,000 tonnes of sugar on Friday, in addition to 200,000 tonnes already exported a few months ago. The IMF has continuously pushed for a policy ending the government’s role in sugar sector operations, from sugarcane crop prices to sugar production and import-export, leaving it to market forces. The fund also minutely remained engaged with the terms and conditions of a ministerial committee which finalised the sugar deregulation policy. Pakistan has been in breach of the structural benchmark of the IMF since June 2026 and its approval was mandatory for scheduling the IMF executive board meeting, now set for Dec 11. The policy approval was hampered by Sindh’s virtual veto, although three other provinces had already consented to the draft. After hectic backdoor negotiations, the Sindh government finally cleared the policy on Nov 21 after securing certain amendments. The policy was finalised by a ministerial committee led by Power Minister Awais Leghari. The committee also includes the ministers for finance, food security and economic affairs; the premier’s adviser for industries; the chairman of the Federal Board of Revenue (FBR); secretaries of concerned ministries and provincial chief secretaries, as well as other stakeholders. Being a structural benchmark of the IMF, the timeline for the completion of the process was June 30, 2026. The fund proposed revised Terms of Reference (ToRs) on July 31, 2026 for incorporation in the ToRs of the committee, which were agreed to by the prime minister, official records seen by Dawn suggested. The Ministry of Industries and Production, on the IMF’s directions, “inserted some amendments and shared the said draft policy with the IMF” by the end of August, which were fine-tuned by the fund again on Oct 1. The policy provides a predictable mechanism for imports and exports with a complete end to export subsidies, the cabinet was told. There will be no restrictions on the inter-provincial movement of sugar, and transitional arrangements have been provided for the current crushing season. At the input stage, the government has removed restrictions on planting zones and cane varieties, providing freedom to cane producers to sell their crop to any buyer and mills to sell their product. The sugarcane price has now been left to market forces to determine, but there will be a negative list of banned sugar varieties. At the processing stage, there will be no restriction on the establishment of new mills or capacity approvals for mills; the millers will have the freedom to choose raw material sources and take market-based production decisions. Price controls by the governments will come to an end. For creating an enabling environment for smooth transition, the deregulation policy provides certain safeguards to the farmers in the shape of third-party validation of weighing scales, sucrose testing framework and interest payments by millers on the late payment of sugarcane dues. The sugarcane cess has been promised for effective use of research, as well as easy access to credit for farmers and millers, along with farm mechanisation schemes going ahead. The policy, nevertheless, retains the government’s right to maintain buffer sugar stocks and take measures to access correct and timely information on sugarcane crop, sugar production and market availability. It will also encourage working towards mandating ethanol blending and providing a fair and competitive market.
