What are US H-1B visas for skilled workers and what changes loom?

US employers seeking to hire skilled foreign workers can use the federal government’s H-1B visa programme for a fee, allowing tech and other professionals from abroad to work in the United States for a few years. US President Donald Trump, who has pursued a broad immigration crackdown since taking office last year, has lambasted the programme and wants to permanently raise the fee from between $2,000 and $5,000 to more than $100,000. Despite criticism that some companies use the visas to hire lower-paid foreign workers instead of Americans, business groups and companies say they help recruit highly skilled professionals and address a lack of qualified US workers in some industries. The visas, established by Congress in 1990, are especially critical for tech companies seeking to recruit from India and China. Consultancy companies such as Deloitte, PwC and Ernst & Young as well as outsourcing giants such as Tata Consultancy Services, Infosys, HCL Tech and LTIMindtree are also top H-1B sponsors. Here are more details about the programme and what changes are happening under the Trump administration Who is fighting over the fees? Trump last year dramatically raised the cost of H-1B visas to $100,000, prompting several lawsuits. While his initial fee increase is set to expire in September, the Department of Homeland Security this week proposed raising it to a permanent $103,265. A group representing India’s technology industry has said it is engaging with US stakeholders over the proposed increase, and urged the administration to consider the benefits it offers companies seeking to temporarily address worker shortages. Tesla, Amazon, OpenAI and other companies have all faced questions over their H-1B visa use, especially amid US layoffs and a tighter job market facing American workers. Could existing lawsuits challenge the new rule? A Boston-based appeals court is reviewing a June decision by a federal judge who ruled that the fee was illegal and blocked the Trump administration from collecting it. Another court is also considering whether a judge properly rejected a challenge to the fee by the US Chamber of Commerce, the largest US business lobbying group. Democratic-led states as well as a coalition of unions and employers have also filed lawsuits. The pending legal cases could be amended to challenge this week’s proposed rule after it is finalized. How is enhanced vetting hitting employers? Changes to how potential visa holders are scrutinised and processed have impacted companies’ hiring and expansion plans, with some top H-1B users, such as Google, moving to increase operations in India. Trump’s administration in December said it would increase its vetting of H-1B applicants with a particular focus on what it deemed censorship and free speech. The State Department ordered US consulates to scrutinise the applicants’ LinkedIn profiles and resumes as well as those of any accompanying family members for all visa applicants but particularly those with technology backgrounds seeking H-1Bs who may have been “involved in the suppression of protected expression.” How are registration numbers being impacted? The H-1B programme offers 65,000 visas annually, with another 20,000 for workers with advanced degrees, typically granted for three years and renewable to six. As of late February, about 70 employers had paid the $100,000 fee on a total of 85 visa applications, court filings show. Employers last year registered for about 344,000 H-1B visas, down more than 25 per cent from 2024 and fewer than half of the 759,000 visas sought in 2023, data from US Citizenship and Immigration Services shows.

Pakistan's amateur radios are tuned in for the apocalypse

When the earthquake struck in 2005, two men in Islamabad loaded their bags and headed for Azad Kashmir. They made it as far as Kohala bridge where the road got cut off. From there on, they made it by foot and hitchhiked on motorcycles and the back of pick-up trucks to Muzaffarabad. One of the few offices still functioning there was Rescue 15 whose building had half collapsed. The men asked the surprised police officials inside if they had a car battery. The officials led them to a crushed Suzuki Mehran. The men got to work. A radio transceiver was plugged into the battery and they started transmitting messages to Islamabad since telephone landlines had collapsed. In the next few days they sent nearly 3,000 messages to Islamabad by radio, which their affiliates spread throughout Pakistan by phone. Most messages were confirmations of survivals and deaths. These men belonged to the voluntary Pakistan Amateur Radio Society (PARS) which has been around since 1970. It is made up of licensed professionals who operate amateur or ham radio, which keep people connected when all else fails. Decades later, the society is still going strong, and since its fledgling foray into Kashmir has increasingly taken up the cause of rescue and relief work when humanitarian disaster strikes in Pakistan. Ham radios come to Pakistan Ham radios emerged at the end of the 19th century with licensing starting from the US around 1912. One theory suggests that it was the initials of a group of early adopters—Hyman, Almy, and Murray at Harvard University’s radio club—that shortened first into HYALMU and then the name HAM. A more popular theory is that in the early days professionals looked down at the amateurs as ‘hams,’ as in ham-fisted or unskilled. Despite the pejorative etymology, the name stuck and is used proudly today. Amateur radio operators have operated here since before Partition. The oldest membership card was issued in November 1947 and a Pakistan Amateur Radio Society one from 1950 carries the call sign AP5B. Records show that one was active in Karachi in 1949 and from Quetta’s Chiltan Hotel in 1953. Old QSL cards from Pakistan (clockwise from top): From 1947 issued under the old VU call sign, from 1949 with the new Pakistani AP call sign, the oldest society card from 1950, and the card for Quetta’s Chiltan Hotel. Photos via PARS Though these records appear in 1950, former PARS President Nasir Khan (call sign AP2NK) said in a 2019 interview that the society was formed in 1970. (This discrepancy could be explained by a change in registration rules after which the society began anew). According to Nasir Khan, many of the initial members, including him, were associated with the telecommunications industry which is why their professional interests led them to ham radio, and ultimately to create the society. When Nasir Khan passed away in 2025, PARS announced that AP2NK was now a ‘silent key’. Ham radio work is driven by enthusiasm and charitable spirit but it is still governed by regulations which say a licensed operator has to be a member of PARS to legally function. Licenses were first issued by the Post and Telegraph department, followed by the Telephone and Telegraph department, and since 1996, the Pakistan Telecommunication Authority (PTA) which now has a dedicated Radio-Based Services section. PTA records thus date back to 1962-63 even though files and folders have moved around between departments. Radio operators in Pakistan must be at least 16 years old. They join PARS as a ‘short-wave listener’ (SWL) for at least one year (like a driving learner’s license) before earning a full license (two years if you are under 18). SWLs can listen but not speak on the radio and use this time to learn mannerisms and techniques. Applications require details of equipment and operators are subjected to security verification which takes three months or more. A written test and interview follow at the PTA. Each newly licensed operator is then assigned a unique call sign, which in Pakistan begin with AP2 (or 5 for organisations) followed by the person’s initials. Radio communication takes place with phonetic codes, so AP2 will become “Alpha Papa 2”, and universally recognised Q codes, such as QTH relating to location and QSL relating to receipt of transmission. Codes such as “5 and 9”, meaning readability and signal strength respectively are at their highest, are also used. Audio of communication between hams in Islamabad and Lahore, a distance of over 283 kilometres. They announce their call signs, and confirm readability and signal strength by saying “5 and 9”. PARS helps applicants prepare and for several years, tests were conducted in coordination with the PTA. The license fee is Rs2,000 and has to be renewed every five years. Members pay the Society Rs2,000 a year, students Rs1,500 for three years and SWLs Rs4,000 for a three-year term. People over 65 years are granted lifetime licenses. Licenses can be revoked legally but according to the PTA no such case has arisen so far. Licenses usually end when holders pass away or fail to renew. The PTA does not allow mobile radio stations in its law. Exceptions can, however, be made for temporary periods in times of emergency and special need. This provision, in particular, is central to how the Society has worked in rescue efforts. ‘It’s not a hobby’ Today the group has around 600 active members of whom around 160 are amateur radio operators and the rest are SWLs or others. Most members have full-time jobs or businesses. One works in the oil and gas industry, some are telecom engineers, another is a cardiologist and several are serving members of the armed forces. Former Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Governor Amir Gulistan Janjua (AP2AGJ) was also a member. There is nothing ‘amateur’ about PARS. During initial interviews, my use of the word ‘hobby’ was met with polite clarification that every member was a professional and the operations are considered a service. Indeed, the Facebook page and website lists PARS as a non-profit organization with coordinators and representatives in all provinces. It does not accept donations or raise funds and the rule is that no politics or personal matters can ever be discussed on air. Equipment is considered expensive but lasts decades. Members learn how to repair their own machines and one member even created a specialized battery that can power several radios at once. This spirit of invention also takes members to science fairs and expos. At an event in NUST in 2026, for example, PARS taught students how to make an antenna using a clothes hanger, with which they talked to someone in Gujranwala. Some of the society’s events, such as the annual meeting of its general body, take place at a university managed by one of the members. No premises have ever been acquired and the Society’s headquarters are generally based at the licensed location of a senior member. At one point it was a school owned by a senior office bearer and later another member’s house.
