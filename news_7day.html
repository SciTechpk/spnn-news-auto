Pakistan links regional cooperation with Afghanistan to action against terrorism at ECO moot

Pakistan desires a peaceful, prosperous and stable Afghanistan, but meaningful progress towards regional cooperation requires the Afghan authorities to address the legitimate security concerns of neighbouring countries, particularly the threat of terrorism, Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar said on Monday. He was addressing the 30th meeting of the Economic Cooperation Organisation (ECO) Council of Ministers in Islamabad, where dignitaries from ECO member states have converged to attend the moot. Currently, Pakistan, Afghanistan, Azerbaijan, Tajikistan, Iran, Turkiye, Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, Kyrgyz Republic and Uzbekistan are part of the ECO. However, no one from Afghanistan is attending the moot, according to the list of delegations for the meeting shared by the Foreign Office. In his opening remarks at the meeting, Dar said a stable Afghanistan, at peace with itself and all its neighbours and fully integrated into the ECO framework, would benefit the Afghan people as well as the wider region by opening up avenues for trade, transit and economic cooperation. “At the same time, meaningful progress requires the Afghan authorities to address the legitimate security concerns of the neighbouring countries, particularly the threat of terrorism,” he added. He stressed that a secure and stable environment was essential for sustained regional economic partnerships, adding that these considerations remained central to Pakistan’s broader connectivity agenda. “Regional connectivity rests on more than the routes that link us. It depends upon trust between states and confidence that international commitments will be honoured. Unresolved disputes and the non-fulfillment of international obligations can erode that trust and impede economic progress,” said Dar. The foreign minister emphasised that respect for international law and treaties is essential to enduring economic partnerships between countries. Pakistan, which holds the ECO chairmanship for 2026-27, is hosting the meeting under the theme “Reviving Regionalism for a Resilient ECO”. Five priorities Outlining Pakistan’s priorities during its chairmanship of the ECO, Dar identified five key areas of focus, including expanding trade and investment, strengthening regional connectivity, advancing the ECO Trade Agreement, building regional resilience and promoting human development. The foreign minister said the region’s natural resources, energy reserves, agricultural potential and strategic location offered considerable opportunities for economic cooperation, but realising that potential required functioning transport corridors, efficient border procedures and predictable transit arrangements. He said Pakistan would work towards wider market access, stronger regional value chains and greater private sector participation, particularly by small and medium-sized enterprises, to boost trade and investment among member states. “Closer interaction among our business communities can help translate commercial potential into greater trade and investment.” On connectivity, Dar said Pakistan would seek to improve transport and transit links between Central Asia, South Asia and Pakistani ports on the Arabian Sea. Harmonised customs procedures, efficient border management and predictable transit arrangements were essential to making regional trade routes commercially viable, he added. The foreign minister also highlighted the need to advance the ECO Trade Agreement, saying the negotiating strategy and roadmap approved by ECO trade ministers in November 2025 provided a basis for modernising the agreement. Pakistan would support efforts to develop a more effective, inclusive and digitally enabled regional trade framework, he said. On regional resilience, Dar called for closer cooperation in disaster preparedness, early warning systems, sustainable water management and climate-resilient infrastructure, noting that climate change and growing pressure on water and energy resources posed serious challenges to regional economies. He also underscored the importance of human development, particularly in education, research, innovation and vocational training, saying greater opportunities in these areas could equip young people with skills needed by changing economies. Promoting entrepreneurship and professional exchanges would further strengthen regional integration, he added. The foreign minister maintained that the five priorities were interconnected and their success would depend on collective efforts by member states. He expressed Pakistan’s readiness to work with ECO members and the organisation’s secretariat on initiatives relating to trade facilitation, transit connectivity and disaster preparedness. Regional peace and diplomatic efforts Dar cautioned that peace and stability were essential for economic development, warning that conflict and insecurity in the region could disrupt trade routes, energy supplies, investment and economic projects. “Peace and development reinforce each other,” he said. He added that Pakistan remained “deeply concerned about conflict and instability” in the region. “Their consequences extend beyond national boundaries, disrupting trade routes, energy supplies, investment, and the economic projects of our peoples.” He said that Pakistan has consistently advocated for “the peaceful resolution of disputes through dialogue and diplomacy in accordance with international law and the United Nations Charter”. Referring to Pakistan’s diplomatic efforts in the US-Iran war, Dar said, “Pakistan will continue these diplomatic efforts in support of dialogue, de-escalation, and lasting regional stability”. He said that Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif and Chief of Defence Forces and Chief of the Army Staff Field Marshal Asim Munir “remained closely engaged with brotherly countries and international partners to facilitate peaceful outcomes”. According to a statement issued by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (MoFA), the Senior Officials Meeting (SOM) was held on Nov 15 under the chairmanship of Foreign Secretary Ambassador Asim Iftikhar Ahmad. The meeting was attended by senior officials from ECO member states, representatives of the ECO Secretariat, and delegates from the organisation’s specialised agencies and regional institutions. The officials reviewed preparations for the ministerial meeting and finalised its agenda and the draft Islamabad Communique for consideration by the foreign ministers. They also reviewed the activities undertaken by the ECO Secretariat during the year, including ongoing programmes and initiatives, outcomes of sectoral ministerial meetings, and the work of subsidiary bodies and specialised institutions, the ministry said. Administrative and financial matters of the organisation also came under discussion, with participants exchanging views on measures to improve its effectiveness. The SOM serves as a preparatory forum for the Council of Ministers, enabling member states to assess progress, discuss issues of mutual interest and build consensus on proposals and draft documents ahead of ministerial-level deliberations. Historic Malot Fort in Chakwal presents a picture of neglect

From a distance, Malot Fort looks like a postcard. Perched on a hilltop in the picturesque Salt Range near Kallar Kahar, the historic monument offers breathtaking views of the surrounding landscape. As visitors climb the hill, the Malot Temple comes into view, standing alone against the vast sky. On a clear day, the River Jhelum can also be seen from the fort. For a moment, the visitor may believe the promotional video circulating on Facebook that describes the site as “Punjab’s undiscovered heritage jewel”. But the experience changes as one approaches the fort. The road disappears into scattered stones, while thorny kikri (Prosopis juliflora) bushes encroach on the pathway. Parthenium, one of the most invasive weeds, has spread across the complex. Newly installed benches lie broken, and a half-built structure stands abandoned. A newly-installed bench lies broken at Malot Fort, reflecting the lack of maintenance at the historic site. Inside the fort, donkeys and cattle graze and rest in areas meant for tourists. Mobile phone companies have also installed towers beside the thousand-year-old monument, creating a strange juxtaposition of modern infrastructure and historic heritage. Described as the most beautiful fort in the Salt Range, Malot has been the subject of two consecutive projects undertaken by the Punjab Archaeology Department and the Tourism Development Corporation of Punjab (TDCP) for its conservation, restoration and development. Despite these initiatives, toilets have yet to be constructed, development work has been halted due to a shortage of funds, and animal waste is scattered across the premises. The fort and village of Malot are named after Raja Mal or Mallu, the first ruler and ancestor of the Janjua tribe of Punjab, who converted to Islam in the early decades of the 11th century during the reign of Mahmood of Ghazna. The centuries-old Malot Temple, built of red sandstone, stands on a hilltop in the Salt Range. The temple was recently conserved by the Archaeology Department though bushes can still be seen growing from its upper structure. The temple in Malot village holds a unique position among the temples of the Salt Range because of its imposing gateway, which was probably constructed at a later date. At present, there is no passage connecting the two structures. The floor levels of both structures are considerably higher than the present ground level of the surrounding area. However, clear evidence indicates that a built-up passage once connected them, approximately 2.4 metres above the current ground level. The distance between the temple and its gateway is 17.4 metres. A crudely built canopy on top of the temple is a much later addition, most probably constructed to serve as a watchtower. Although the exact dates of construction of the Malot Temple and its gateway are unknown, their Kashmiri architectural style places them between the 7th and 10th centuries of the Christian era, when the Salt Range was dominated by Kashmiri Hindu-Shahi dynasties. Both the temple and gateway are protected under the Antiquities Act, 1975. A close-up view of the intricate carvings on the wall of Malot Temple, showcasing the craftsmanship of its builders. The temple was recently conserved by the Archaeology Department. The Directorate of Archaeology, Punjab, launched a conservation and preservation project at Malot in 2021. The project was completed in 2023 at a cost of Rs12 million. The temple was conserved and restored, while wild plants and weeds were also removed from the site. However, in the absence of a caretaker, the weeds gradually returned. Another project for the beautification and development of Malot Fort is being undertaken by the TDCP. Explore the magnificent heritage of Punjab with TDCP.” Featuring a picture of Chief Minister Maryam Nawaz Sharif in its upper-right corner, the video showcased the fort’s scenic beauty and highlighted the development work being carried out at the site. SHANGHAI: China’s independently developed 10-ton electric commercial aircraft CE-25A completed its maiden piloted flight on Saturday, according to the Commercial Aircraft Corporation of China (COMAC). Designed mainly for intercity commercial flights, it can carry 19 passengers and has a maximum takeoff weight of 10 metric tons. Developed jointly by COMAC, Chinese battery maker CATL, Shanghai Jiao Tong University and other partners, the CE-25A has completed 19 unmanned autonomous flight validations. Instead of conventional turbofan or turboprop engines, the CE-25A uses a distributed electric propulsion system consisting of 10 electric ducted fans mounted along the top of its wings. This layout aims to reduce noise and enable takeoffs and landings on shorter runways. The Civil Aviation Administration of China (CAAC) has formally accepted COMAC’s type certificate application under transport-category regulations, initiating the inspection process required before commercial passenger service can begin. How identity politics is redrawing the India-Bangladesh borderlands

THE India–Bangladesh borderlands are not simply spaces of territorial contestation. They are landscapes shaped by shared histories, cultural continuities and deeply intertwined human relationships. These borderlands hold a profound place in Bangladesh’s history. At Baidyanathtala (later renamed Mujibnagar), near the Indian border, the country’s first government took its oath on April 17, 1971. For decades, despite political tensions and an increasingly securitised border, communities on both sides maintained relationships through cross-border marriages, religious practices, trade, shared languages and everyday interactions. The political meaning of these borderlands extends beyond territorial security and migration management, raising a more fundamental question: who has the legitimate right to belong? Recent developments in India’s northeastern border states point towards an increasingly troubling politics of identity. Demolition drives in Assam’s Dhubri district targeting villages largely inhabited by Bengali-speaking Assamese Muslims, the normalisation of derogatory expressions such as Miya, and controversies surrounding villagers’ encounters with the Indian Border Security Force in Tripura have raised questions that extend beyond individual incidents. Who is considered an Indian? Why are certain communities repeatedly required to prove their legitimacy? These questions reveal a changing relationship between territory, identity and citizenship. For people living along the border, belonging is increasingly negotiated through markers that extend beyond legal documentation: their names, language, attire, religious practices and perceived cultural affiliations. The border, in this sense, is becoming more than a geographical boundary. It is also a political instrument for distinguishing between those who are considered insiders and those who are treated as outsiders. This is the cartography of othering: the process through which political narratives redraw the social boundaries of belonging, even within internationally recognised national territories. The problem becomes particularly acute when religious identity is conflated with national allegiance. Bengali-speaking Muslims in West Bengal, Assam and Tripura are frequently situated within political narratives of illegal migration and demographic insecurity. Migration and citizenship are legitimate matters of public policy. States have the authority to regulate their borders and establish legal procedures. But when an entire community is persistently viewed through the lens of suspicion, the distinction between immigration enforcement and identity-based exclusion becomes increasingly difficult to maintain. The political influence of the Bharatiya Janata Party in India’s border states has also contributed to a changing political vocabulary around migration, nationalism and religious identity. Polarising and hateful statements by political leaders, particularly those associated with the political leadership of Assam and West Bengal, have contributed to the normalisation of exclusionary narratives, gradually shifting the boundaries of what is considered acceptable in public discourse. The territorial imagination underlying some political statements is eq­­u­­ally significant. References by Ind­­ian politicians to Chittagong, Noak­hali and Sylhet as territories that should have belonged to India, or as historical mistakes associated with Partition, are not simply rhetorical provocations. They invoke an unfinished geographical imagination in which historical grievance, religious majoritarianism and territorial nati­o­­n­­alism intersect. Such narratives deserve scrutiny not because every political statement signals an immediate territorial ambition, but beca­use they can reshape how neighbouring societies perceive one another. The consequences of this politics do not stop at the border fence. For Bangladesh, these developments carry both political and social implications. Social media has become an important arena where statements and incidents from India’s border states are circulated, interpreted and sometimes amplified without sufficient context. Reports of discrimination or violence can generate legitimate concern, but misinformation and emotionally charged responses can also deepen antagonism within Bangladesh. The result is a transnational politics of suspicion in which developments on one side of the border influence perceptions and political narratives on the other. In this context, Myanmar’s Rohin­gya crisis offers a crucial warning. The progressive exclusion of the Roh­i­­n­­gya from citizenship, their institutionalised marginalisation and eventual mass displacement demonstrate how the gradual denial of belonging can develop into a profound humanitarian catastrophe. Bangladesh continues to bear the consequences nea­rly a decade after the mass exodus of 2017. India and Myanmar are fundamentally different political contexts. The comparison, therefore, should not suggest an inevitable repetition of the Rohingya experience. History, however, offers an important lesson: major crises rarely emerge overnight.
