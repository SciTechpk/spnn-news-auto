Labour in a changing world

“The times, they are a-changin,” is a 1964 iconic song by legendary Bob Dylan. Times have even changed and evolved in the world of work, even in the Pakistani industrial relations ecosystem. Gone are the days when ego-centric labour leaders wielded influence on members, energising them with demagoguery and rabble-rousing. Gone are the days when they would exert their self-generated importance to browbeat and intimidate employers. Within a few years, they faded away in the sunset. They rarely developed their successors. Narcissism prevailed over pragmatism. A new cadre of labour leaders gradually came to the fore, but by that time the worker movement had lost steam, and the number of labour unions across the country had substantially declined; today, only about one per cent of enterprises have registered unions. This new cadre faced, and is still facing, a difficult task in motivating workers to form unions and display brotherhood. They are reasonably successful in stimulating unions in the public sector but have to move boulders, cross turbulent seas, and remove bottlenecks to achieve entry into targeted individual enterprises. The Pakistan United Workers Federation was formed through the merger of ten moderate labour federations. PUWF is the largest and represents over one million workers in 637 unions. The next large federations are the National Labour Federation and the Pakistan Central Mines Workers Federation. However, most workers’ federations lack the critical mass to build a movement to register unions and have their voices heard in the corridors of power. Although major political parties do have their wings or bureaus, the latter seldom have a formidable say in the parties’ policies. There are labour leaders who are still in a time warp. Their speeches revolve around three or four issues that are ignored by employers and, to some extent, by the government. Their favourite is the issuance of appointment letters for workers because, in their opinion, an appointment letter guarantees job security, even though any worker who is underperforming, displays signs of hostility, or is a chronic absentee will soon be chucked out. Even if that worker knocks at the portals of the courts and even if reinstated, that worker will undoubtedly face resistance from management. Another beef against employers, and this is a genuine complaint, is that the prescribed minimum wage is not given to most of the workers. The minimum wage for unskilled workers provides an essential floor of protection, helps prevent exploitation, and supports the welfare of vulnerable workers. The difficulty for employers arises when the Minimum Wage Board sets wages for semi-skilled and skilled workers, since it may not be in the broader interest of industry and labour market dynamics. Skills, productivity, experience, specialisation, market demand, and sectoral requirements generally determine wages of semi-skilled and skilled workers. Such wages are more appropriately regulated through market mechanisms, collective bargaining, and enterprise-level arrangements. In the majority of countries, a single minimum wage is announced by the government, which ensures that it is paid to every worker. It is standard practice and an accepted norm that the employer determines the wages of skilled employees. A skilled worker will not accept minimum wage unless in dire circumstances. Then there is the mantra of what labour leaders refer to as contract workers. This system is more aptly known as outsourcing of manpower or business functions or third-party employment. It is an employment arrangement in which workers perform services for a principal employer but are formally employed by another entity, such as a contractor, service provider, or outsourcing company. Third-party employment is widely used in both the private and public sectors, particularly for labour-intensive functions or those requiring staffing flexibility. They prefer third-party employment for specific reasons, such as operational flexibility, cost efficiency, specialised expertise, business focus, risk management, and project-based employment. Although there is no bar to outsourcing, this system cannot and should not be used as a means to circumvent existing labour laws. Labour leaders agitate over this issue because such workers cannot join the enterprise union, as they are technically employees of the service provider. On the other extreme, some labour leaders possess a negative attitude and are instrumental in promoting and enriching themselves by disseminating complaints against local enterprises, especially garment and knitwear units, to their foreign associates. These foreign entities, usually radical activists, pump up these complaints by sending them to brands that import from Pakistan and strongly demanding that they cease business with that unit. Although it is much ado about nothing, it impacts the nation’s image as well as exports. The world has changed dramatically over the last few decades, and this transformation is a result of increasing globalisation of business, the growth of multinational corporations, the climate crisis, privatisation, and the rapid pace of technological change. Workers must join hands with employers and agitate for the rationalisation of heavy taxation measures, vociferously condemn and denounce the increase in blatant extortion demands on businesses by extremists and criminals, and influence the government to introduce favourable policies to spur industrialisation. Bob Dylan’s message must be absorbed by labour leaders who are still trapped in a time warp. The lyrics are captivating: “Admit that the waters / Around you have grown / And accept it that soon / If your time to you is worth savin’ / And you better start swimmin’ / Or you’ll sink like a stone / For the times they are a-changin’. Rethinking our wheat policy

Pakistan is set to import one million tonnes of wheat as the federal government finds strategic reserves insufficient to meet the provinces’ requirements. More surprisingly, Punjab — the country’s only wheat-surplus province — has also sought 1m tonnes. Wheat imports are not unusual for Pakistan. The country has imported wheat in several financial years over the past decade to bridge supply gaps or rebuild strategic reserves. What makes the current decision unprecedented, however, is its timing. It comes barely three months after harvest, despite repeated official claims, made until only a few weeks ago, that 29.8m tonnes of domestic production, along with over 2m tonnes of carryover stocks, would be sufficient to meet the country’s annual requirements. Adding to the puzzle, poultry feed mills are not consuming wheat this year because maize — the poultry sector’s preferred feed grain — is considerably cheaper. Moreover, the border with Afghanistan has remained closed to both formal exports and cross-border smuggling. Yet wheat prices have surged from around Rs3,300 to Rs4,700 per 40 kg since harvest — well above the import parity price. It reflects a significant supply-demand imbalance. All these developments raise serious questions about the credibility of official wheat production estimates, adequacy of strategic reserves, and the overall management of Pakistan’s wheat supply chain. The proposed 1m tonne import has revived farmers’ fears of a repeat of FY24, when imports triggered a price collapse The core issue is that, while complying with international financial institutions’ conditions, Pakistan’s federal and provincial governments pursued a wheat policy that failed to balance the interests of farmers, who produce the crop, and consumers, who seek affordable flour. They abandoned the decades-old public procurement system without first establishing a credible market-based alternative that could ensure fair returns for farmers, market stability, and adequate strategic reserves. Since Punjab province produces 77 per cent of Pakistan’s wheat, its wheat policy largely shapes the country’s wheat supply chain. Over the last three crops, it adopted three different policies: in 2024, it abruptly stopped wheat procurement from farmers; in 2025, it introduced the Electronic Warehouse Receipt system; and in 2026, it shifted to a private sector-led procurement model by engaging 11 companies. However, none of these delivered the desired results. Meanwhile, despite claiming wheat market deregulation, the Punjab government increasingly relied on administrative interventions — district-specific prices, artificial price caps, raids on stocks held by farmers and private stockists, and restrictions on wheat movement during 2025 and 2026. These measures, aimed at keeping wheat flour affordable for urban consumers, may have temporarily contained prices, but they did so at the expense of farmers by distorting market incentives. The question now is: why has this supply-demand mismatch emerged barely three months after harvest? The answer partly lies in the lower yields and changing behaviour of market participants. After recurring financial losses, farmers cut back on costly phosphate fertilisers. Moreover, heat waves and crop lodging at the maturity stage adversely affected the crop. Consequently, yields declined by an estimated three to five maunds per acre compared with the previous year’s crop. Similarly, on the supply side, learning from last year’s experience, many farmers who could meet their immediate financial needs chose to retain part of their wheat stocks. While individual holdings may be small, the cumulative effect of millions of farmers retaining just a few bags extra translates into millions of tonnes. Likewise, a large number of urban consumers, who usually purchase wheat for a few months’ consumption during harvest, appear to have stocked more this year. Concerns over the Iran conflict, rising world energy prices, and disruptions in global fertiliser supplies fuelled fears of global food inflation. Another important market development has been the growing presence of private stockists in agricultural commodity markets as investors have diverted capital from manufacturing, services, and real estate toward commodity trading. Unlike most farmers, these investors — many of them politically well-connected — have the financial capacity to hold stocks for extended periods. Together, these market dynamics have reduced market supplies and tightened wheat availability. Against this broader backdrop, the central question is: what should Pakistan’s wheat policy be for the next crop? Farmers, already on the verge of financial collapse, are demanding only a harvest-time price at least equal to import parity to offset soaring costs of diesel, electricity, fertilisers, and pesticides. Balancing this legitimate demand while safeguarding consumers’ interests calls for a fundamental rethink of wheat policy. The following three reforms are proposed as a starting point for a broader national debate. First, instead of announcing a support or indicative price, the government should allow unrestricted wheat exports during the harvest time while removing inter-provincial movement restrictions. This would automatically align domestic prices with international markets. Once this happens, exports would naturally become financially unviable for exporters. Second, the government should build and maintain strategic wheat reserves of at least 4m tonnes. These must be procured and released at prevailing market prices rather than under the current practice of administratively fixed rates. These reserves should serve as a market stabilisation tool, released whenever domestic prices rise above import parity levels. If international prices rise sharply, low-income households should be protected through targeted subsidies instead of suppressing farmers’ prices. Third, in a deregulated market, the government should recognise the legitimate role of private stockists — as in the rice and maize markets — rather than branding them as hoarders. By absorbing surplus wheat during harvest, they perform an essential supply-chain function and provide farmers with an alternative marketing channel that reduces the risk of exploitation. However, the government should promote a broad-based network of stockists to prevent excessive market concentration. In conclusion, the proposed 1m-tonne import has revived farmers’ fears of a repeat of FY24, when imports of 3.59m tonnes triggered a collapse in wheat prices. It is high time to formulate a wheat policy through public debate and consensus among all stakeholders, rather than allowing the whims of a few to shape decisions that create opportunities for vested interests and ultimately harm both farmers and consumers. Agriculture: Water’s cycle of debt and poverty

Pakistan today faces a double-barreled water emergency: first, the drying up of surface storage; and second, the relentless depletion of groundwater resources. The country is frequently cited as facing severe water stress, with an estimated storage capacity of only about 30 days compared with an international benchmark of 120 days. In March 2025, Tarbela and Mangla reservoirs fell to or near dead level, and Sindh faced about a 50 per cent water shortage at the start of the kharif season; the Indus River System Authority described depletion to dead levels as a common or usual occurrence in that season. Geopolitical treaty tussles aside, a major challenge in Pakistan’s water sector is that within its own borders, institutional paralysis, siltation of the dams, and huge losses in conveyance are sucking the country’s freshwater into the sea. Nasa’s Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment identifies the Indus Basin as one of the world’s most stressed aquifers, and the rate of depletion is fast, particularly in central Punjab, due to unchecked extraction, climate stress, and failure of regulatory bodies. Pakistan’s vanishing water buffer has resulted in storage gaps and aquifer depletion that impacts small farm holders the most The impact is overwhelming. Driving aquifer depletion places smallholders in a cycle of debt and poverty, and their forced dependence on heavily polluted groundwater leads to chronic illness, child mortality, and the erosion of human capital. Agriculture, which contributes almost 24pc to Pakistan’s GDP and employs more than one-third of the workforce, is the primary driver of water stress, fueled by inefficient canal networks and over 1.3 million unregulated tube wells. Pakistan’s agricultural economy is based on suboptimal and unregulated pumping, which is leading to unsustainable aquifer depletion and aggravating the country’s critical storage problem, making it uncompetitive in the international market. The Pakistan Institute of Development Economics’ research shows that the industry’s demand for water rose by 20pc in the past decade, particularly in the textile, tannery, chemical, and cement sectors. Over 90pc of the industrial plants in Punjab and Sindh operate without regulation, accelerating the rate of groundwater depletion and saline intrusion into important freshwater aquifers. The qualitative deterioration of the country’s water supply is severe, as only a small proportion of wastewater is recycled, and industrial hubs such as Karachi, Faisalabad, and Lahore dump raw chemicals, dyes, and toxic waste into water bodies, thereby constantly polluting domestic groundwater. Without treatment, these toxins can percolate down into the underlying aquifer and directly contaminate the drinking water source for millions of people. The domestic sector accounts for only 4–5pc of total freshwater withdrawals, yet some cities face water shortages; however, governance failures and uncontrolled aquifer mining have direct impacts on municipal access. Consequently, women and young girls suffer from water poverty, spending countless hours walking long distances in rural and peri-urban areas, giving up education, health, and economic independence. The lack of trust and water disputes between provinces undermine national cohesion. Trust among the provinces in the Indus Basin has been fractured by disputed formulas for sharing scarce water and unreliable telemetry data. The reduction in freshwater availability due to climate stress is causing ecological collapse downstream of Kotri, with effects on agricultural GDP, industrial development, and national cohesion, leading to bitter political conflict with the upstream provinces. Stronger provincial environmental protection agencies, backed by strong political will, with clear timelines and dedicated funding, are also critical components of a stronger national and provincial water framework to address policy gaps related to weak targets, neglected water quality, and poor integration with the Sustainable Development Goals. Pakistan also has a plethora of policy documents, such as the National Water Policy (2018) and the provincial water policies (the Punjab Water Act 2019 and the Balochistan Groundwater Ordinance), which provide clear guidelines on water storage, groundwater regulation, and efficient irrigation. However, performance remains poor. If a water security task force is unable to carry out six key imperatives — increasing storage capacity, improving efficiency, managing groundwater, implementing digital telemetry, strengthening climate resilience, and centralising national funding — these policies are unlikely to deliver results. Sustained investment and planning have resulted in higher levels of reserves in other countries, such as Australia (600 days) and Morocco (700 days); Pakistan remains constrained by decades of underinvestment in storage, irrigation modernisation, and wastewater treatment infrastructure. To solve Pakistan’s water problem, there is a need for comprehensive reforms to groundwater recharge, the efficiency of water storage systems, and strong water management policies. The solution to Pakistan’s water crisis requires a combination of policy instruments, such as volumetric pricing of groundwater, non-compliance fines for auditing water-consuming sectors, and subsidised adoption of drip irrigation, and water infrastructure investments, such as small delay-action dams and co-financed industrial effluent treatment plants. Strategic public–private partnerships among industry, donors, and civil society are also critical to rapidly scaling up the use of high-efficiency water technologies. Extensive water depletion and mismanagement not only deplete Pakistan’s aquifers but also cause the loss of flood runoff. Aquifer recharge must be achieved on a large scale. In the short term, farming efficiency has to be improved and surface storage enhanced to secure the nation’s water future. Water is life, but unchecked abstraction and inadequate storage are a direct threat to Pakistan’s survival. However, with good governance, judicious investment and political will, Pakistan can still ensure its hydrological future—because the cost of inaction outweighs the cost of reform. As part of their two-stage strategy, terrorists first strike a police installation and engage personnel deployed inside the facility. At the same time, they deploy another team along possible reinforcement routes. As additional forces move in, they become vulnerable to at least three threats: roadside explosions, ambushes and loitering quadcopters dropping munitions. The dispatched party gets little time to respond and often ends up suffering heavy losses. Repeated ambushes of police teams rushing to defend attacked posts highlight how predictable response patterns are turning reinforcements into targets During the past three years, at least 16 police personnel, including three deputy superintendents of police, have fallen victim to this ‘two-stage’ strategy, where additional forces dispatched to assist under-fire personnel end up coming under attack themselves. That attack marked the third such incident, and all three attacks showed that the heaviest losses were suffered not in the initial strike, but during the subsequent attack on reinforcements. The first such incident took place on Jan 15, 2023, when DSP Sardar Hussain, along with his two gunmen, was martyred in an improvised explosive device (IED) explosion while responding to a terrorist attack on the Sarband police station on the outskirts of Peshawar. The official also revealed that the district police officer had been constantly warning the dispatched party not to move towards the checkpost, but the party did not follow the orders and ended up suffering heavy losses despite carrying anti-drone guns. “Creating departments within departments will never help overcome the enemy until these daily parades are replaced with modern warfare training,” the official said. It said that in some instances, police personnel suffered losses while moving as reinforcements towards an attacked installation. The CPO said there were numerous cases where, keeping in view the possibility of an ambush, reinforcements were not moved blindly. It added that the situation was assessed, routes were checked and necessary precautions were taken before moving the force, but such successful responses usually did not get highlighted. “Drones are also being employed to clear and monitor routes before movement of reinforcements, particularly during night operations where such aerial surveillance is a must,” it said. “Our strategy is being continuously reviewed and improved in light of emerging tactics and operational experience, and the force is being guided accordingly to deal with such situations,” it added. The economy Pakistan forgot to measure

Pakistan’s economic discussion centres around what is measured. However, one of the nation’s most significant productive sectors remains mostly unnoticed, not due to a lack of importance, but rather because it is unpaid. Every morning, well before the offices open and the markets buzz with activity, countless individuals in Pakistan are already engaged in their work. Some dairy animals are milked before dawn. Some individuals contribute to the operation of family farms, shops, workshops, and small businesses without earning a wage. Simultaneously, countless individuals engage in meal preparation, home cleaning, childcare, elder care, water collection, animal husbandry, and a myriad of other responsibilities that ensure the smooth operation of households. Before the official start of the formal economy, a significant amount of productive work has already taken place. However, a significant portion of this effort is not reflected in Pakistan’s economic data. GDP growth, exports, investment, and employment are the main topics of discussion in Pakistan’s economy. These signs are definitely important, but they only provide a portion of the picture. They mostly record work that is officially recognised and paid for. They fail to consider a much more important issue: what happens to productive work when no wage changes hands? If unpaid market and domestic work are considered together, nearly 78pc of women are engaged in productive activity The answer reveals an economy that is both vast and remarkably diverse. Two different types of unpaid labour make up Pakistan’s hidden economy. Unpaid market labour is the first. This includes those who labour in household businesses, retail stores, livestock operations, family farms, and workshops without getting paid directly. Because they are unpaid family workers who contribute to the production of goods and services, their contributions are sometimes overlooked in economic debate. According to the 2024 Labour Force Survey (LFS), 5.9 million men and almost 8.8m women work in the unpaid market. In actuality, nearly half of working women support their families without being paid. Despite not being listed on a payroll, these workers are unquestionably a part of Pakistan’s productive economy. The second component is unpaid non-market work. Unlike unpaid market work, these activities are performed within households for the direct benefit of family members rather than for sale in the market. The Labour Force Survey recognises 14 categories of such activities, including cooking, cleaning, childcare, caring for elderly family members, collecting water, livestock care, and other household responsibilities. These services are indispensable to family well-being and labour productivity. If households did not provide them, someone would have to be hired to perform the same tasks. Yet because they take place within the home, they are rarely viewed as part of the economy. The study by the Pakistan Institute of Development Economics (Pide) reveals that although three-fourths of working-age women are outside formal economic activities, 76 per cent continue to perform non-market domestic work every week. They are at work but not counted. Women in Pakistan spend two to 10 times as many hours as men on various non-market domestic activities, specifically 2.1 times more on care work and nine times more on cooking, cleaning, and household management. Together, these two forms of unpaid work challenge one of the most common assumptions about Pakistan’s labour market that women do not work. The evidence tells a different story: once unpaid market work and unpaid domestic work are considered together, nearly 78pc of women are engaged in productive activity. Women are not absent from work; rather, much of their work falls outside conventional definitions of employment. This also sheds light on one of Pakistan’s enduring policy enigmas. If women are becoming more educated, why is women’s participation in the workforce still among the lowest in the area? The response is not merely a matter of unwillingness to engage in work. For many women, the burden of unpaid duties in the home and family businesses often leaves little time or flexibility to engage in paid work. Marriage and raising children significantly increase the burden of unpaid caregiving duties, especially during the peak years of one’s career. The issue at hand is not women’s participation in the workforce, but rather that a significant portion of their contributions goes unpaid, unacknowledged, and consequently overlooked in economic policy discussions. There are costs associated with ignoring this hidden economy. When unpaid labour is excluded from economic assessments, its significance is overlooked in policy considerations. Family businesses that depend on unpaid work continue to be neglected in productivity initiatives. The significant weight of unpaid care work is regarded as a personal household issue rather than an economic constraint. As a result, funding for childcare, rural support services, reliable public transport, water supply, and efficient infrastructure is frequently seen as merely social welfare initiatives rather than strategies that can enhance labour productivity and contribute to economic growth. Recognising this hidden economy does not mean redefining economics; rather, it means enhancing the methods of economic measurement. Pakistan should start by consistently integrating the significance of unpaid work into satellite national accounts, allowing policymakers to grasp the complete extent of productive activity. Labour statistics need to more clearly differentiate unpaid market workers, making sure that contributing family workers are recognised in employment, agricultural, and enterprise policies. Simultaneously, alleviating the strain of unpaid caregiving through accessible childcare, enhanced rural infrastructure, dependable water access, and adaptable work options would empower more women to engage in the workforce, while also recognising the economic significance of their existing contributions. Economic policy relies heavily on the quality of the evidence that supports it. When millions of people contribute to production without receiving a wage, their work does not become economically irrelevant; it simply becomes statistically invisible. Pakistan cannot hope to formulate effective labour, productivity and growth policies while ignoring a sector worth one-third of its economy. The economy Pakistan forgot to measure has always existed. The challenge now is to ensure that it is finally recognised, valued and reflected in the country’s economic decisions.
