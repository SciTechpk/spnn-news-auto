Makkah moment

THERE is no disputing that the coming together of Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye comes at a critical juncture for West Asia, even if several aspects around the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement’s precise operationalisation remain unclear. There are good reasons for Islamabad to view this as a significant strategic opportunity. The arrangement, forged in the shadow of the US and Israeli war on Iran, responds to a growing imperative for regional stakeholders to exercise greater control in stabilising a region fractured by conflict. It also builds on long-standing relationships between three important middle powers whose security is bound up in regional outcomes. Pakistan and Saudi Arabia have decades of military and diplomatic cooperation behind them. Turkiye has important extensive defence ties with both. With US retrenchment from the region increasingly appearing inevitable, institutionalising these relationships offers some much-needed strategic coherence to a region beset by complex challenges. But for Pakistan to make the most of this moment — and for it to translate the compact into lasting strategic advantage — several caveats deserve consideration. First, for this ‘non-directional’ arrangement to truly be regionally tenable, it must avoid becoming a source of further division in the Middle East. For Pakistan, the present crisis involving Ansarullah (the Houthis) in the Red Sea offers an early test of whether the alliance can resist being drawn into the region’s existing rivalries. There is no disputing the fact that the stability of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia matters enormously to Pakistan: it is an important source of energy and financial support, and home to a large Pakistani diaspora whose remittances contribute significantly to foreign exchange earnings. The strategic opportunity comes with caveats. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has also dramatically increased Pakistan’s dependency on the flow of oil through the Red Sea. But direct military involvement against the Houthis does risk drawing the alliance deeper into existing regional rivalries, risking Islamabad’s position with Tehran, and testing Pakistan’s stated policy commitment to avoiding camp politics. Ultimately, Pakistan’s usefulness to the alliance might lie in its ability to exploit the diplomatic space it retains with both Tehran and Riyadh, using those channels to encourage dialogue and de-escalation. Second, in terms of objectives, military coordination, defence-industrial cooperation and, ultimately, collective defence are not incompatible with one another. But it is that final commitment, however, that will require the most careful thinking about where Pakistan’s obligations to the other two countries begin and end. A decade ago, in 2015, Pakistan’s parliament played an important role in the decision not to commit Pakistani troops to the Saudi-led war in Yemen. While the intervening decade has arguably seen an erosion of Pakistan’s democratic institutions, the country’s leaders must still remember that major commitments abroad still require political dialogue and consensus at home, not least given the country’s demographic diversity and the ability of external security choices to reverberate internally. Finally, and relatedly, those shaping Pakistan’s strategic direction would be wise to recognise that Pakistan’s expanding strategic horizons cannot be divorced from the pressures it confronts at home and that while there is no arguing that regional influence can be an asset, it is important to build the domestic capacity to sustain. At present, Pakistan confronts a serious trifecta of terrorism, political instability and persistent economic fr­­agility. This year has been among Pakis­tan’s deadliest for terrorism in recent memory. Fraught go­­vernance challe­nges in KP and Ba­­lochistan add to that insecurity. For a country already contending with multiple sources of instability at home, expanding commitments abroad without thought to improved governance and/or a corresponding domestic reset at home risks weakening the very foundations on which any newfound regional influence must ultimately rest. None of these caveats detracts from the rare opportunity Makkah presents. In the shadow of looming threats from India and Afghanistan, and general instability unleashed by America’s war in Iran, the arrangement offers Pakistan the chance to do several things: to extract strategic currency from its military capacity, to expand defence-industrial cooperation with important partners, and to eventually be a consequential voice in shaping whatever security architecture emerges from the ashes of the US war against Iran. But these things also require more than aspiration. For Islamabad, that means navigating existing regional fault lines and developing a better political compact at home. However, the country may require waivers from the IMF executive board for slippages on structural benchmarks. The finance ministry said announcement of the strategic plan for the LCBM by Sept 30 was req­uired under the IMF programme. The plan sets out reforms the government, regulators and market institutions will undertake to build a deeper, more liquid and resilient market for government and, over time, corporate securities denominated in Pakistani rupees. It is based on a joint IMF-World Bank diagnostic study of Pakistan’s LCBM. Commercial banks currently hold 78 per cent of government securities, while sovereign paper accounts for about 62pc of banking-system assets. This concentration supports government securities auctions but favours holding securities over trading them, while limiting banks’ capacity and incentives to finance the private sector. The joint study noted that Pakistan had built much of the institutional framework of an emerging LCBM, but market outcomes remained closer to those of a developing market. Development was uneven across six pillars, with money market and financial infrastructure foundations comparatively advanced, while primary-market predictability, secondary-market liquidity and the legal and regulatory framework remained below the practices of larger emerging markets. The narrow investor base was identified as the biggest gap. Therefore, the plan seeks to develop a liquid, transparent and diversified LCBM to reduce the cost and risk of government financing over the medium term, support effective monetary-policy transmission and provide a reliable benchmark yield curve for private-sector financing. It pursues five strategic objectives, including strengthening institutional capacity and coordination, ensuring clear ownership and accountability, and making primary issuance more predictable and market-based through a published benchmark policy and a well-informed medium-term debt strategy. It also seeks to build executable secondary-market liquidity and a functioning private securities-finan­cing (or repo) market, broaden the investor base across institutional, retail and foreign investors, modernise market infrastructure and remove legal and tax impediments to trading and secured funding. Under the plan, eligible bank customers would be allowed to trade exchange-listed government securities through their banks under the supervision of State Bank, Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan, Pakistan Stock Exchange and Central Depository Company. The Ministry of Finance and SBP would also review the primary dealer framework for the fiscal year 2027-28 so that secondary-market performance, including quote performance drawn from E-Bond, is taken into account. The plan envisages a securities-lending facility for primary dealers, covering its operating model, eligible securities, risk controls and fiscal implications.
