The US & India

A CRITICAL look at US-India relations is essential to understand US-Pakistan ties. It might help dispel a long-held public perception in Pakistan that US-India relations have somehow been a function of the ups and downs in US-Pakistan ties. And that in establishing the extraordinary relationship with India in the last couple of decades Washington has finally “chosen” India over Pakistan. The foreign policy establishment acknowledges the cyclic nature of US-Pakistan relations. But uplifted by the recent fissures in US-India ties, charmed by President Trump’s compliments, infused by an enduring faith in the US-Pakistan alliance, and confident of Pakistan’s coveted geopolitical salience, it remains ever optimistic that Washington would come back, and relations would be rekindled to their “halcyon” days. Both the public and official views need revision, based as they are on faulty assumptions that consider the two relationships as competitive and comparable. That is why one keeps hearing successive Pakistan leaders asking the US to treat the two countries equally, and arguing at home that Pakistan needs to keep a balance in relations with China and the US. This is an erroneous view. Indians forget Washington’s critical help in many areas. The problem is Pakistan cannot wean itself away from the pride of having been a preferred ally of the US once upon a time. But this notion refers to a world of the Cold War days in which the US had a single interest in South Asia, which Pakistan was capable and India was unwilling to advance. That South Asia is no more. There is a new world out there. Its complexity and disorder present a multiplicity of security and strategic challenges, its globalised economy offers exceptional opportunities, and its uncertainty compels countries to hedge, expand, and diversify their partnerships. In this world, India and Pakistan would both have roles for the US, though relations with Pakistan would be transactional and of limited bandwidth, and with India strategic and broad-based. These are two different relations, and neither derives from the strength or weakness of the other, or is based on “choosing” one over the other. Coming to US-India ties, beneath the trade dispute and H1-B visa frictions US-India strategic cooperation and economic relations continue to thrive. There is substantial collaboration on defence production, space, AI, and maritime security. In a recent piece, Sameer Lalwani of the Carnegie Endowment pointed out that trade rose to its highest level of $240 billion last year. And the new 10-year framework for the US-India Major Defence Partnership calls for advanced exercises and closer defence-industrial ties. Major US tech companies pledged nearly $68bn towards AI infrastructure investments in India last year. Micron, a leading semiconductor firm, opened its first assembly and test facility in India early this year. And recently Applied Materials and Lam Research announced investments in India’s semiconductor industry. Prime Minister Modi bruised President Trump’s ego by not acknowledging his role in the 2025 India-Pakistan ceasefire. But this has not impacted the substance of relations. Trump has not reversed ties; he has simply put a brake on them. As for tariffs, these have indeed caused strains but they are not India-specific; they are being applied across the board as Trump’s economic instrument and geopolitical weapon. The reality is he would not allow countries to become economic competitors and geopolitical rivals with America’s help and at its expense. US Deputy Assistant Secretary of State Christopher Landau said in his speech at the Rai­sina Dialogue ear­­ly this year that the US would not repeat mistakes made with China with India. India was so pa­­­mpered by Pre­­sident Biden and Trump in his first term that it came to have a sense of entitlement. Now it is complaining about losing its extra-special status. Indians forget Washington’s critical help in many areas. The US, for instance, has long supported Indian indigenous technology development. GE engines have been central to India’s domestic fighter jet industry. India’s first indigenous fighter aircraft Tejas relied on an F-16 testbed, and its development of a fifth-generation fighter relies on US wind tunnels. From Pakistan’s standpoint, what is im­­p­ortant is that US-India differences have not impinged on India’s importance for Wash­in­­gton in the US-China strategic competition. And it will likely endure. Any occasi­o­nal adjustment in it will not bring dividends for US-Pakistan ties. We need to delink the two relationships in our analysis. The bottom line: the US will continue to engage both India and Pakistan as there are no alternatives to each relationship. The alternative to India is India, and the alternative to Pakistan remains Pakistan. The writer, a former ambassador, is adjunct professor at Georgetown University. Problems and prescriptions

THE upward trajectory of the Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) reading, which clocked in at 110 in October 2026, is a frequent subject of debate among economic commentators. Some argue that it shows the currency is being ‘artificially managed’, as was the case in past such episodes which usually preceded painful adjustments. Others have pointed out that the REER is just one indicator among several to assess the position of an economy’s health. An excellent piece by Dr Ishrat Husain on these pages lays out the latter’s position quite categorically: “REER has a legitimate but limited purpose. It measures changes in the nominal exchange rate adjusted for relative price movements against trading partners. But an index reading of 100, based on a selected base year, doesn’t establish an optimal exchange rate. Nor does a deviation from 100 automatically signal overvaluation or undervaluation. REER says little about balance-of-payments sustainability, foreign exchange availability from non-trade sources or exporters’ ability to compete through quality, technology, productivity, and delivery.” Knowledgeable experts will continue to weigh in on this issue, but it is worth looking at the exchange rate from the economy’s current vantage point. A minor current account surplus, a primary (fiscal) surplus, monthly central bank purchases, not sales, of dollars from the open market, increasing reserves, and a very narrow spread between the interbank and open market rate suggest there is nothing immediately scandalous about the current rate. The PKR’s value is where it is because the inflow of dollars has been robust on account of some export growth plus significant remittance inflows, positive real interest rates, selective administrative checks on imports, and the dollar’s own position against other currencies. FDI’s future looks uncertain under geopolitical fracturing and tariffs are loudly back on the menu. Taking a slightly broader timeframe, what is also clear is that at its existing level of productivity the Pakistani economy’s current rate of sustainable growth — ie growth that doesn’t blow up the external sector and/or cause domestic inflation — is around 4pc. Of this, 2.5pc is attributable to natural population growth, and the remaining is what the economy contributes by way of actual productivity. It is with this bleak baseline condition that many experts push for a gradual, enforced devaluation of the currency as a policy position. The argument here is that this downward flexibility is an important ingredient, among others, for export competitiveness. Given the government’s stated position of catalysing export-led growth, enforced depreciation along with import liberalisation appears to be the logical path to take. This prescription can be found with near-universal frequency in government documents, multilateral donor/lender documents, and in expert/technocratic commentary at any point over the last two decades. The fact that it is never acted upon in full is a political economy question. The government, in practice, prioritises some societal segments over others (importers over exporters), picks some objectives over others at different times (stabilisation versus growth), and chooses to inflict pain on some rather than others (who gets rents versus whose subsidies get taken away). But there is another cloud looming on the horizon of this prescription. What if the country has missed the boat of beneficial global trade integration altogether? The strategy of economic development of much of the Third World/Global South since the mid-20th century has gone through three phases: protectionist import-substituting industrialisation from the 1950s to the 1970s; structural adjustment and market-led liberalisation (starting in the 1980s); and dreams of integrating into global value chains (GVCs) through export-led strategies (from the 1990s onwards). Pakistan repeatedly pays homage to the third approach, but success remains limited at best. It has achieved reasonable integration in textiles and a few other sectors, but has little else to boast about. The wide range of conflicts that characterise its political economy have constrained baseline productivity to such a large extent that the country possesses or makes little of note that the world wants. Its natural advantage in cheap labour has been undone by poor human capital formation and other burdens, such as its energy sector and struggles with political violence. As a result, its industrial base remains shallow and growth remains anaemic. In the current moment, the prospects for favourable integration look even dimmer. If AI and robotics development continues at pace, the marginal cost of labour across the world will fall further. Why then would integrating Pakistan’s cheap and mostly unskilled labour into any value chain, at any level of sophistication, make sense? Bluntly put, where the world produces the things it consumes has already been decided to a very large extent. China is the factory of the world, and those economies functionally integrated with China’s factors (its South-East Asian partners) have a future in global trade. The rest of the developing world mostly does not. Simultaneously, we’re witnessing increased insistence on new domestically oriented industrial policies, which prioritise re-industrialisation and on-shoring. FDI’s future looks uncertain under geopolitical fracturing and tariffs are loudly back on the menu of policy options to protect industrial employment. This means that an emerging multipolar world order might see economic delinking rather than greater integration. All of this is to say that while the benefits of export-led growth are well known, the world in which export-led growth is possible for Pakistan may no longer exist. If this admittedly pessimistic reading is correct, does our current conversation have any space for a different type of economic strategy to shake off this anaemic 4pc per annum baseline? Do we, in effect, have a backup answer for how Pakistani labour and Pakistani capital can be productively combined to generate employment, reduce poverty, and improve human flourishing? A lopsided arrangement for KP?

• Since the imposition of governor’s rule, KP has committed Rs157bn a year to the Centre, a contribution the ousted PTI government had withheld • While Rs17.7bn has already been deducted from KP’s NFC share, the PM’s recently announced Rs160bn package for the province has yet to take shape ONE of the most significant developments during the first 43 days of emergency and governor’s rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa was the signing of a memorandum of understanding (MoU) with the federal government, which commits the province to an estimated annual cash grant of Rs157bn. The amount forms part of the Rs1.035 trillion in financial contributions sought by the federal government from the four provinces for national economic initiatives. Islamabad had been pressing for these contributions, but the dysfunctional chief minister, Sohail Afridi, had made KP’s contribution conditional on a meeting with his imprisoned party founder Imran Khan – a demand the federal government refused to entertain. The interim administration’s signing of the MoU has effectively provided formal cover for Rs17.7bn already deducted at source by the federal government from KP’s share under the National Finance Commission (NFC) award during the July-October 2026 period. With the agreement now in place, the federal government has secured KP’s contribution for the current fiscal year, marking one of the major financial outcomes of governor’s rule. While the province’s contribution to the Centre has been settled, little is clear about what flows the other way: the details of the prime minister’s Rs160bn package for KP, the funding gap in the merged districts and how federal resources will be divided between the two administrative structures created under governor’s rule. The Rs160bn question Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif announced Rs160bn for the province on Oct 22, with 70pc earmarked for the Area Development Secretariat (ADS), the new administrative unit for 12 districts. A senior official of the KP Finance Department said the provincial government had since received no formal word, nor any details regarding the proposed package. It remains unclear whether the package represents fresh funding or includes the Rs150bn already allocated for the newly merged districts (NMDs) in FY2026-27. The mechanism for releasing and distributing the funds has also not been explained. Adviser on Information Samar Haroon Bilour said 70pc of the announced resources would go to the 12 ADS districts and the remaining 30pc to the other 24 districts. But instead of providing additional financial assistance, the official claimed, the federal government had deducted more than Rs17bn from the province’s NFC share within four months. He maintained that KP had already generated a fiscal surplus in line with commitments made under the IMF programme and that, despite the deductions, the province had faced no significant pressure on the cash flows required to meet its current expenditure. “This shows that KP has maintained fiscal discipline and is not facing any shortage of funds,” the official said. Parallel set-ups? On Oct 30, the KP government issued a notification transferring the functions of designated officers to the ADS, while financial authority remained with the provincial Finance Department. The 12 districts have been placed under an additional chief secretary (ACS) acting as administrator, while the remaining 24 fall under Governor Faisal Kareem Kundi and his 11-member advisory council. Despite the creation of two parallel administrative structures, there is little clarity on how federal funds will be allocated between them. Under the five-page notification, the KP Human Resource Management Department transferred administrative control over designated officers and staff of 32 directorates and major institutions to the ADS. These officers now report to the respective ADS secretaries. However, the arrangement falls short of establishing a fully autonomous administration, as no independent directorates have been created, while the provincial chief secretary and Human Resource Management Department retain key powers over personnel transfers. Financial arrangements also remain under the KP Finance Department, which will handle budget allocations and financial authorisations in consultation with the ADS. The notification thus separates administrative functions without fully transferring financial, legal and personnel authority to the new secretariat. Former KP finance adviser Muzzammil Aslam, who oversaw the provincial Finance Department for two and a half years, said the status of the new administrative unit was still unclear. “If it is under federal government control, then definitely the provincial financial management needs adjustment,” he said. Former KP finance minister Taimur Jhagra said appointing an ACS and establishing a separate secretariat would make little difference, particularly when the new secretariat was based in Peshawar rather than in the area it was meant to administer. He said Prime Minister Shehbaz had still given no clarity on whether he was investing a single extra rupee of federal money in KP, even in the name of governor’s rule. Former KP home and tribal affairs secretary Dr Akhtar Ali Shah questioned the rationale for creating a parallel administrative structure, recalling that the federal government had exercised direct control over the tribal areas through designated officials under the Frontier Crimes Regulation (FCR), yet the law and order situation had continued to deteriorate. “It is well documented that a political agent in Waziristan could not even travel by car,” he said, referring to the security situation in 2006, when a parallel system of authority had emerged. Dr Shah questioned how another administrative structure would improve security when direct federal control had failed to prevent the killing of tribal chiefs. “If history is any guide, then listen to it,” he said, describing the new arrangement as an attempt to reinvent the wheel. The ousted PTI government had also claimed to have initiated work in June 2024, during the tenure of former chief minister Ali Amin Gandapur, on a separate administrative secretariat covering 11 districts across Kohat, Bannu and Dera Ismail Khan divisions, including the relevant merged districts. Ms Bilour credited Prime Minister Shehbaz with establishing the ADS. She acknowledged that the concept may have originated under the PTI government but maintained that the prime minister had implemented it. Transfers and liabilities The administrative division has brought KP’s unresolved financial arrangements into sharper focus. Since the 2018 merger, the province has maintained separate budgetary provisions for settled districts and the NMDs, whose expenditure continues to depend heavily on federal transfers. For FY2026-27, the federal government allocated Rs95bn for current expenditure and Rs55bn for development under the Annual Development Programme (ADP) and Accelerated Implementation Programme (AIP), totalling Rs150bn. However, the financing shortfall is estimated at Rs80bn to Rs100bn, which the province will meet from resources allocated to settled districts, a senior KP Finance Department official told Dawn. The official said development allocations for the NMDs declined from Rs69bn in FY24 to Rs52bn in FY25 before rising to Rs55bn in FY26. Current expenditure allocations increased from Rs72bn in FY25 to Rs80bn in FY26 and Rs95bn in FY27. According to the official, the NMDs’ estimated 2.5pc share under the NFC arrangement would amount to around Rs300bn. However, the absence of a dedicated allocation had deprived the tribal districts of these resources, a grievance repeatedly raised with Islamabad. Dr Shah argued that while security operations could restore the writ of the state, adequate fiscal transfers were necessary to strengthen state capacity. According to him, KP’s existing 14.62pc share under the NFC award was determined before the merger, when the population, area, poverty and development needs of the former tribal districts were not included in the province’s calculations. He said the province’s share should have increased to 19.46pc following the merger, but the additional resources had not been provided. Ms Bilour said the NMDs’ financial share and outstanding federal liabilities were discussed during the prime minister’s meeting at Governor House, where he directed the formation of federal and provincial committees to reconcile the claims. Mr Jhagra, however, doubted that the province would benefit. He argued that KP already contributed to the federation in four ways. “The rest of Pakistan benefits from our cheap hydro-energy, and from our oil and gas. Then, KP is leaving a Rs220 billion surplus. Then, it is giving Rs130bn on top of that to the Centre by force, for strategic spending,” he said. He added that, with a reduced NFC share based on the 1998 census, the Centre used KP’s NFC share to finance current and development expenditure in ex-Fata areas. “The federal grants for ex-Fata are short of current expenditure alone by Rs50bn. Shehbaz Sharif has solved none of this. So how will anything change?” he asked. Mr Jhagra also called for the restoration of trade with Afghanistan and Central Asia, saying it was in the interests of Pakistan and particularly of KP. According to Mr Aslam, KP currently receives 14.62pc of the divisible pool, 1pc for the war on terror and Rs150bn (current and development) for the merged areas from the federal government, and is expected to generate Rs182bn from its own sources. “Any deviation or amount withheld of federal resources will definitely change the budgetary estimates. The current NFC will be under question as well,” he said, adding that the merged areas had been deprived of an NFC share since the merger. On the IMF programme, Mr Aslam said it closely tracks provincial spending on health and education and, on the revenue side, the agricultural income tax. “So definitely, a lot of federal and provincial deliberation [is] required to ensure programme compliance,” he said. He added that resource allocation needed political consensus within KP, which would require legislation through a functioning provincial assembly. Revenue distribution Another unresolved issue concerns the distribution of provincial revenues, particularly oil and gas royalties and mineral receipts. A Finance Department official said a substantial share of KP’s oil and gas royalties originated from Kohat and Karak, while mineral royalties generated around Rs16bn, largely from the NMDs and Malakand region. The question is whether the ADS will receive a defined share of these revenues or continue to depend on transfers through the provincial financial system. Financial experts warned that dividing administrative authority without clearly defining revenue and expenditure responsibilities could complicate budget preparation and execution. Officials fear that reconciling existing liabilities, revenue entitlements and spending obligations will take time, potentially affecting development projects, public services and provincial finances. With Islamabad already facing fiscal constraints, it remains uncertain whether the federal government can sustain the new arrangement through additional funding or whether the burden will continue to fall on KP’s existing resources. Security spending: charge sheet against PTI The charge sheet against the ousted PTI government was lengthy, but three allegations stood out: failure to act against undocumented Afghan nationals, inadequate funding for security operations and persistent governance problems in the former tribal districts. However, official financial records and subsequent administrative decisions raise questions about the grounds for federal intervention. A Finance Department official said KP had provided Rs31bn for temporarily displaced persons (TDPs) in four districts over the past two years. Until 2022, Islamabad had provided Rs17bn annually for TDPs before discontinuing the allocation. Since 2024, the province has also been spending Rs400m monthly on displaced families living in camps at Bakakhel, the official said. Finance Department documents put KP’s security budget at Rs191bn, or approximately 8.8pc of the provincial budget. “The federal government criticises the KP government for not supporting security operations in the province,” the official said, arguing that expenditure on security and displaced families presented a different picture. Dr Shah, who also served as additional inspector general of KP police, rejected claims that the provincial government had neglected policing. He said the police force had expanded from 32,000 to 82,000 personnel between 2006 and the launch of Operation Zarb-i-Azb in 2014, and had since increased to 125,000. The expansion, he said, required substantial investment in recruitment, training, equipment and infrastructure. “It is wrong to say that the provincial government has done nothing on the policing front,” he said, pointing to the establishment of new police stations, police lines and the provision of modern weapons. These investments, he added, were financed from provincial resources. Instead of providing the promised financial resources to improve infrastructure and strengthen institutions in the merged districts, he said, the federal government had opted to establish another parallel administrative structure. The governor’s record Apart from the MoU, the governor’s most visible measures so far have included an anti-encroachment campaign in 24 districts and action against rickshaw drivers in Peshawar. Ms Bilour rejected claims that the advisory council had failed to address the allegations. She cited an unprecedented compensation package for police personnel killed in the line of duty, alongside the provision of armoured vehicles, ambulances and other security equipment. Defending the anti-encroachment drive, she said removing illegal structures and improving cleanliness were necessary to restore Peshawar’s appearance, with similar measures planned for other cities. Ms Bilour said the governor’s administration had six months to implement its agenda. Deputy commissioners were directed to set a November 1 deadline for undocumented Afghan nationals to leave voluntarily, and enforcement action against those who remained is continuing. She had also said the Area Representative and Development Council (ARDC) would soon formulate a plan for security operations in the designated districts. PTV asked to disclose details of foreign trips at public expense

ISLAMABAD: The Pakistan Information Commission (PIC) has directed Pakistan Television Corporation (PTV) to disclose details of foreign trips undertaken by its officials at public expense since January 2025, ruling that information about the utilisation of public funds cannot be withheld merely by declaring it confidential or sensitive. Allowing an appeal filed by journalist and Right to Information (RTI) activist Saddia Mazhar, the commission directed PTV’s managing director to provide the complete information sought in her request within 10 days of receiving the order, with a copy to be submitted to the commission. The order was signed by Information Com­m­issioner Ijaz Hassan Awan and Chief Information Commissioner Shoaib Ahmad Siddiqui. Ms Mazhar had submitted an information request on June 21, 2026, seeking details of PTV officials and other officers who had travelled abroad at public expense to cover or attend various events from January 2025 onwards. She specifically sought the names of the officials concerned, the composition of teams deputed for foreign assignments, the nature of their assignments and the expenditure incurred by the government or PTV. Journalist seeks names of officials, assignments, expenditure since January last year However, PTV resisted disclosure, maintaining that the information formed part of the corporation’s official and confidential records. In its written response submitted to the commission on Aug 25, 2026, the state-owned broadcaster argued that disclosure could involve matters relating to official deployments, security considerations, operational arrangements and other sensitive aspects of high-level official engagements. PTV also invoked exemptions under Section 16(d)(ii) and (iii) of the Right of Access to Information Act, 2017, requesting the commission to allow it to withhold the information. The corporation maintained that its officials were deputed on foreign tours for official assignments, including coverage of prime ministerial visits, high-level government engagements, international conferences and other important meetings. It said specific teams were allocated according to the nature and requirements of these engagements. Ms Mazhar challenged PTV’s position, arguing in her rejoinder that the information concerned public expenditure and did not fall within the exemptions invoked by the broadcaster. She maintained that the records constituted public information under the RTI law. After hearing the arguments and examining the available record and relevant legal provisions, the commission rejected PTV’s objections. In its order, the commission observed that the requested information fell within the ambit of Sections 5(g) and 6(b) of the Right of Access to Information Act, 2017, as it related to expenditure incurred on foreign tours by officials from the allocated budget of a public body. “Such information pertains to the utilisation of public funds and, therefore, constitutes public information subject to disclosure under the Act,” the order stated. The commission also rejected PTV’s argument concerning the potential impact of disclosure on its financial interests and commercial activities, observing that the corporation had failed to establish how providing the information would damage its financial interests or adversely affect its lawful commercial activities. It also observed that merely describing information as confidential or sensitive, without establishing the applicability of the relevant exemption under the law, was insufficient grounds for withholding it. OBITUARY: The last of the Mohicans

HUSAIN Naqi was undoubtedly one of the most courageous and outspoken journalists this country ever produced. His honesty, integrity and truthfulness were never in question. Throughout his life, both as a journalist and human rights activist, he remained fully engaged in raising his voice for just causes and underserved communities. Freedom of expression and issues affecting the disenfranchised segments of society were particularly dear to him. His unflinching commitment to advocating people’s causes brought him hardship, unemployment and even imprisonment, but nothing deterred Naqi. Dissent was a duty and compromise was not a word that was part of his dictionary. As the sun rose on Sunday, the news arrived that he was no more. It was a one-line message from his son, Akbar, in Lahore. I must confess I was not prepared for the sad tidings. We had been in regular contact for most of our relationship, but in recent days I had been less proactive about reaching out, mainly due to my own lethargy. Even though Naqi’s championing of just causes brought him personal hardship, compromise was not a word that was part of his dictionary Not Naqi, though. On Saturday, just hours before his death, he attended a book launch organised by the Human Rights Commission of Pakistan (HRCP), meeting fellow journalists, lawyers and human rights activists. Unf­or­tunately, he suffered a heart attack later that evening and passed away in hospital in the early hours of Sunday at the age of 88. A large number of his comrades, journalists, rights defenders, political figures and members of civil society attended his funeral, held at the Jamia Al-Muntazir in Model Town, Lahore. Student politics Born in 1936 into a large household headed by a prominent lawyer in Lucknow, he received his early education in his hometown. His family wanted him to pursue engineering, which took him to Aligarh, but his destiny lay elsewhere. In the mid-1950s he moved to Pakistan, marking the beginning of his long journey of fighting for just causes. After being elected vice president of the Karachi University Students’ Union from the platform of the left-wing National Students Federation, his first confrontation with university authorities came when he took up the cause of victimised students from Balochistan. Dr I.H. Qureshi was the vice chancellor at the time. The ideological differences between the two were sharp, ultimately resulting in his expulsion from the university, which, according to him, was not such a bad deal. He also campaigned for better educational facilities, safe transport for female students, timely examinations and the restoration of student unions. He strongly believed that student unions helped develop democratic attitudes and taught young people to respect differences. Naqi’s involvement in student politics came at a volatile time, when students were agitating against the despotic policies of the state. Conscious of the prevailing political climate, Naqi found himself among contemporaries such as Mairaj Mohammad Khan, Fatehyab Ali Khan, Ali Mukhtar Rizvi, Johar Husain and many others, who would galvanise the country’s politics in later years. Foray into journalism His expulsion from university propelled Naqi towards the world of journalism. His fight against obscurantist forces continued. Having weathered many challenges, he eventually moved to Lahore, where he became deeply involved with the city’s politicians and intellectuals. He joined Pakistan Press International (PPI), eventually becoming its bureau chief, but later parted ways with the organisation rather than compromise his principles. Despite being an urbanite from Oudh, he comfortably adjusted to life in the heartland of Punjab, launching what many consider to be the first proper Punjabi daily newspaper, Sajan. During a long and illustrious career, he remained associated with several newspapers and publications, including The News, Frontier Post, Frontier Guardian, Daily Star etc. Guided by the philosophy that one must continue to fight, even when standing alone, he also brought out Punjab Punch, a weekly newspaper. Naqi was also committed to organising journalists and defending their professional rights. As a trade unionist, he believed that press freedom could not be secured without protecting the rights, dignity and independence of media workers. He remained associated with journalists’ struggles against censorship, restrictions on freedom of expression and the misuse of authority. For young journalists and rights activists, Naqi was more than a senior colleague, he was a mentor who encouraged them to question official narratives, develop independent opinions and never surrender their professional integrity. Sherry Rehman, mourning his loss, recalled how many young journalists had learnt at his desk and walked in rallies under his leadership. She described him as a person who spoke truth to power but never encouraged hatred, instead defending the right to disagree with dignity and respect. Decades of activism After retiring from active journalism, he joined the illustrious I.A. Rehman at the HRCP, where he continued to work energetically for the causes he held dear for around three decades. He played a part in many campaigns for democracy, civil liberties, minority rights, freedom of expression and the protection of vulnerable communities. Even though his act­­ivism brought him imprisonment and pre­ssure from the authorities, it failed to weaken his determination to speak against injustice. In a statement condoling his demise, HRCP summed it up thus: “One of the strongest voices for people’s rights and democracy in Pakistan has gone silent”. Naqi’s demise marks the end of an era. In a sense, he was among the last of a generation of journalists and public intellectuals for whom speaking truth to power was not mer­ely a professional obligation, but a way of life. At a time when independent journalism, freedom of expression and democratic space continue to face challenges, the life and work of Husain Naqi serves as a reminder that these freedoms should not be taken for gra­nted; they have to be defended through courage, commitment and collective struggle. Independent media not as 'independent' as it could be: Mehdi Hasan

• US journalist says news gatekeeping has been outsourced to Silicon Valley• Says every journalist ‘needs to be’ a content creator now DOHA: Journalism has undergone a major transformation since the advent of social media and independent news platforms, bringing both opportunities and challenges. These changes were the focus of a talk titled The New Gatekeepers by British-American journalist Mehdi Hasan at the Doha Film Festival. Drawing on his experience at mainstream outlets including the BBC, CNN, Al Jazeera, Sky News and MSNBC, Hasan discussed the changing media landscape and his move towards independent journalism through his own media company, Zeteo, after his MSNBC show was cancelled. Speaking about news gatekeepers, he said that historically only a handful of people could communicate with mass audiences because billionaires, corporations and governments controlled the technology and resources needed to spread information. That has now changed. “It’s good that we have democratised access to the news. Today anyone in this room can go out with a microphone, camera and a WiFi connection and potentially reach more people than a BBC, CNN or an Al Jazeera journalist,” he said. However, Mr Hasan stressed he was not opposed to gatekeeping itself, arguing that journalism still needed fact-checking and professional standards. “I am still a journalist, not a YouTuber or content creator. I am still on YouTube, but I do fact-check. The problem with the old gatekeepers was not the gates. It was that they were opening the gates to the powerful and shutting them to the powerless.” Citing a Reuters Institute survey, he noted that, for the first time, more people were getting their news from social media than from news websites and apps. “We are already in a brave new world,” he remarked. But the democratisation of information had not eliminated gatekeeping; instead, it had transferred much of that power to Silicon Valley, where technology executives such as Elon Musk and Mark Zuckerberg exercise considerable influence. Hasan described it as “insane” that platforms controlled by Zuckerberg, including WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook and Threads, were used by a substantial proportion of the world’s population, with many relying on them for news. He warned that algorithms could undermine the news business, as had already happened to numerous publishers. Asked whether independent media was truly independent, Hasan acknowledged that it was not as independent as it could be. Future wars

THE four-day military confrontation between India and Pakistan last year and the US-Iran war this year have revealed how today’s wars have become far more complex than the conventional conflicts humanity has experienced over millennia. These wars involve cyberattacks, economic coercion, psychological manipulation through electronic and social media, and cultural invasions. Critically, today’s wars employ lethal technologies, which have left traditional battlefield concepts behind. These autonomous weapons are guided by specialised semiconductors, microchips, and sensors. The changing nature of war has coincided with the sharp descent of the world order into disarray. Wars and armed conflicts between and within states have broken out in various parts of the world. The pledge that all states had made to the UN Charter to respect each other’s sovereignty and territorial integrity is no longer being honoured. Two distinguishing features of today’s warfare are advanced missiles and armed drones, which have transformed the nature of war by enabling high-precision, long-range, and rapid response capabilities. In last year’s India-Pakistan war, Pakistan used missiles to down several Indian aircraft flying well beyond visual range.
