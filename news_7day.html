Future wars

THE four-day military confrontation between India and Pakistan last year and the US-Iran war this year have revealed how today’s wars have become far more complex than the conventional conflicts humanity has experienced over millennia. The days of massed soldiery, mechanised armour, and standardised formations are over. Today’s wars are hybrid and employ every instrument of power a state can muster. These wars involve cyberattacks, economic coercion, psychological manipulation through electronic and social media, and cultural invasions. Critically, today’s wars employ lethal technologies, which have left traditional battlefield concepts behind. These autonomous weapons are guided by specialised semiconductors, microchips, and sensors. The changing nature of war has coincided with the sharp descent of the world order into disarray. Wars and armed conflicts between and within states have broken out in various parts of the world. The pledge that all states had made to the UN Charter to respect each other’s sovereignty and territorial integrity is no longer being honoured. Two distinguishing features of today’s warfare are advanced missiles and armed drones, which have transformed the nature of war by enabling high-precision, long-range, and rapid response capabilities. In last year’s India-Pakistan war, Pakistan used missiles to down several Indian aircraft flying well beyond visual range. In the US-Iran war this year, both sides relied mostly on missiles guided by infrared sensors and satellite communication rather than conventional confrontation. Autonomous weapons lack the capability to understand the context. Advanced robotic systems are being employed for intelligence, surveillance, and even targeted strikes. Drones and robotic dogs can provide real-time data, improve combat efficiency, and operate for high-risk functions such as clearance of mines or locating captured personnel. Unarmed aerial vehicles provide real-time intelligence, and are equipped with AI-driven capabilities to analyse data. Targets can be independently tracked and even struck by loitering munitions or suicide drones. Transporting supplies and ammunition entails no life losses thanks to unarmed ground vehicles. Drone swarms can now be trained to carry out coordinated attacks. It’s only a matter of time before humanoid robots are deployed for military roles. The spectrum of modern wars employs other technologies as well, such as quantum computing, which solves complex problems faster than classical computers. It ensures unhackable and secure data transmission during conflict situations. Next-generation structural battery composites can store energy for longer periods. Future wars will move further away from face-to-face engagement or human-controlled systems. Intelligent autonomous systems will be central to making decisions, often without human intervention. However, this raises some serious ethical questions. Autonomous weapons lack the capability to understand the context or apply compassion. There is greater likelihood of violating the laws of war and committing war crimes without assigning accountability. Further, the speed and agility of these technologies could cause conflicts to spiral out of control. These technologies will also be used to break an adversary’s morale by targeting its economic assets or supply chains. During the Iran war, for instance, President Trump threatened to destroy Iran’s power grid and bridges, even its civilisation. The kind of technologies a nation possesses will now determine the extent of damage that a future war would impose on an adversary’s policy options. The ability of a nation to acquire and em­­ploy modern technologies is thus critical to both wag­-ing future wars and surviving them. What makes future wars more frequent and unaccountable is the evolving global ecosystem, where use of force has emerged as the primary arbiter of international relations. Major powers have no qualms about using force illegally, unilaterally, and disproportionately to overpower adversaries. In such an uncertain environment, middle and small powers are looking for ways to fend for themselves and make alliances with like-minded countries. This has nudged countries to focus more on R&D for developing not only war-related technologies but also their own digital and satellite-based communication links. That requires a stronger economy, credible information instruments, and creative narrative-building to deter aggression. Diplomatic tools must be geared up to avert such complex multidomain future wars. The world needs a new global compact of indivisible security, akin to the UN Charter, to bind states into mutual respect for each other’s sovereignty and territorial integrity. This is the only way to avert the devastating wars of the future. The writer is Chairman Sanober Institute and former foreign secretary of Pakistan. Published in Dawn, November 22nd, 2026

COP31 climate talks set to collapse without political deal

United Nations climate talks in Turkiye looked set to collapse without a political agreement after COP31 summit president Murat Kurum said there would be no new draft of the deal. Earlier, multiple countries and negotiating blocs said they could not accept the version of the text put before them. “There will be no new text from now on. We will not be carrying out work on a new text in an environment where there is no consensus,” Kurum said. The climate talks fell into disarray on Sunday as the European Union and others rejected a final draft deal put forward by COP31 presidents Turkiye and Australia, saying it was too weak to advance the fight against global warming. After more than two weeks of negotiations, which had been due to end on Friday but stretched into the weekend, Kurum said at the closing plenary that a deal was not approved. “It is clear that there are parties in this hall who oppose this text. We recognise that there is no consensus on this document. Therefore, these decisions have not been adopted,” he said. The last time UN climate talks failed to end with a deal adopted by all was in Copenhagen in 2009. The collapse this time is against the backdrop of competing crises, including wars and public outcry over the cost of living, that have overshadowed the problems caused by a warming climate. Ireland’s environment minister Darragh O’Brien said the European Union could not support the proposed deal, in part because it made no mention of the fossil fuels that scientists say are driving global warming. “It is unconscionable that we would have a climate act that makes no reference to fossil fuel, no reference to transitioning away from fossil fuels,” he said. Panama also said it could not support the current text because it fails to meet the scale of the climate crisis. This year’s COP is the first since the UN’s environmental body in October that confirmed the rise in global temperature was set to exceed 1.5 degrees Celsius, a threshold countries agreed at Paris climate talks over a decade ago to try not to exceed, and beyond which extreme weather impacts intensify. Pressure from Saudi Arabia and other oil producers The proposed deal had acknowledged the overshoot, but called for faster development of carbon-removal technologies to eventually reverse it. Observers at the venue said Saudi Arabia and other big oil-producing nations had pushed hard in negotiations for keeping explicit mention of fossil fuels out of the text, and carbon removal technologies in. While scientists say removing carbon from the atmosphere when paired with steep emissions cuts is vital to avoiding the worst impacts of climate change, critics of the deal said they worried the carbon removal language would be used as a licence to keep pumping oil. A representative for the Saudi delegation told the summit he was surprised at the resistance to technology. “If indeed the climate challenge, as some have mentioned today, is so important, it makes no sense that solutions, concrete solutions… will be taken off the table,” he said. The COP process is the main vehicle through which countries coordinate their efforts to curb global warming. Its annual summit provides an opportunity for nations to signal they are willing to work together to find solutions and help finance the problems rising temperatures cause. While a blow to efforts to ratchet up international action, the failure to agree a political declaration in Antalya does not erase the obligations agreed by previous summits or remove the technical and legal frameworks governing multilateral action against climate change.

Nonetheless, he continued to write for various newspapers, including the Viewpoint, Holiday, Outlook, Frontier Post, Frontier Guardian, Daily Star, Punjab Times and Punjab Punch. Naqi consistently campaigned for the preservation and promotion of the Punjabi language in Pakistan, starting the first-ever daily newspaper in the Punjabi language — Sajjan. Naqi had also been imprisoned a couple of times because of his outspokenness. In the 2011 interview, he recalled those instances with a sense of humour. “I was lucky to invariably have someone favourably disposed towards me at the jail premises, who made sure that I was not mistreated.”

Till the early 1970s, it was often believed that South Asians who travelled to Western countries for studies or migrated there, often adopted ‘Western values.’ In early Indian and Pakistani movies, Pakistanis and Indians returning from the West were often portrayed as enlightened, rational people who scoffed at the superstitious beliefs of their countrymen and women. By the mid-1970s, though, the portrayal of West-returned Pakistanis and Indians changed. They began being portrayed as stupid, dope-smoking, guitar-slinging hippies. But in the decades that followed, reality turned out very differently. Instead of becoming less religious or adopting ‘immoral’ lifestyles, many South Asians living abroad actually became much more religious than their relatives back home. Anyone travelling between major South Asian cities and the suburbs of Western cities can see this contrast. In South Asia, religion is a natural part of everyday life, heard in the call to prayer or seen at local temples. People do not have to work hard to keep it alive. Abroad, however, mosque and temple attendance becomes stricter, food rules become more rigid, and dogmatic religious rules often replace the relaxed, mixed traditions practised back home. According to the American anthropologist Steven Vertovec, moving to a new country rarely weakens religious belief. Instead, it often makes people more conscious of their faith. This change happens because of a deep desire to protect one’s culture, and the political reality of living as a minority in a foreign land. Leaving South Asia means leaving behind extended families, friends, familiar streets and unspoken social rules. In Western countries, society focuses heavily on individual independence and small nuclear families, which can make newcomers feel lonely. Religion fills this void. South Asian immigrant communities in the secular West have often developed stricter religious identities than their counterparts back home. What explains this paradox? According to the Terror Management Theory developed by psychologists Jeff Greenberg, Sheldon Solomon and Tom Pyszczynski, when people feel anxious because their familiar world has disappeared, they hold on tighter to their core beliefs to feel secure. The mosque, temple or the gurdwara in a Western country offers this, more than they do in the homeland of the immigrants. At the same time, rising prejudice, Islamophobia and anti-immigrant attitudes trigger what American sociologists Alejandro Portes and Rubén Rumbaut call “reactive religion.” According to their research, when a minority group feels judged, turning toward religion becomes a way to defend themselves. Wearing visible religious symbols such as a hijab, a turban or a tilak becomes a public statement of strength — at least to the wearers.
