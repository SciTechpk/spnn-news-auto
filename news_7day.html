Innovative policing

IN post-colonial societies, police are averse to innovation. Conversely, militants opt for innovation and cultivate their following, using cyberspace, AI and information warfare innovatively. Is innovative policing (IP) viable under outdated laws, weak capacity, poor accountability and ill-equipped LEAs? Post-colonial police chiefs limit innovation to technology when it should be interpreted elastically. Often, militants get ahead of LEAs; they use drones, night vision goggles, thermal imaging, cyberspace, etc. IP merges tech-driven, community-oriented, problem-oriented and e-policing. Combining traditional police values with modern, evidence-based policing to analyse crime patterns improves public safety and wins community trust, reducing the burden on courts and jails. Policing that doesn’t factor in local needs can be a waste of resources. For IP to succeed, police chiefs and political executives must combine numerical models with technology-led ones rather than rely just on the former. Solutions like drones help LEAs in surveillance; mobile apps improve communication with the public. Deradicalisation and reintegration need collaboration among mental health counsellors, HR advocates and social workers. IP should benefit the public to win its trust and employ new ideas and a community-based approach to law enforcement. A proactive, preventive and participatory mode can help police transition from a force to a service model and boost democratic governance via technology, institutional reforms and enhanced community involvement. Policing today faces fake news and militants’ misuse of technology. It can improve through transparency, training, independent oversight, investigation and witness cooperation. Police can work with LGs, schools and social services to address violent tendencies, reduce crime, strengthen public trust and enhance fairness in policing. IP should benefit the public. Limited finances, few tech-skilled staff and internet disruptions limit innovation. Outdated laws and systems don’t support digital evidence; absent or weak privacy and data protection also hinder innovation. Traditional societies resist change, fearing police may misuse innovation for control and invasion of privacy. A lack of long-term policy vision and reform, together with regular political changes, hinders innovation. Poor outreach limits it too. Corruption undermines transparency. Tech without institutional reform leads to failure. IP enhances public safety through GPS-enabled patrols and emergency apps, improves monitoring of public places and helps reduce street crime. It boosts trust and police-community ties via engagement, decreases fear and improves service delivery through online FIR and complaint registration. Tech use enables police to improve crime investigation, reducing reliance on coercive means. Helplines and apps for children, women and trans persons improve safety. Technology reduces face-to-face interaction. Despite security challenges, KP Police have launched innovative public-friendly initiatives. The Nadra-integrated Police Sahulat Markaz Global App offers many services: IGP complaints access, online complaints for FIR, police character certificates, Talash-i-Gumshda, servant verification, Pink Button for women and children, trans persons’ complaint portal (Police Dost), SOS Alert and Police Sahulat counters at hospitals for medico-legal certificates. Japan’s Koban policing includes neighbourhood police stations in communities; police here work with residents and local ins­titutions, boosting public trust and crime prevention. Singapore has developed tech-led ‘sm­art policing’, combining facial recognition, AI-enabled CCTV and smart patrol vehicles, reducing the need for human resources and enhan-cing response times. In the UK, neighbourhood policing teams work with local councils, NGOs and residents to tackle youth issues, an approach that has led to effective local solutions. Estonia has paperless e-policing and citizen services, including online crime reporting. Scotland’s Violence Reduction Unit addresses violence as a public health issue, fostering collaboration among police and health and education departments. Bodycams during public interaction in some countries have led to increased transparency and reduced misconduct and false allegations. Gender-sensitive policing in Canada and the Nordic states focuses on victims’ unique needs. Without changes to laws and procedures, transparency in public complaints and training aligned with contemporary challenges, IP cannot become a reality, and technology will fail to win public trust. As described above, the Koban system operates effectively and should be modified for use in Pakistan’s rural areas. At some point, four of those names were removed and, by Thursday, the death toll had been reduced to 14, it added. The KP chief minister made the assertion in a rejoinder filed in response to a petition moved by Malik Zaheer Ahmed, who challenged the legal status of the movement. The petitioner argued that the formation of the force to secure the release of incarcerated PTI founder Imran Khan violated the Constitution and posed a threat to public order. A three-judge FCC bench, headed by Chief Justice Aminuddin Khan, will take up the petition on July 29. At the last hearing on April 1, the FCC had directed the KP chief minister to respond to the petition seeking to prevent the mobilisation or activation of the force. The rejoinder stated that the alleged “Imran Khan Rehayi Force” had neither been constituted, notified nor operationalised at any stage. “No armed structure, organisational militancy, coercive framework or paramilitary setup exists or has been contemplated”. Any prior reference to the term “force”, if misinterpreted, has no legal or factual existence and cannot be relied upon, the KP chief minister emphasised, adding that the initiative in question was, in fact, the “Imran Khan Rehayi Aman Movement”, a peaceful, unarmed, voluntary, civic and political movement. Its primary objective, the rejoinder said, was to raise public awareness about due process of law and the state’s responsibility to act fairly in initiating criminal prosecution. The movement, rejoinder explained, was strictly aimed at lawful, democratic and constitutional advocacy for the release of a political leader, without any element of force, militancy or coercion. The comparison drawn by the petitioner between the present movement and alleged historical organisations such as the Al-Zulfiqar Organisation or elements associated with the Muttahida Qaumi Movement is entirely misplaced, factually incorrect, misleading and legally untenable, it contended. Such comparisons, it argued, are based on political conjecture, generalisations and historical references that have no nexus with the present facts and cannot be used to infer illegality, militancy or a threat to public order. The rejoinder stated that the responsibilities of the KP chief minister are confined to public welfare, development, governance and the well-being of the people of the province. It added that policy decisions regarding the formation of any movement, including the Imran Khan Rehayi Aman Movement, fall within the domain of the political party and its organisational structures, and not within the constitutional or administrative functions of the chief minister acting in his official capacity. In his capacity as KP chief minister, he neither has the authority nor the mandate to unilaterally constitute any such political or organisational movement in an official capacity. Therefore, the allegation is legally misconceived and factually misplaced, the rejoinder argued. It maintained that the petition fails to meet the constitutional threshold under Article 175(3), as no question of public importance involving the enforcement of fundamental rights had been made out. It argued that no actual, imminent or continuing violation of fundamental rights exists and that the petition is based entirely on assumptions, media interpretations and speculative fears. “It is settled law that constitutional jurisdiction cannot be invoked on hypothetical or anticipatory grounds,” the rejoinder said, adding that the petition is therefore not maintainable in law and is liable to be dismissed for being premature, speculative, hypothetical and based purely on conjecture and apprehensions, without any actual or imminent violation of fundamental rights. Moreover, the petitioner has failed to establish any locus standi, legal injury or enforceable cause of action against the KP chief minister, it said, adding that the petition is politically motivated, misconceived and based on a misrepresentation of facts, intended to portray lawful political activity as an unlawful enterprise. The petitioner has not approached the FCC with clean hands, having selectively misquoted and mischaracterised statements, the rejoinder said. It added that the petition suffers from vagueness, lack of material particulars and absence of evidence, and is therefore liable to be dismissed on this ground alone.
