What does Alberta's referendum on separation from Canada mean?

Alberta is holding a non-binding referendum on Monday to ask residents if they want to remain part of Canada, marking the first time the oil-rich province has ever put the question of separation to the public. Polls have consistently shown that most Albertans — around 70 per cent — want to stay in Canada, with roughly a quarter of residents supporting an independent Alberta. What will residents be asked? There are 10 questions, each with its own colour-coded ballot. The last ballot voters see is question 10, on whether Alberta should remain a Canadian province or start the legal process required to hold a binding referendum on separation. A simple majority in favour of separation could trigger that process. There are nine other nonbinding questions about immigration, access to provincial services including education, social care and healthcare and voter identification requirements. Supporters of Alberta separating from Canada hold a banner and flags during a rally in May. — AFP/File Why is Alberta having this referendum? Alberta Premier Danielle Smith said in May it was time to have a vote, understand the will of Albertans on this subject, and move on, when she announced the government would add a question on separation. Smith’s decision followed months of campaigning by a small but vocal group of separatists, who argue Alberta’s energy wealth is subsidising poorer parts of the country and that bureaucracy in Ottawa has stifled the province’s economy. Some members of the group had backed Smith’s candidacy for premier. Smith says she voted for Alberta to remain in Canada during advance polling, according to a video she posted on social media on Wednesday. Have there been previous separatist referendums in Canada? Yes, but not in Alberta. The majority French-speaking province of Quebec has had two referendums, in 1980 and 1995. Quebecers voted to remain in Canada both times, but by the slimmest of margins in 1995. The separatist Parti Quebecois (PQ) won a provincial election earlier this month and has pledged to hold a third referendum, but only after US President Donald Trump has left office and if the party holds a majority. The PQ was elected with a minority government on October 5, meaning it will rely on smaller parties to pass legislation. Canada is a federation of 10 provinces and three territories. While Ottawa is responsible for matters of national interest, such as defence, trade and foreign policy, the provinces and territories hold significant power over medical care, education and land rights. When will the results be known? Polls are scheduled to close at 8pm on Monday (0200 GMT on Tuesday). Elections Alberta says it will count ballots by hand and that the question on separation will be counted first. It will publish results on the Elections Alberta website as they are received, and unofficial results are expected within 48 hours after voting ends. What happens if most Albertans vote to start the process of leaving Canada? Smith has said she will respect the result and quickly begin the process to hold a legally binding referendum. But separation would not happen imminently. After the 1995 Quebec referendum, Canada’s Parliament passed the Clarity Act in 2000, which says talks about secession can only be triggered if there is a clear majority from the public, without specifying the necessary threshold. The act also states provinces and territories have no unilateral right to leave Canada and that a constitutional amendment negotiated by provincial, territorial and federal governments is required. In May, an Alberta court judge ruled the province’s chief electoral officer was wrong to allow separatists to collect signatures requesting a referendum, because the process should have prompted a consultation with Indigenous peoples whose rights might be violated by separation. Alberta has appealed the decision. What has PM Carney said about the referendum? Carney, who was largely raised in Alberta, has previously said the best place for Alberta is in Canada and that the country cannot succeed if it’s divided. He released a video on Canadian Thanksgiving, October 12, saying national unity is critical as Canada fights threats from abroad, including a trade war with the US. Carney has campaigned for Alberta to remain in Canada.

Karachi reports its first polio case in over 1.5 years, bringing this year's countrywide tally to 7

ISLAMABAD: Pakistan has reported its seventh polio case of the year, with a six-month-old boy from Karachi’s East district testing positive for wild poliovirus, the National Emergency Operation Centre (NEOC) said on Monday. The affected child belongs to Union Council Jamali Colony and was identified through the polio surveillance network. The case was confirmed by the Regional Reference Laboratory for Polio Eradication at the National Institutes of Health (NIH) in Islamabad, it said in a statement. The NEOC said the child was malnourished and had fallen victim to virus circulation already present in Karachi. According to an official of the polio programme, the latest case is the first reported from Karachi since December 2024. The city has faced intense virus circulation for the past two years, although focused door-to-door vaccination campaigns and improvements in essential immunisation had contributed to a marked reduction in transmission. Only one environmental surveillance site in Karachi tested positive in September, NEOC said, but vulnerable children remained at risk as long as the virus continued to circulate. A comprehensive investigation has been launched, while the Polio Eradication Initiative (PEI) is assessing the “best science-based response to tackle and prevent further transmission, including targeted vaccination activities and support to strengthen routine immunisation”. NEOC also underscored the importance of vaccinating children against the incurable, paralytic disease. Of the seven cases detected across Pakistan this year, five were from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa — including four from Bannu division — and two from Sindh. The latest case has emerged about two weeks after this year’s sixth case was reported from KP’s Khyber district, where a seven-year-old boy tested positive for the disease. Polio is a highly contagious and incurable disease that can cause lifelong paralysis and, in some cases, death. It can, however, be prevented through safe and effective polio vaccines. Pakistan and Afghanistan are the only two countries where polio is still endemic. According to NEOC, Pakistan had recorded a 99.8 per cent reduction in polio cases from an estimated 20,000 in the early 1990s to 31 in 2025 and seven so far this year. Four nationwide polio campaigns have already been conducted in 2026, protecting more than 45 million children multiple times, besides targeted oral and injectable vaccination activities in high-risk areas. The next subnational campaign is scheduled for Nov 2-8 and will target nearly 24m children in 97 districts, including all districts of Karachi, in synchronisation with Afghanistan.

For those already struggling to make ends meet, another failed harvest or flood could mean borrowing again simply to survive. The same disaster that may loosen one family’s dependence on a landlord can deepen another’s debt to one. “Wherever law ends, tyranny begins”—John Locke A few years after my resignation from the Income Tax Service, I happened to visit the offices in Lahore as a representative of a corporate client. A female officer made a remark that left me somewhat bewildered. According to her, laws were binding on taxpayers, not on those administering them. Having spent nineteen years within the department, I had thought I had understood the relationship between law and administration reasonably well. Her statement appeared astonishing then. However, as I watch the annual ritual surrounding income tax returns unfold today, I sometimes wonder whether she was merely articulating an institutional philosophy that the rest of us had failed to recognise. For individuals and associations of persons (AOPs), the statutory date is on or before September 30 following the end of the tax year. In the case of companies, section 118 of the Income Tax Ordinance, 2001 prescribes December 31 where the tax year ends between January 1 and June 30, and September 30 in other cases. The words “on or before” are important. A normal tax year ends on June 30, which corresponds to the financial year (July to June) adopted by the state. Nothing in the statutory formulation suggests that a taxpayer who is ready to comply should have to wait until September merely because the administration has not yet made its prescribed return operational. It stipulates the last date by which the obligation must be discharged but there is one rather substantial difficulty. While the taxpayer may be ready, the FBR may not. Income tax returns in Pakistan are now overwhelmingly filed electronically through the online portal named IRIS (Inland Revenue Information System). Taxpayers cannot invent their own return form. They cannot prepare an Excel sheet containing what they consider adequate information and declare that they have complied with section 114. They must use the prescribed electronic return made available by the FBR. The state, therefore, controls the only doorway through which compliance is possible. Compliance cannot be a one-way street. Once the state makes itself the exclusive gatekeeper of compliance, its own preparedness ceases to be an internal administrative matter. It becomes part of the taxpayer’s ability to obey the law. This becomes particularly important because every Finance Bill, once passed an Act of Parliament, introduces amendments affecting rates, exemptions, deductions, credits, withholding provisions, declarations and sometimes the very manner in which income is to be reported. Naturally, the return form has to accommodate those changes. Nobody disputes that software requires modification in the light of these amendments. What is difficult to understand is why this annual phenomenon continues to be treated as an unforeseen emergency. Pakistan does not suddenly discover on June 30 or before, that a Money Bill, presented with the annual federal budget, on approval by the National Assembly, has become a Finance Act, effective from the first day of July. The budgetary exercise begins months earlier. Proposed amendments are available through the Finance Bill. The FBR itself participates intimately in the legislative exercise. Its policy officers know what changes are being contemplated and its information-technology teams should be working simultaneously on alternative versions of the return. Tax Year 2026 provides a particularly revealing example. The FBR issued draft electronic returns for individuals, SMEs, AOPs and companies on May 6, 2026. Yet the final electronic returns for Tax Year 2026 were notified only at the beginning of September — SRO 1495(I)/2026 dated September 2. In other words, although the taxpayer’s statutory deadline remained September 30, the final return itself arrived when nearly two-thirds of the presumed compliance period had already vanished. Consider what this means. The statutory deadline remained September 30, but taxpayers and practitioners were effectively given certainty about the final prescribed electronic return only when September had already begun. Millions of returns then had to be prepared, checked, reconciled with wealth statements and submitted through a single electronic system within a dramatically compressed period. Inevitably, tax bars and trade organisations sought an extension. The original September 30, 2026, deadline was first extended to October 15, 2026 and, on October 15, the FBR extended it again to October 31, 2026, expressly referring to requests received from trade bodies and tax bar associations. And here lies the irony. The FBR calls this an extension. Taxpayers may reasonably regard part of it as restitution. The extension is presented as a concession to taxpayers but is it really so? If an authority takes a substantial portion of the statutory filing period to make the final compliance mechanism available, the additional time subsequently granted cannot honestly be regarded entirely as administrative generosity. Part of it merely restores time which taxpayers never effectively possessed. We have somehow reversed the relationship between citizen and state. The taxpayer is admonished to comply punctually. Failure attracts consequences. Late filing affects status and can invite statutory penalties and other disadvantages. Notices, payments, appeals and responses carry dates. The taxpayer lives by the calendar. Yet the institution that determines whether electronic compliance is even possible appears to face no comparable clock. When it delays, taxpayers must petition that very institution for additional time. Administrative default is thus converted into administrative discretion. This is not merely an issue of convenience. It raises a basic question of administrative fairness. A government cannot reasonably demand punctual compliance with an obligation while withholding, until late in the prescribed period, the instrument through which that obligation must be performed. Imagine a court requiring an appeal to be filed by September 30 but refusing to provide the prescribed appeal form until September 2. Would we seriously congratulate the court for subsequently extending the deadline? Digitalisation was supposed to facilitate compliance. It should not convert administrative preparedness into a condition precedent to the taxpayer’s ability to obey the law. Nor is the answer complicated. The return for the following tax year should be substantially designed alongside the Finance Bill. Drafts can be programmed and tested before June. Once Parliament enacts the Finance Act, final changes can immediately be incorporated. The electronic form should ordinarily be operational from July 1, or as close to that date as technically unavoidable. More importantly, the law itself should guarantee a minimum effective compliance period — say ninety days — beginning only when the final prescribed electronic return is fully operational. Every day of administrative delay should automatically move the statutory deadline by an equivalent period. If the prescribed electronic return is not available by July 1, the statutory filing date should automatically move by the corresponding period. Better still, legislation could provide that no taxpayer shall have fewer than, say, ninety days from the date on which the final electronic return becomes fully operational. Then nobody would need to plead for extensions. There would be no annual suspense. No last-minute circulars. No ritual exchanges between FBR and tax bars. In addition, no pretense that restoration of lost compliance time amounts to benevolence. The FBR rightly expects taxpayers to organise their affairs, maintain records, understand amendments and respect statutory deadlines. Taxpayers are equally entitled to expect their tax administration to anticipate legislation, prepare its systems and provide the prescribed mechanism in good time. The rule of law does not mean that citizens obey while administrators improvise. It requires the State itself to organise its conduct around the law it enforces. Perhaps that officer’s remark from many years ago deserves to be remembered — but as a warning, not a principle. Laws are not made merely for those upon whom the state imposes obligations. They bind the institutions entrusted with administering them as well. Until that elementary proposition enters our tax culture, the ritual will continue: The FBR will provide the instrument late, taxpayers will plead for more time, and the restoration of lost days will be announced as facilitation. That is not taxpayer facilitation. It is compliance by endurance.
