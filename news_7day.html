HYDERABAD: Carpeting of a new road and opening it for traffic, despite many electricity poles still standing in the middle of the dual carriageway, has triggered a heated debate on social media as travelers risk life and limb. Responding to the criticism from people, the Sindh government defended its decision to complete and open the road — connecting Alamdar Chowk with the old National Highway near Qasim Chowk — saying that the Hyderabad Electric Supply Company (Hesco) had been showing lethargy in fulfilling its responsibility of relocating the poles after which such a decision was taken. The poles carry high-tension lines emanating from near the 132-kV grid station of Qasimabad that feeds a considerably large part of Hyderabad city. Social media users had uploaded videos of the road dotted with the poles a couple of days back with crispy comments. CM says carriageway carpeted and opened to force Hesco into relocating transmission lines The poles existed in their place when the road was being given the first layer of carpeting. Since the two ends of the road were not blocked with barriers, vehicles started plying along it supposing that there was no risk in using it. However, the poles posed a great risk to commuters’ life. Motorists and bike-riders have to drive in a zig-zag manner to avoid hitting the poles. On Friday, the criticism forced Sindh Chief Minister Syed Murad Ali Shah to publicly explain that it was Hesco’s responsibility to relocate the poles. The power utility was provided funds for the work, but it did not carry out the work, he said while speaking at the inaugural ceremony of the World Culture Festival in Karachi. The chief minister said there was a major issue of land availability for the construction of the road. The chief secretary, he said, contacted the military estate authorities and got the land use permission. He explained that as a matter of strategy, the government constructed the road first and restored the traffic so that pressure would come upon Hesco. He pointed out: “As soon as the road was constructed, public pressure increased and Hesco was forced to remove the poles”. The road is a 1.6km-long dual carriageway which is part of three road projects involving a cost of Rs1.06 billion. It is entirely new for the people of Hyderabad and connects the city’s bypass from the Qasim Chowk road. Until now used to be known as Ghora Ground, the artery has been converted to an asphalt road, the landscape otherwise being an abandoned waterlogged area used for electricity transmission to Deh Fouj Gah through the poles. The area is located within the administrative territory of the Cantonment Board Hyderabad and Military Estate Office (MEO). Chief Secretary Sindh Asif Hyder Shah had obtained permission from the two offices to build this road in order to lessen the traffic pressure on Qasim Chowk and Wadhu Wah in Qasimabad. The work on the road project had begun in April this year. Hyderabad mayor’s spokesman and district administration also had to respond to the criticism from people on social media. Sindh government’s spokesman Sadia Javed clarified the situation, but admitted that there was some sort of negligence seen on the part of the administration and that it should have put up barricades there with warning signs for commuters. “When development schemes are executed, some issues do crop up,” she maintained. According to Hesco spokesman Sadiq Kubar, Qasimabad grid station located near Alamdar Chowk feeds electricity to 27 feeders. Similarly, he explained, 100 high-transmission lines needed to be shifted and out of them, 50 were positioned in the area until this road project was conceived. He said that relocation of the poles involved a cost of Rs79m. He said that while these 50 poles stood removed from the road, as many others were located along the grid station. The cost of injustice

TO live in Karachi, or really any city in the tumultuous world that is our reality, is an exercise in dodging misfortune. When one leaves the house, one braces for survival. Traffic has to be navigated, angry or errant drivers avoided, beggars ask for money and there is filth on the street. By the time one reaches the destination, one is in a state of high vigilance. Dangers and disruptions are everywhere. This means that most in the city live in a constant state of hypervigilance. Some would say that wealth can insulate a person from this avalanche of potential misfortune. There is material evidence of this: the wealthy have drivers who take them places, their cars and homes are cool with air conditioning and electricity outages are eliminated with generators. They see the best doctors and eat the best food. If you belong to this class, the injustice that you see everywhere in a megacity is made distant. The maimed beggar, the dog being beaten, the woman wrapped in black trying to navigate a street full of men are things that exist in some different world. The car window, the tall gate, the armed guard — these, it is assumed, make the cruelties of the context irrelevant. If they are considered, it is only in a calculation that assures you that your good fortune is deserved and those suffering must have earned it through some arithmetic of divine distribution. The truth is that life in an unjust society exacts a cost. It does not matter how well you may take care of yourself; context matters. In her extraordinary book Weathering: The Extraordinary Stress of Ordinary Life in an Unjust Society, Dr Arline Geronimus has shown that the constant physical toll of coping with injustice can have deep physical and mental consequences. Naturally, the most disadvantaged suffer the worst consequences, but the effects of living amid inequality accrue on everyone in a society. Diseased societies produce diseased people. One category of disadvantaged people in Pakistani society is women. In a world in which patriarchy reigns, women are always playing defence. Marriage is a minefield where, if the demands of a husband or in laws are not met, every day can become a living hell. A long list of dead women that I regularly write about in this column is evidence of this. If a woman is unable to bear a child for instance, she may either face divorce or her husband taking a second wife. If children do come, they can increase a woman’s dependence on her husband, making it harder to resist whatever strictures are imposed upon her. The cost of all this is a constant state of vigilance, and Geronimus showed just how it can physically wear one down. Repeated activation of the stress response can disrupt hormonal regulation, raise blood pressure and contribute to inflammation. In the long term, this increases vulnerability to chronic disease and early death. Diseased societies produce diseased people. A 2021 systematic study and meta-analysis found that 37 per cent of pregnant women experienced antenatal depression, while 30pc experienced postnatal depression. Intimate partner violence and poor relationships with spouses were among the reported risk factors. These figures can be attributed to the fact that women do not feel confident about their own or their children’s futures because they live in a society where injustice is common. The most interesting thing about research on men and women in unjust societies is that men suffer too. Research examining sexism in US states has linked patriarchal contexts to poorer health outcomes in both men and women. People believe that individual actions, status or material wealth can allow them to ev­­ade the conditions they see others endure. All this suggests that individual choices and we­­alth are not the biggest determinants of health in a society. Instead, it is the availability of collective justice and respect for life that affect health outcomes. This should give all Pakistanis pause. The poor are treated horribly, and we see terrible crimes committed against the weak every day. Women are regularly treated like chattel, children are routinely abused and caste and wealth determine the course of one’s life. The worst curse is that we have learned to rationalise all of this, to absorb it as the way things are, the way things have been and the way things will always be. But being inured to injustice and quietly accepting of it is making us sick. Our tolerance of it creeps into our cells and our bones. It is time we understood that there is no individual cure for these conditions and what ails us is a collective issue — deeper and more entrenched than improving our diets, quitting smoking or hitting the gym. Our habit of seeing so many wrongs and ignoring them is quite literally what is killing us. Pakistan’s reforms deficit

PAKISTAN has never suffered from a shortage of civil service reform proposals. It has suffered from a shortage of reforms that survive implementation. Since independence, successive governments, commissions and committees have attempted to restructure the administrative machinery inherited from the colonial state. Yet familiar complaints persist: excessive generalism, weak accountability, politicisation, distorted incentives, slow decision-making and a bureaucracy often better equipped to process files than solve complex problems. The latest reform package is significant not because Pakistan has rediscovered civil service reform, but because it attempts to address several structural weaknesses simultaneously. It proposes 11 specialised professional cadres, competency-based recruitment, performance-linked remuneration, restructuring of training and human-resource management, and a National Executive Service (NES) under which BS-20 and above positions could be opened to serving officials, academics and private-sector professionals. Pakistan inherited the powerful, generalist Civil Service of Pakistan from the colonial administrative tradition. The most dramatic restructuring came under Zulfikar Ali Bhutto in 1973, when the CSP structure was abolished and occupational groups reorganised. But restructuring did not democratise opportunity within government. The devolution reforms introduced under Gen Pervez Musharraf in 2001 represented another major intervention, transferring substantial authority towards elected local governments and altering traditional district administration. But institutional discontinuity and subsequent political changes prevented the system from developing into a durable architecture of local governance. Reform is often treated as an event rather than a sustained process. This history reveals Pakistan’s recurring problem: reform is often treated as an event rather than a sustained process. The strongest feature of the present package is its recognition that a modern state cannot be managed through transferable generalists. Professional cadres in fields including IT and telecom, energy, climate, engineering, health, education, law, agriculture and science address a genuine administrative weakness. Opening senior positions to competition could be still more consequential. It could bring specialists into government who would otherwise never enter conventional public service and allow capable career officers to compete beyond rigid cadre boundaries. But lateral entry carries an equally obvious risk: without credible safeguards, it can become lateral patronage. International experience offers principles rather than readymade templates. Pakistan should borrow principles, not blindly transplant institutions. Performance-linked remuneration illustrates the challenge. Annual performance agreements for ministries, flowing down to individual officers, could improve accountability. But who determines whether a secretary has performed well? If targets are vague or assessments depend excessively upon political superiors, financial incentives could rew­ard proximity to power rather than performance. The ultimate measurement must therefore be governance. Does a business licence take days instead of months? Are teachers and doctors present? Are development projects completed on time? Can citizens obtain public services without intermediaries? Can civil servants make lawful professional decisions without seeking political protection? Significantly, the Police Service and Foreign Service would remain outside the proposed NES. There are sound reasons for treating them as specialised career services requiring institutional continuity, accumulated professional experience and service-specific training. Their exclusion from the NES, however, need not mean exclusion from reform. For the police, Pakistan does not need to start from scratch. In May 2018, then chief justice Mian Saqib Nisar initiated a Police Reforms Committee bringing together nine retired and seven serving Inspectors General of Police. After extensive deliberations, it produced the two-volume Police Reforms: Way Forward, launched in Jan 2019. Building upon the Police Order 2002 and subsequent experience, the exercise addressed some of the most persistent weaknesses of policing: accountability, quality and independence of investigation, complaint redressal, urban policing, alternative dispute resolution, counterterrorism and the legal framework governing the police. It proposed a model police law while recognising the constitutional and administrative realities of Pakistan’s federal system. Yet the principal structural problems identified by the committee remain largely unresolved. Its convener, former IGP Afzal Ali Shigri, observed as recently as Feb 2026 that fragmented legal frameworks, weaknesses in investigation and uneven accountability mechanisms continue to impede reform. The 2019 police reform report thus stands as a telling case study of Pakistan’s persistent gap between designing reform and implementing it. The exclusion of the Police Service from the NES presents an opportunity rather than necessarily a deficiency. Instead of forcing policing into an administrative model designed for civilian mi­­nistries, the government can revive Police Reforms: Way Forward, update it and implement its still-relevant recommendations. The Foreign Service can similarly retain its distinctive professional structure while pursuing the broader reform principles of merit, specialisation, transparent advancement and measurable performance. The government proposes an ambitious implementation timetable for the new civil-service package. Speed can create momentum, but changing regulations is easier than changing administrative culture. No examination reform can compensate for arbitrary postings. No specialised cadre can flourish if expertise is subordinated to connections. No performance contract creates accountability if influence protects poor performance. And no reform report, however professionally drafted, improves governance while it remains on a shelf. Pakistan has spent decades reforming bureaucracy on paper. The real reform is to build institutions where competence matters more than cadre, performance more than seniority, expertise more than connections and public service more than institutional privilege. Excessive punishment

THE supposed ‘crime’ did not justify a trial under terrorism laws, and certainly not the punishment. On Thursday, human rights lawyer Imaan Mazari-Hazir and her husband and fellow lawyer, Hadi Ali Chattha, were sentenced to 10 years each for moving a security barrier reportedly placed to protect a touring cricket team, as well as for “incitement to public unrest” during a face-off with police. The trial, held in jail, drew persistent criticism over an alleged lack of due process. The judge held that Ms Mazari-Hazir and Mr Chattha had attempted to “compromise the security arrangements [made for the team] through pressuring security officials”. The judgement cited the 2009 attack on the Sri Lankan cricket team in Lahore — an assault that had been perpetrated by more than a dozen terrorists armed with heavy weapons — to reason that the lawyers’ act of removing the barrier and ‘assaulting’ police personnel “put the security of an international team visiting Pakistan at risk [and] clearly shows the intentions and objective of the accused to create sense of terror [sic]”. Just last month, after a lengthy appeals process, the Supreme Court ordered the suspension of the sentence handed to the two lawyers in the so-called social media posts case and ordered their release pending the Islamabad High Court’s decision on their appeal against the trial court’s verdict. However, they were promptly rearrested in connection with this case, and have now been handed a lengthy sentence that will keep them behind bars while they appeal. Ms Mazari-Hazir is one of the most prominent champions of civil and human rights from the legal community, and has often taken cases involving some of Pakistan’s most vulnerable citizens pro bono. She is also an outspoken critic of government and state excesses. It is unfortunate to see her and her husband punished so harshly for acts that seem downright frivolous compared to the violence actual terrorists routinely perpetrate. The higher courts must hear their appeal swiftly, and the state must stop stretching antiterrorism laws to cover acts that bear no resemblance to terrorism. Such verdicts send a chilling message to every lawyer willing to defend the vulnerable. The law need only appear to serve narrow interests, rather than justice, for public faith in it to be shaken. PM Shehbaz forms body to review proposed LPG policy reforms

• Policy seeks to shift LPG imports and price-setting from Ogra to SOEs• State-owned enterprises to introduce import-parity pricing based on Saudi Aramco rates and actual import costs ISLAMABAD: Amid soaring prices following supply disruptions, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif has constituted an eight-member ministerial committee to review a set of policy changes proposed by the Petroleum Division for liquefied petroleum gas (LPG). The proposed reforms envisage allowing state-owned enterprises (SOEs) to enter the LPG import business and take over price-setting from the regulator, with prices subsequently passed on to the private sector. The committee was constituted after LPG stakeholders, including marketing companies and dealers, protested against the finalisation of the new LPG policy without consulting them. The policy was earlier scheduled to come into force within a week. Stakeholders said SOEs had limited LPG import experience and warned that the proposed levy on locally produced LPG would push prices higher, calling for a greater private-sector role. LPG prices in the market range between Rs6,000 and Rs7,200 per 11.57kg cylinder, according to reported market rates, compared with the Oil and Gas Regulatory Authority’s (Ogra) notified price of Rs3,296, as operations at the Iran border came to a halt earlier this month and imports through sea routes declined. The committee, headed by the petroleum minister, will include the ministers for economic affairs and information, the PM’s adviser on interprovincial coordination, special assistant on energy, petroleum secretary, DG liquefied gases and head of the Government Relations Unit (GRU). The committee has one month to review the proposed LPG reforms, cylinder standards and local LPG model, and submit a comprehensive report to the prime minister. A cabinet summary, seen by Dawn, proposes retaining monthly LPG pricing but removing Ogra’s role, with SOEs, including PSO, Sui companies, PARCO and Pakistan LNG, leading the shift to import-parity pricing. Under the proposal, SOEs would import LPG in bulk and set monthly prices based on Saudi Aramco’s FOB rates, plus actual premiums, freight, insurance, incidental expenses and import taxes incurred in the previous month. “Given the urgency and need for greater participation by larger market players, it is being proposed that financial offers by the bidders would not be published on the PPRA website (exemption), in line with general market practice,” the summary stated. It also proposed that Ogra immediately stop issuing price notifications for locally produced and imported LPG. The summary proposed keeping the monthly producer price linked to Saudi Aramco’s current-month contract price (CP) on an FOB basis, with adjustments according to the propane-butane ratio. Local producers would be entitled to receive only this producer price, while the difference between the Saudi CP-linked producer price and PSO’s base price would be deposited into a dedicated fund to subsidise poor consumers through the Benazir Income Support Programme (BISP). The private sector wants to end Saudi CP-linked pricing, arguing that most LPG imports come from countries such as Iran and Oman. When SOEs do not import LPG, the previous month’s premiums, incidental costs and taxes would apply alongside the current month’s CP, as in the pricing mechanism for petrol and diesel. The summary also proposed increasing the margins of LPG marketing and importing companies to a minimum of Rs50,000 per tonne from the existing Rs35,000 per tonne. The increase has been proposed as an inflation adjustment to margins that have remained unchanged since the 2016 LPG policy. However, the revised margins would remain subject to competition. The Competition Commission of Pakistan and Ogra would be responsible for preventing collusion among industry players. The proposal also calls for a Producer-Price Differential Fund, with refineries and other producers depositing the difference between local and imported LPG prices into a dedicated Public Account. The fund would be audited by the Auditor General of Pakistan and used by the federal government to provide targeted subsidies to vulnerable LPG consumers through the BISP scorecard. The proposal allocates 10pc of locally produced LPG to state-owned marketers at producer prices to supply Gilgit-Baltistan, Northern Areas and other remote regions through cylinders and existing air-mix plants. The remaining 90pc would be allocated to prequalified bottlers through transparent monthly auctions, with a floor price set at five per cent of PSO’s import-parity price.
