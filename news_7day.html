Bad COP, worse COP: What’s next after climate talks fail?

The collapse of marathon climate talks in Turkey has renewed questions about reform of their consensus requirement, and galvanised calls for legal action and ambitious side agreements between like-minded countries. The “conference of the parties” talks, better known as COP31, ended without agreement and two days over schedule on Sunday, as countries deadlocked on fossil fuels, finance, science and carbon-removing technologies. It was an ignominious end for an unusual dual-presidency shared by Australia and Turkey, and negotiations chair Chris Bowen, who is Australia’s climate minister, said it was “very disappointing”. For many observers, the outcome was the inevitable result of a process that has regularly been hamstrung by its insistence on consensus. “Decision-making that requires every country to agree on every comma is not fit for a climate emergency,” said Nikki Reisch, director of the Center of International Environmental Law’s climate and energy programme. Bowen identified the same issue. “The fundamental root of the problem is that when COP was created, for reasons I understand, it was made consensus only,” he said. “That’s a very tough ask.” ‘Significant reform’ Sunday’s collapse has renewed calls for a reform to that consensus-based process, in which any one country, or a small minority, can nix progress. “It has been clear for years that the COP process is in need of significant reform,” wrote former US vice-president Al Gore on X. He urged a “reinvigorated multilateral process that reflects the latest science and represents the will of the majority of the world.” But as Bowen noted, “you also need consensus to change consensus” — in other words, all countries would have to agree those reforms. He described that as a “particular challenge”, although others might characterise it as simply impossible. The COP31 talks deadlocked over multiple issues, notably whether the so-called cover decision would mention phasing out planet-warming fossil fuels. Several countries refused a deal without that mention, while others including Saudi Arabia were vehemently opposed to its inclusion. There were other serious fault lines, including the timing of scientific work from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, and language on carbon capture, a technology championed by the fossil fuel industry but criticised by some as costly and ineffective. Crucially for developing countries, there was also no progress on finance — the money badly needed to help countries transition energy systems and adapt to the natural disasters climate change is already worsening. “The fundamental problem for globally agreed climate policy is more starkly obvious than ever: fossil fuel powers will not agree to have their resource wealth devalued, and rich countries won’t provide enough climate finance to the developing world,” said Frank Jotzo, professor of environmental and climate change economics at the Australian National University. ‘Doesn’t replace COP’ Other COPs have ended in failure before, including most recently in 2009. But for all the forum’s failings, most agree that the COP process remains important for its unique format, bringing together virtually every country in the world and offering equal billing to all. As a result, few suggest the process should be abandoned, but there are growing calls for alternatives to advance climate change action, including legal suits. That route is already being pursued, most notably with an International Court of Justice advisory opinion that confirmed states have a binding legal obligation to combat climate change. “If the negotiations are not delivering what individual states need, then they’ll certainly consider turning to the ICJ and other international courts,” said Harj Narulla, an international lawyer who advised the Solomon Islands at COP31. Others expect growing interest in agreements made outside the COP process, like the Santa Marta initiative, bringing together countries committed to phasing out fossil fuels. The process grew out of the COP30 meeting in Brazil, where interested nations sought to bypass a lack of consensus on the issue. Members held talks earlier this year, and agreed some first steps, including an expert scientific panel to advise on pathways. These “plurilateral processes are growing,” said Jotzo, and more are needed, including on carbon removal technologies and finance for adaptation. Bowen too said he sees the Santa Marta process becoming “increasingly important.” “Doesn’t replace COP. It’s just another way of doing it,” he added.

Any talks with India on terrorism must be 'evidence-based', says Musadik Malik

Federal minister Musadik Malik said on Sunday that any dialogue between Pakistan and India on terrorism must be “based on evidence”. Malik is in New Delhi on a three-day trip to attend the SCO Civilisational Dialogue Forum, making him the first Pakistani minister to visit the neighbour since former foreign minister Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari attended an SCO meeting in May 2023. Pakistan has repeatedly offered to hold talks with India to resolve outstanding disputes, including the water issue. In video clips shared by India’s ANI news agency on Sunday, Malik responded to queries from Indian media and stressed that he had repeatedly presented Pakistan’s stance before the reporters. “I am giving you a stable response that whenever discussions take place, they will be based on evidence, and they will certainly happen,” he affirmed, saying if New Delhi wanted a “lasting solution”. “You must also have some issues, [though] our issues are much greater than yours, and we also need a lot of evidence […] Whenever dialogue takes place, it will be based on evidence,” the climate change minister reiterated. Malik said Pakistan had repeatedly stated that evidence relating to terrorism could be presented through both bilateral and multilateral forums. “You may bring evidence; we also have evidence that we want to present,” he added. India’s foreign minister had said in June 2024 that his country wanted to “find a solution to the issue of years-old cross-border terrorism” with Pakistan. However, a military conflict between the neighbours last year intensified tensions and escalatory rhetoric from India. In May 2025, a four-day confrontation between Pakistan and India was sparked by an attack on tourists in occupied Kashmir’s Pahalgam, which New Delhi, without evidence, linked to Pakistan. Islamabad had strongly denied responsibility while calling for a neutral investigation. In another clip, Malik noted that India had invited a representative from Pakistan to attend the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation’s (SCO) Civilisational Dialogue Forum, a multilateral event. “I am your guest. You invited me to a specific forum; I am present before you. I answered all those questions you asked about this forum,” the minister said, remarking that he had conveyed the “voice of my heart” to the people across the region through the forum. Malik stated, “You are today asking me questions for which you did not invite me. When you invite me for that question, I will appear. However, you first ought to invite me and say ‘we want to speak bilaterally with you’.” ‘Will speak on IWT when invited to do so’ The minister was also asked about his remarks on the Indus Waters Treaty (IWT), but said he would comment on the matter when invited to do so. “You have currently invited me to speak on topics about which I have already spoken, that when temperatures rise, climate is destroyed, Glofs happen. Glofs and glaciers are blind, they do not know whether to fall on the right or the left,” he said, highlighting the shared threat climate change poses to both neighbours. He further said, “You have called me for culture and civilisation [discussions]. I am your guest, you are my host […] You have asked me the same question a 100 times and I have responded to it a 100 times.” In his remarks at the SCO forum on Saturday, Malik had termed climate change an “enemy” of civilisations and pointed out its impact on South Asian countries that bore the brunt of climate destruction. Water and the IWT remain a contentious issue between Pakistan and India, following New Delhi’s unilateral abeyance of the accord in April last year after the Pahalgam attack, which was followed by the four-day military conflict between the two sides. Following India’s move, Pakistan termed any attempt to suspend its water share an “act of war”, noting the IWT had no provision for unilateral suspension. It later argued that New Delhi’s action was a violation of the 1969 Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties. Backing Islamabad’s stance, the Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA) on Aug 31 ordered New Delhi to uphold the IWT, declaring that the treaty remained fully in force, as India had no justification for ending or suspending the agreement. The IWT, brokered by the World Bank in 1960, allocates the three western rivers — Indus, Jhelum and Chenab — largely to Pakistan, and the three eastern rivers — Ravi, Beas and Sutlej — to India. The treaty has survived wars, crises, and political tensions for more than six decades and is considered a rare example of durable water-sharing cooperation between the two neighbours.

His 48-member bloc, which needs 61 lawmakers to reach a majority, remains divided over whether to build a coalition through an alliance with ultra-Orthodox parties, a rapprochement with Arab parties, or a national unity government with Likud. Netanyahu is pushing for a national unity government, which could see a rotation of the premiership between the two men, a mechanism already tried in Israel. Such an arrangement could allow Netanyahu to keep his political protection against the corruption charges he faces, as well as continue to oppose the creation of an independent commission of inquiry into the security failures that preceded the Hamas raid of Oct 7, 2023. But that prospect runs up against Eisenkot’s refusal to serve in a cabinet led by Netanyahu, against whom he centred his campaign. His ally Naftali Bennett, a former prime minister, also reiterated on Saturday his refusal to join a coalition with “Netanyahu or his bloc, under any circumstances”. National ‘unity’? The US & India

A CRITICAL look at US-India relations is essential to understand US-Pakistan ties. It might help dispel a long-held public perception in Pakistan that US-India relations have somehow been a function of the ups and downs in US-Pakistan ties. And that in establishing the extraordinary relationship with India in the last couple of decades Washington has finally “chosen” India over Pakistan. The foreign policy establishment acknowledges the cyclic nature of US-Pakistan relations. But uplifted by the recent fissures in US-India ties, charmed by President Trump’s compliments, infused by an enduring faith in the US-Pakistan alliance, and confident of Pakistan’s coveted geopolitical salience, it remains ever optimistic that Washington would come back, and relations would be rekindled to their “halcyon” days. Both the public and official views need revision, based as they are on faulty assumptions that consider the two relationships as competitive and comparable. That is why one keeps hearing successive Pakistan leaders asking the US to treat the two countries equally, and arguing at home that Pakistan needs to keep a balance in relations with China and the US. This is an erroneous view. Indians forget Washington’s critical help in many areas. The problem is Pakistan cannot wean itself away from the pride of having been a preferred ally of the US once upon a time. But this notion refers to a world of the Cold War days in which the US had a single interest in South Asia, which Pakistan was capable and India was unwilling to advance. That South Asia is no more. There is a new world out there. Its complexity and disorder present a multiplicity of security and strategic challenges, its globalised economy offers exceptional opportunities, and its uncertainty compels countries to hedge, expand, and diversify their partnerships. In this world, India and Pakistan would both have roles for the US, though relations with Pakistan would be transactional and of limited bandwidth, and with India strategic and broad-based. These are two different relations, and neither derives from the strength or weakness of the other, or is based on “choosing” one over the other. Coming to US-India ties, beneath the trade dispute and H1-B visa frictions US-India strategic cooperation and economic relations continue to thrive. There is substantial collaboration on defence production, space, AI, and maritime security. In a recent piece, Sameer Lalwani of the Carnegie Endowment pointed out that trade rose to its highest level of $240 billion last year. And the new 10-year framework for the US-India Major Defence Partnership calls for advanced exercises and closer defence-industrial ties. Major US tech companies pledged nearly $68bn towards AI infrastructure investments in India last year. Micron, a leading semiconductor firm, opened its first assembly and test facility in India early this year. And recently Applied Materials and Lam Research announced investments in India’s semiconductor industry. Prime Minister Modi bruised President Trump’s ego by not acknowledging his role in the 2025 India-Pakistan ceasefire. But this has not impacted the substance of relations. Trump has not reversed ties; he has simply put a brake on them. As for tariffs, these have indeed caused strains but they are not India-specific; they are being applied across the board as Trump’s economic instrument and geopolitical weapon. The reality is he would not allow countries to become economic competitors and geopolitical rivals with America’s help and at its expense. US Deputy Assistant Secretary of State Christopher Landau said in his speech at the Rai­sina Dialogue ear­­ly this year that the US would not repeat mistakes made with China with India. India was so pa­­­mpered by Pre­­sident Biden and Trump in his first term that it came to have a sense of entitlement. Now it is complaining about losing its extra-special status. Indians forget Washington’s critical help in many areas. The US, for instance, has long supported Indian indigenous technology development. GE engines have been central to India’s domestic fighter jet industry. India’s first indigenous fighter aircraft Tejas relied on an F-16 testbed, and its development of a fifth-generation fighter relies on US wind tunnels. From Pakistan’s standpoint, what is im­­p­ortant is that US-India differences have not impinged on India’s importance for Wash­in­­gton in the US-China strategic competition. And it will likely endure. Any occasi­o­nal adjustment in it will not bring dividends for US-Pakistan ties. We need to delink the two relationships in our analysis. The bottom line: the US will continue to engage both India and Pakistan as there are no alternatives to each relationship. The alternative to India is India, and the alternative to Pakistan remains Pakistan. The writer, a former ambassador, is adjunct professor at Georgetown University. Problems and prescriptions

THE upward trajectory of the Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER) reading, which clocked in at 110 in October 2026, is a frequent subject of debate among economic commentators. Some argue that it shows the currency is being ‘artificially managed’, as was the case in past such episodes which usually preceded painful adjustments. Others have pointed out that the REER is just one indicator among several to assess the position of an economy’s health. An excellent piece by Dr Ishrat Husain on these pages lays out the latter’s position quite categorically: “REER has a legitimate but limited purpose. It measures changes in the nominal exchange rate adjusted for relative price movements against trading partners. But an index reading of 100, based on a selected base year, doesn’t establish an optimal exchange rate. Nor does a deviation from 100 automatically signal overvaluation or undervaluation. REER says little about balance-of-payments sustainability, foreign exchange availability from non-trade sources or exporters’ ability to compete through quality, technology, productivity, and delivery.” Knowledgeable experts will continue to weigh in on this issue, but it is worth looking at the exchange rate from the economy’s current vantage point. A minor current account surplus, a primary (fiscal) surplus, monthly central bank purchases, not sales, of dollars from the open market, increasing reserves, and a very narrow spread between the interbank and open market rate suggest there is nothing immediately scandalous about the current rate. The PKR’s value is where it is because the inflow of dollars has been robust on account of some export growth plus significant remittance inflows, positive real interest rates, selective administrative checks on imports, and the dollar’s own position against other currencies. FDI’s future looks uncertain under geopolitical fracturing and tariffs are loudly back on the menu. Taking a slightly broader timeframe, what is also clear is that at its existing level of productivity the Pakistani economy’s current rate of sustainable growth — ie growth that doesn’t blow up the external sector and/or cause domestic inflation — is around 4pc. Of this, 2.5pc is attributable to natural population growth, and the remaining is what the economy contributes by way of actual productivity. It is with this bleak baseline condition that many experts push for a gradual, enforced devaluation of the currency as a policy position. The argument here is that this downward flexibility is an important ingredient, among others, for export competitiveness. Given the government’s stated position of catalysing export-led growth, enforced depreciation along with import liberalisation appears to be the logical path to take. This prescription can be found with near-universal frequency in government documents, multilateral donor/lender documents, and in expert/technocratic commentary at any point over the last two decades. The fact that it is never acted upon in full is a political economy question. The government, in practice, prioritises some societal segments over others (importers over exporters), picks some objectives over others at different times (stabilisation versus growth), and chooses to inflict pain on some rather than others (who gets rents versus whose subsidies get taken away). But there is another cloud looming on the horizon of this prescription. What if the country has missed the boat of beneficial global trade integration altogether? The strategy of economic development of much of the Third World/Global South since the mid-20th century has gone through three phases: protectionist import-substituting industrialisation from the 1950s to the 1970s; structural adjustment and market-led liberalisation (starting in the 1980s); and dreams of integrating into global value chains (GVCs) through export-led strategies (from the 1990s onwards). Pakistan repeatedly pays homage to the third approach, but success remains limited at best. It has achieved reasonable integration in textiles and a few other sectors, but has little else to boast about. The wide range of conflicts that characterise its political economy have constrained baseline productivity to such a large extent that the country possesses or makes little of note that the world wants. Its natural advantage in cheap labour has been undone by poor human capital formation and other burdens, such as its energy sector and struggles with political violence. As a result, its industrial base remains shallow and growth remains anaemic. In the current moment, the prospects for favourable integration look even dimmer. If AI and robotics development continues at pace, the marginal cost of labour across the world will fall further. Why then would integrating Pakistan’s cheap and mostly unskilled labour into any value chain, at any level of sophistication, make sense? Bluntly put, where the world produces the things it consumes has already been decided to a very large extent. China is the factory of the world, and those economies functionally integrated with China’s factors (its South-East Asian partners) have a future in global trade. The rest of the developing world mostly does not. Simultaneously, we’re witnessing increased insistence on new domestically oriented industrial policies, which prioritise re-industrialisation and on-shoring. FDI’s future looks uncertain under geopolitical fracturing and tariffs are loudly back on the menu of policy options to protect industrial employment. This means that an emerging multipolar world order might see economic delinking rather than greater integration. All of this is to say that while the benefits of export-led growth are well known, the world in which export-led growth is possible for Pakistan may no longer exist. If this admittedly pessimistic reading is correct, does our current conversation have any space for a different type of economic strategy to shake off this anaemic 4pc per annum baseline? Do we, in effect, have a backup answer for how Pakistani labour and Pakistani capital can be productively combined to generate employment, reduce poverty, and improve human flourishing? A lopsided arrangement for KP?

• Since the imposition of governor’s rule, KP has committed Rs157bn a year to the Centre, a contribution the ousted PTI government had withheld • While Rs17.7bn has already been deducted from KP’s NFC share, the PM’s recently announced Rs160bn package for the province has yet to take shape ONE of the most significant developments during the first 43 days of emergency and governor’s rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa was the signing of a memorandum of understanding (MoU) with the federal government, which commits the province to an estimated annual cash grant of Rs157bn. The amount forms part of the Rs1.035 trillion in financial contributions sought by the federal government from the four provinces for national economic initiatives. Islamabad had been pressing for these contributions, but the dysfunctional chief minister, Sohail Afridi, had made KP’s contribution conditional on a meeting with his imprisoned party founder Imran Khan – a demand the federal government refused to entertain. The interim administration’s signing of the MoU has effectively provided formal cover for Rs17.7bn already deducted at source by the federal government from KP’s share under the National Finance Commission (NFC) award during the July-October 2026 period. With the agreement now in place, the federal government has secured KP’s contribution for the current fiscal year, marking one of the major financial outcomes of governor’s rule. While the province’s contribution to the Centre has been settled, little is clear about what flows the other way: the details of the prime minister’s Rs160bn package for KP, the funding gap in the merged districts and how federal resources will be divided between the two administrative structures created under governor’s rule. The Rs160bn question Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif announced Rs160bn for the province on Oct 22, with 70pc earmarked for the Area Development Secretariat (ADS), the new administrative unit for 12 districts. A senior official of the KP Finance Department said the provincial government had since received no formal word, nor any details regarding the proposed package. It remains unclear whether the package represents fresh funding or includes the Rs150bn already allocated for the newly merged districts (NMDs) in FY2026-27. The mechanism for releasing and distributing the funds has also not been explained. Adviser on Information Samar Haroon Bilour said 70pc of the announced resources would go to the 12 ADS districts and the remaining 30pc to the other 24 districts. But instead of providing additional financial assistance, the official claimed, the federal government had deducted more than Rs17bn from the province’s NFC share within four months. He maintained that KP had already generated a fiscal surplus in line with commitments made under the IMF programme and that, despite the deductions, the province had faced no significant pressure on the cash flows required to meet its current expenditure. “This shows that KP has maintained fiscal discipline and is not facing any shortage of funds,” the official said. Parallel set-ups? On Oct 30, the KP government issued a notification transferring the functions of designated officers to the ADS, while financial authority remained with the provincial Finance Department. The 12 districts have been placed under an additional chief secretary (ACS) acting as administrator, while the remaining 24 fall under Governor Faisal Kareem Kundi and his 11-member advisory council. Despite the creation of two parallel administrative structures, there is little clarity on how federal funds will be allocated between them. Under the five-page notification, the KP Human Resource Management Department transferred administrative control over designated officers and staff of 32 directorates and major institutions to the ADS. These officers now report to the respective ADS secretaries. However, the arrangement falls short of establishing a fully autonomous administration, as no independent directorates have been created, while the provincial chief secretary and Human Resource Management Department retain key powers over personnel transfers. Financial arrangements also remain under the KP Finance Department, which will handle budget allocations and financial authorisations in consultation with the ADS. The notification thus separates administrative functions without fully transferring financial, legal and personnel authority to the new secretariat. Former KP finance adviser Muzzammil Aslam, who oversaw the provincial Finance Department for two and a half years, said the status of the new administrative unit was still unclear. “If it is under federal government control, then definitely the provincial financial management needs adjustment,” he said. Former KP finance minister Taimur Jhagra said appointing an ACS and establishing a separate secretariat would make little difference, particularly when the new secretariat was based in Peshawar rather than in the area it was meant to administer. He said Prime Minister Shehbaz had still given no clarity on whether he was investing a single extra rupee of federal money in KP, even in the name of governor’s rule. Former KP home and tribal affairs secretary Dr Akhtar Ali Shah questioned the rationale for creating a parallel administrative structure, recalling that the federal government had exercised direct control over the tribal areas through designated officials under the Frontier Crimes Regulation (FCR), yet the law and order situation had continued to deteriorate. “It is well documented that a political agent in Waziristan could not even travel by car,” he said, referring to the security situation in 2006, when a parallel system of authority had emerged. Dr Shah questioned how another administrative structure would improve security when direct federal control had failed to prevent the killing of tribal chiefs. “If history is any guide, then listen to it,” he said, describing the new arrangement as an attempt to reinvent the wheel. The ousted PTI government had also claimed to have initiated work in June 2024, during the tenure of former chief minister Ali Amin Gandapur, on a separate administrative secretariat covering 11 districts across Kohat, Bannu and Dera Ismail Khan divisions, including the relevant merged districts. Ms Bilour credited Prime Minister Shehbaz with establishing the ADS. She acknowledged that the concept may have originated under the PTI government but maintained that the prime minister had implemented it. Transfers and liabilities The administrative division has brought KP’s unresolved financial arrangements into sharper focus. Since the 2018 merger, the province has maintained separate budgetary provisions for settled districts and the NMDs, whose expenditure continues to depend heavily on federal transfers. For FY2026-27, the federal government allocated Rs95bn for current expenditure and Rs55bn for development under the Annual Development Programme (ADP) and Accelerated Implementation Programme (AIP), totalling Rs150bn. However, the financing shortfall is estimated at Rs80bn to Rs100bn, which the province will meet from resources allocated to settled districts, a senior KP Finance Department official told Dawn. The official said development allocations for the NMDs declined from Rs69bn in FY24 to Rs52bn in FY25 before rising to Rs55bn in FY26. Current expenditure allocations increased from Rs72bn in FY25 to Rs80bn in FY26 and Rs95bn in FY27. According to the official, the NMDs’ estimated 2.5pc share under the NFC arrangement would amount to around Rs300bn. However, the absence of a dedicated allocation had deprived the tribal districts of these resources, a grievance repeatedly raised with Islamabad. Dr Shah argued that while security operations could restore the writ of the state, adequate fiscal transfers were necessary to strengthen state capacity. According to him, KP’s existing 14.62pc share under the NFC award was determined before the merger, when the population, area, poverty and development needs of the former tribal districts were not included in the province’s calculations. He said the province’s share should have increased to 19.46pc following the merger, but the additional resources had not been provided. Ms Bilour said the NMDs’ financial share and outstanding federal liabilities were discussed during the prime minister’s meeting at Governor House, where he directed the formation of federal and provincial committees to reconcile the claims. Mr Jhagra, however, doubted that the province would benefit. He argued that KP already contributed to the federation in four ways. “The rest of Pakistan benefits from our cheap hydro-energy, and from our oil and gas. Then, KP is leaving a Rs220 billion surplus. Then, it is giving Rs130bn on top of that to the Centre by force, for strategic spending,” he said. He added that, with a reduced NFC share based on the 1998 census, the Centre used KP’s NFC share to finance current and development expenditure in ex-Fata areas. “The federal grants for ex-Fata are short of current expenditure alone by Rs50bn. Shehbaz Sharif has solved none of this. So how will anything change?” he asked. Mr Jhagra also called for the restoration of trade with Afghanistan and Central Asia, saying it was in the interests of Pakistan and particularly of KP.
