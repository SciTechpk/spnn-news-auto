Democracy and the market

EVERY decade in Pakistan’s history has seen a moment when a robust defence of democracy as the best form of government for a country like ours is needed. The never-ending struggle for supremacy between the democratic and non-democratic forces make it necessary. One of the key ways in which non-democratic forces advance their argument is to amplify all the bad things that happen under democratic rule, and all the flaws that democratically elected leaders bring to their position. They advance the idea that a more selfless leadership will be better for the country since it will put the national interest above its own. This is the central argument which numerous non-democratic forces have used to try and justify their intervention in politics and governance. But there is a flaw in this premise. No leadership is selfless. Not in the political arena. Every contender for leadership emerges only by harnessing the powers inherent in society. Non-democrats, for example, have had the organised power of their respective institution behind them. And therefore, they have not been able to operate outside the limits set by their own affiliation with the institution that supplied them with the power to rule and to attack other institutions — which cannot, by any stretch of the imagination, be seen as selfless actions taken for the sake of the country. In fact, to use the words of Hamilton in the Federalist Papers — the founding documents of the American system — democracy is predicated on the idea that human beings, or at least those who seek power and thereby become players in the political arena, are “ambitious, vindictive and rapacious”. The idea that people (or at least those who enter the arena of politics) are inherently selfish, self-serving, rapacious and ambitious was ingrained in the philosophical traditions from which democracy arose. Hume wrote that, when devising a system of rule, “every man ought to be supposed a knave, and to have no other end, in all his actions, than private interest”. Montesquieu asserted that “[c]onstant experience shows us that every man invested with power is apt to abuse it … it is necessary from the very nature of things that power should be a check to power”. Kant took the argument even further, effectively saying that it should be possible to build a system of rule even for a nation of devils, because a rightly arranged constitution forces their selfish inclinations to check one another, producing an outcome identical to what good moral character would have produced. Both democracy and the market are designed to harness the worst motivations of individuals — towards the pursuit of the common good of all. The list goes on and on. Democracy is premised on the idea that rulers will bring bad motivations to their position, and it works on this premise to build a system where the separation of powers and the accountability and transparency that come with it will work to constrain these motivations, and channel them towards the collective good. This is one foundational element that democracy has in common with the other big idea that emerged at around the same time: the market as the organising principle of economic life. The market also assumes that human motivations are greed and self-interest. When we meet greedy and self-interested people, most of us are usually not impressed. But taken collectively, the idea behind the market was to harness these energies towards the pursuit of the collective good. Both democracy and the market are designed to harness the worst motivations of individuals — towards the pursuit of the common good of all. They aim to do this by pitting these bad motivations against each other and via a scheme of separation of powers where no single branch of government should exercise sway over the entire apparatus of state. Today, if there is any separation of powers left in the country, it is the federal versus the provincial governments. Little wonder that a demand materialises from silent places for undoing this as well. When one points to the defects of individual democratic rulers, parties or governments, one should remember that non-democratic forms of government are not going to be without defects of their own. But they will be better at ensuring their defects are not amplified in the national conversation, so the common citizenry is left with the impression that only democratic rule has defects. The bigger problem is that non-democratic rulers, meaning those who come to the political arena via a route other than the ballot box, have no checks on their motivations, because they usually stand above the separation of powers and all mechanisms of accountability that any political system has. As such, their self-serving motivations get a free, unchecked rein; our own history bears witness that their periods of rule ended up doing lasting damage to Pakistan. This is what must be kept in mind when working through the conversation these days around breaking up the provinces, or creating new ‘administrative units’. The first question to ask is ‘where is this demand coming from?’ Is it coming from below or above? There is a demand, for instance in south Punjab to create a new province and that demand has been acknowledged by all three major democratic parties in the country today. But where is the demand in the rest of the country? The bottom line is this: if the demand to restructure the federation is not coming from below, then it should not be pursued. The federation is not a toy to be played with, and not someone’s snack to be cut up into bite-sized nuggets because a handful of people think that by doing so some kind of benefit will follow. The report also points to growing operational and facilitation linkages among the TTP, Al Qaeda, Islamic State-Khorasan (ISIL-K) and the Baloch Liberation Army (BLA). The 38th report of the UN Analytical Support and Sanctions Monitoring Team, covering the period from Dec 16, 2025, to June 9, 2026, said that “cross-border attacks between Pakistan and Afghanistan escalated, provoked by a high number of attacks by Tehreek-i-Taliban Pakis­tan against Pakistan”. Like previous editions, the report was unequivocal in its assessment of Kabul’s role, saying that “the de facto authorities (Taliban) continued their support to the Tehreek-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP), which escalated attacks against Pakistan, provoking military exchanges”. It noted concern among member states that the continuing conflict could create opportunities for terrorist groups and generate additional security challenges for the region. Taliban’s dual-track approach The assessment nevertheless recorded some initial steps by the Taliban authorities to contain the TTP, although these appeared aimed more at managing the group than dismantling its infrastructure. The report said Afghan authorities had relocated around 2,000 TTP fighters and their families from areas along the Pakistani border to deeper inside Afghanistan in an effort to rein in the group. It also said Taliban leader Hibatullah Akhundzada had warned the TTP leadership through backchannel communications to halt attacks against Pakistan or risk losing Taliban support. The developments point to what appears to be a dual-track Taliban approach, continuing to provide the TTP space and support because of shared ideological ties while moving some fighters away from the border to reduce attacks on Pakistan, avoid a major escalation with Islamabad and address growing international concerns. For Pakistan, however, relocating fighters deeper inside Afghanistan would do little to address the underlying problem as long as the TTP’s command, training and logistical networks remain intact. Meanwhile, the TTP has continued to expand its organisational structure and operational reach. The report said that following its annual rahbari shura in December, the group announced a revised structure with two new divisions covering Balochistan and the central region, and Kashmir and Gilgit. The restructuring also included a new division described by the group as the “TTP Air Force”, suggesting an effort to broaden its operational capabilities. In March, the group announced in several languages its spring campaign, “Operation Khyber”, calling for increased attacks against Pakistani security forces and civilians. One of its deadliest attacks during the reporting period came on Feb 16 in Bajaur, where a vehicle-borne improvised explosive device martyred 11 security personnel and a child and wounded others. The TTP claimed responsibility and was reportedly operating from Afghan­istan, the report said. Blurred lines The UN assessment also points to increasingly blurred lines between terrorist groups operating in and from Afghanistan. Al Qaeda’s official media outlet, As-Sahab Media Foundation, issued a statement on April 28 supporting the Taliban and, for the first time, backing the TTP in its operations against Pakistan. The report said that while Al Qaeda had previously provided ideological guidance, training and support to the TTP, the latest statement represented more direct backing. “One member state reported that BLA terrorists were trained along with TTP by Al Qaeda trainers in Afghanistan,” it said. The report also noted that Al Qaeda in the Indian Subcontinent (AQIS) formed the backbone of Ittihadul Mujahideen Pakistan, which was reportedly involved in several attacks in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. AQIS leadership was reported to remain in Kabul after being issued tazkiras, suggesting “close cooperation between the Taliban and AQIS”, according to the report. The assessment thus points towards a wider militant ecosystem rather than an isolated TTP problem, with training, facilitation and logistical networks increasingly cutting across organisational boundaries. ISIL-K remains another major component of the threat. The report described it as the greatest threat to the Taliban authorities and the region, despite serious setbacks suffered as a result of counterterrorism operations by Pakistan and the Afghan Taliban. ISIL-K was reported to have moved its operational infrastructure towards northern Afghanistan while dispersing training networks across the Pakistan-Afghanistan border region, including Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and retaining a presence in the suburbs of Kabul. The UN report said ISIL-K was in a rebuilding phase, having shifted from attempts to hold territory to conducting fewer but high-profile, mass-casualty attacks, demonstrating resilience and adaptability. The group also claimed responsibility for the May 5 assassination of Pakistani cleric Sheikh Muhammad Idrees in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. The findings suggest that the threat facing Pakistan from Afghanistan is becoming more complex, with the TTP retaining Taliban ideological and logistical support, Al Qaeda providing training and ideological backing and ISIL-K and the BLA benefiting from overlapping facilitation networks. The Taliban’s reported relocation of TTP fighters and warning to its leadership may reduce immediate pressure along the border, but the UN assessment does not suggest that Kabul has taken the more consequential step of severing its relationship with the group. WHEN Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi recently declared that national security requires deep reforms, and ISPR publicly endorsed the call, both were arguing for the same things: coherence, coordination, service delivery pushed closer to the doorstep, a state that functions as a state should. Nothing wrong with that ambition. The problem is what it left out, and how familiar that omission is. Pakistan has set out for this destination before. Every serious reset since 1947 has restated some version of that list: Peace. Prosperity. Rule of law. A federation the provinces actually believe in. None of it was ever the wrong destination. What keeps happening is Odysseus’s wandering. His ship knows exactly where Ithaca lies and still takes the long way, blown off course by whichever storm is loudest that year. Each course correction adds distance instead of closing it. This is not a nation still discovering what it wants. It is a nation that has known the address for decades but keeps failing to arrive. Climate change is the clearest evidence of that failure, the one threat that has already reshaped the economy and governance more than any militant group, hostile neighbour, or political crisis since 2010. Yet, it barely features in the national security conversation beyond a meek reference in the 2021 National Security Policy. Pakistan’s own climate finance planning puts annual investment need at roughly $56.6 billion, one and a half times what defence and debt servicing cost combined, nearly 20 times the federal development budget. Climate investment is deferred, year after year, for the next government or generation to absorb. The reason is structural, not a matter of neglect. Hydrological volatility asks a different set of questions than the ones conventional threats ask. A flood breaches an embankment, and the people who decide whether that embankment holds are provincial irrigation engineers. An evacuation is run by a district administration working off exposure lists, not from provincial headquarters. Urban flooding is a municipal failure before it becomes a national one, a drainage line that was never cleared. The Constitution already assigns disaster management, water, agriculture and local infrastructure to the provinces. National security planning grew up answering more conventional and transboundary threats. A constitutional division of labour has put the actual climate mandate one or more levels below where the security doctrine is written. Each course correction adds distance instead of closing it. Naqvi’s critics have already converged on the obvious corrective, but have failed to offer a political economy analysis: why has local government never taken root? Pakistan has built it three times, Ayub’s Basic Democracies, Zia’s local bodies, Musharraf’s LGO 2000 — all three imposed by military leaders, all three the same design: an election without power, a base for the man at the top, no foundations underneath. Civilian governments never fixed this. The reign of top-down development has continued. Each time reform came up, provincial and federal legislators were lured by the discretionary schemes that let them decide what gets built and funded in their constituencies. A local politician’s power comes from controlling those schemes, and real local government would take that power away. This has replaced planning with ad hocism, turning much of the PSDP into an investment in random, uncoordinated allocations rather than resilience. Local government cannot consolidate, and climate vulnerability cannot be addressed, until this political economy architecture is dismantled. As long as politicians control allocations to their own constituencies, neither local government nor climate resilience gets to compound the benefits. Only the losses do. Pakistan’s fiscal policy has run the same choice for decades: squeezed development budget on terms set by lenders, or populist spending. Climate resilience loses either way, cut first under austerity because it produces no immediate political return, and starved again under populist spending because discretionary schemes absorb whatever fiscal space survives. Flood protection spending is patchy, addressed only after a flood has already caused the damage. Elite capture runs well past the PSDP and local government. The automobile sector sits behind tariff walls, shielded from competition. Circular debt in the hydrocarbon energy sector is never resolved, only refinanced at a higher cost each time. Independent fossil-fuel power producers collect capacity charges regardless of what the country actually uses, a guaranteed return baked into the same contracts feeding the circular debt spiral. Undervalued official real estate rates let landowners dodge capital gains and wealth tax, channelling investment into speculative housing societies rather than production. And government borrowing crowds out private credit: banks holding government debt earn a safe return and have little appetite left to lend to the SMEs that would otherwise finance flood-resistant infrastructure or NAP and NDC implementation, the same SMEs that take the direct hit when a flood wipes out inventory or halts production. None of this is hidden: protected manufacturers, power producers, landowners, and captive banks are confident that the system built to check them answers, in practice, to them. None of this invalidates the proposed reforms. But the reforms do not touch what has broken Pakistan’s economy and governance repeatedly, nor do they restore the local government capacity that decades of centralising incentive have deliberately eroded. Floods have destabilised the country more than insurgencies have. A weak district administration has cost more in a monsoon season than most internal security lapses cost in a year. A reform agenda that does not start there is not incomplete. It is aimed at the wrong problem, and it is one more course correction that adds distance instead of closing it. The demand for structural national security reform is correct. It will be a decades-long electoral process, building the capacity to manage administrative units before creating more of them.
