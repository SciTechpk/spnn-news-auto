Another crisis creeps up at Pims

ISLAMABAD: Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences (Pims) is on the verge of another crisis as supplies of free medicines for inpatients and other items might be discontinued due to procurement issues. The situation has become worse as not a single tender has been signed during the last six weeks, a senior officer said. However Executive Director (ED) Pims Prof Dr Tanwir Khaliq, who took charge of the post on Tuesday, said he had taken up the issue and would show zero tolerance when it came to providing facilities to patients. A senior doctor of the hospital, requesting anonymity, said issues of procurement of different supply items were increasing. Free medicine supply to inpatients likely to be discontinued any time “In Pims medicines and injectables are given free of cost to all indoor patients and for that continuous supply of medicines has to be ensured. However tenders could not be approved during the last six months due to which supplies have been stopped and the available stocks are rapidly depleting,” he said. It is worth mentioning here that after the fire incident at Pims, in which 14 newborn babies had died on August 26 and later the 15th baby also passed away, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif took notice and suspended as many as eight officers. Unlearned lessons

THE talks between the government and the opposition were doomed from the outset, even though the fact that the two sides sat face to face at the negotiating table was a positive sign. The meeting took place under the shadow of the PTI’s threatened long march to Islamabad, while the government brandished the option of governor’s rule in KP. The tension was palpable. The capital was sealed off with containers and the highways were blocked. Days before the talks, the military leadership warned against any repeat of a May 9-like situation — a clear message to the opposition not to cross the red line. Some opposition leaders, for their part, wanted a negotiated deal to bring down the political temperature. The prolonged discussions suggested a measure of seriousness on both sides, but mutual distrust and brinkmanship blocked any breakthrough. The two sides gave contrary accounts of why the talks collapsed after a marathon sitting. The government contended that the opposition backed out after agreeing to a final draft; the other side denied this, saying the differences were too wide to bridge. That so-called final draft is not in the public domain, but two major points of difference appear to have defeated the agreement. The PTI insisted on a meeting with Imran Khan, while the government wanted the KP provincial government to give unqualified support to the counterterrorism operation being carried out by the security forces. While conceding to the opposition’s demand that the former prime minister be allowed to meet his family and his lawyers, as ordered by the Islamabad High Court, and agreeing to implement the Supreme Court order to arrange his medical treatment, the government was not prepared to let PTI leaders meet and consult him before finalising the agreement — a condition it found unacceptable. The prevailing political instability has provided greater space to the militants. The real cause of the stalemate, however, was the issue of provincial government support, and taking ownership of a planned counterterrorism military operation in five southern districts, including the merged Fata regions. For the provincial government this was an extremely tricky matter: it had strongly opposed any kinetic operation involving the displacement of the population, and the bitter memories of earlier operations had created intense public opposition to any such action. A multiparty meeting called by the provincial government early this year — whose participants included provincial legislators belonging to PML-N, PPP and ANP — had unanimously opposed any large-scale military operation. Considering this, it was hard for the PTI to take ownership of a highly unpopular action. It may be true that the PTI has no clarity over how to combat the menace of terrorism that has swept a large part of the province. Its inaction, often seen as capitulation, has added to the problem. The ill-equipped police and Counter Terrorism Department have been a major target of militants, resulting in a virtual collapse of the administration in the troubled districts. That inaction could also be attributed to the ongoing confrontation between the PTI and the establishment-backed federal government. Then there is the question of whether we have a coherent, overarching national security policy and counterterrorism strategy to deal with this existential threat. There has rarely been any debate in parliament on internal security policy. It is all left to the security establishment, with hardly any policy input from the civilian leadership. KP has witnessed a strong resurgence of militancy over the last five years, which has claimed the lives of thousands of civilians and security personnel. Last year saw the highest number of terrorist attacks in the province since 2013, despite claims of successful intelligence operations that killed hundreds of terrorists. The prevailing political instability has provided greater space to the militants, who are now better equipped and better organised, and who have extended their activities to other parts of the province. Pakistan has blamed Afghanistan’s Taliban regime for providing sanctuaries and aiding the TTP, and each major terrorist attack is now answered by Pakistani strikes across the border. The closure of the border and of trade between the two countries over the past year has directly affected the local population and added to the tension between Peshawar and Islamabad. There is a serious disconnect in the policy the establishment pursues in dealing with terrorism, and the government needs to address those concerns. There are no two views about the imperatives of a national security policy, but there is also a need to bring all stakeholders on board for an overarching policy that should not rest entirely on kinetic operations. It is important to address the social and economic issues in order to win public support in the affected areas. The widening trust gap between the state and the people, and the politics of confrontation, are the biggest hurdles in combating terrorism. With little hope of the two sides returning to the negotiating table, the confrontation has now escalated: the PTI has begun its protest march from the southern KP districts. It is apparent that the PTI has changed its strategy of marching directly on Islamabad. Instead, the party now plans to mobilise its supporters in the province and camp close to the capital rather than march into the city. It is the first instance of a chief executive leading a protest that paralyses his own province. Meanwhile, the federal government is considering removing the PTI government and imposing a state of emergency or governor’s rule in the province. That would be an extremely dangerous move in a province already facing terrorist attacks, rising nationalism and tension on its border. Any such move by a state already fighting a separatist movement in Balochistan and a revolt-like situation in Azad Kashmir would be disastrous. The province is sitting on a powder keg, and the removal from power of an elected government that enjoys an overwhelming majority in the provincial assembly and broad popular support could ignite it in a way the state would struggle to extinguish. We never learn lessons from our own history, and make the same mistake again and again and expect different results. Saudi deployment

WITH confirmation that members of the Makkah Defence Alliance will be deploying their forces to Saudi Arabia, it appears that the kingdom is preparing for a full-scale war with the pro-Iran Houthi Ansarallah movement. Senior civil and military leaders from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkiye met in Riyadh on Monday to chart a course forward. Though details are sparse, the three defence allies agreed to ensure “rapid deployment in the Kingdom, in accordance with … the national legislation of the parties.” As collective defence is a central pillar of the trilateral alliance, such developments were expected. Members of the MDA also condemned the attacks on Saudi Arabia, particularly alleged attempts to target the holy cities in Hejaz. It is hoped that, from Pakistan’s perspective, these developments only reflect a commitment to defend Saudi Arabia from outside aggression. It would be ill-advised for Pakistan, Turkiye or any other external actor to jump into the Yemeni quagmire. Pakistan has decades-old defence ties to the kingdom, and the pact only reflects a solidification of this relationship. The defence of the holy cities is also a sensitive matter, and Pakistan has in the past contributed troops to protect them. But getting dragged into the Yemeni civil war — which has pit Ansarallah against the Yemeni government, backed by Saudi Arabia — is another story altogether. There was considerable pressure on Pakistan in 2015 from Riyadh to get involved in its operation against the Houthis. Pakistan, thanks to the collective wisdom of parliament, at that time had wisely stayed away from any such undertaking. Similar wisdom is required at this juncture. This country values its deep ties with Saudi Arabia, and should do all it can to protect the kingdom from external aggression. But putting Pakistani boots on the ground in Yemen should be avoided. The fighting in Yemen, both between Ansarallah and the government, as well as between the Houthis and the Saudis, has intensified. At this point chances of a ceasefire seem remote. This conflict has been raging since 2015, and previous external attacks, including by the US and Israel, have failed to dislodge the Houthis. To prevent a fresh humanitarian catastrophe within Yemen, as well as the spread of this conflict across the region, there is an immediate need to end the hostilities. States such as Oman, which has channels open both with the Houthis as well as Saudi Arabia, can perhaps play a mediatory role. The basic requirements of a truce should include an end to all attacks on Saudi Arabia, free passage through Bab al-Mandeb, as well as a halt to all external attacks across Yemen. Only a political process led by Yemenis, and acceptable to all factions in the country, can bring internal peace, as well as guarantee the security of neighbouring states. US Vice President Vance says Iran must cut enrichment to end war

US Vice President JD Vance has said in an exclusive interview with Reuters that Iran must make a meaningful reduction in its nuclear enrichment capacity to satisfy US demands and end the country’s seven-month war, even as Washington was unsure how Tehran makes decisions in negotiations. Vance, a longtime sceptic of US involvement in foreign wars, has taken a leading role in negotiating a settlement to the conflict and brokered a provisional peace agreement in June that quickly fell apart. The war has jolted global energy markets and become a political liability for President Donald Trump and fellow Republicans ahead of November midterm elections. Speaking on Monday after a campaign trip in Alaska, Vance said any agreement would require Iran to reduce its nuclear enrichment capacity, rather than offer assurances of future nuclear drawdowns. “If you don’t want a nuclear weapon, then why do you need 60 per cent enriched fuel? And if you want to show commitment to not building a nuclear weapon, don’t build highly enriched fuel. That’s a very basic threshold issue,” Vance said. “I think if they want to show a commitment to not building a nuclear weapon, they would do something meaningful on their enrichment capacity.” Iran and the US have exchanged proposals in recent weeks aimed at ending the war and reopening the Strait of Hormuz, a vital global energy shipping route. Trump rejected Tehran’s latest offer and has warned that military operations could intensify after the midterms. Vance said the US remained open to an agreement but would require concrete Iranian nuclear concessions. “We’re not going to trade words for actions,” Vance said. Unclear how Iran makes decisions Complicating matters, Vance said Washington is uncertain who makes decisions in Tehran. Vance said the US was in talks with Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi. But it was unclear how much authority they wield within Iran’s power structure, he said, after joint US-Israeli strikes at the start of the war assassinated Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei. “One of the things we picked up on is it’s not totally clear how their country makes decisions,” Vance said. Separately, Trump said on Tuesday his “biggest problem” is no one knows who is running Iran. Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesperson said on Sunday that US officials are well aware of who their Iranian counterparts are and how Iran’s decision-making system works. “The problem is actually the opposite: it lies in the contradictory positions and mixed messaging from US officials,” Esmaeil Baqaei told a weekly press conference. Vance told Reuters he has held personal conversations with field commanders to gauge battlefield conditions. He dismissed concerns over critical munitions shortages and added that while Iranian troops are periodically able to strike commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz, they are largely unable to prevent energy exports through the waterway. Vance declined to say if he was leading peace talks, though he is frequently cited as the White House’s top negotiator. “We’re certainly involved,” he said. “Ultimately, the president of the United States is driving things.” The war and its $3 billion monthly price tag have become a key issue on the campaign trail. Higher energy prices have put Trump and Republicans on the defensive in competitive states that will decide control of Congress. Vance has sought to temper criticism of Trump’s handling of the conflict, telling voters they should not let that issue keep them from backing Republicans in next month’s elections. In Alaska, Vance rallied for Republican Senator Dan Sullivan, who is locked in a tight reelection battle with Democratic challenger Mary Peltola. Trump carried Alaska by 13 percentage points in 2024, but rising everyday costs have put the state in play. Before speaking with Reuters, Vance focused much of his speech on affordability concerns, especially around energy. Alaska’s remote locale makes consumer goods more expensive, and energy production is a key industry. Rally attendees roared when Sullivan nearly introduced Vance as the president of the US, rather than the vice president. Vance is seen as a leading Republican contender to succeed Trump in the White House. Vance told Reuters afterwards that he had not decided whether to seek the presidency in 2028. “I’m inherently a procrastinator,” he said. “I really haven’t thought about it much.”

Dual citizen? Debarred

IT was a Friday afternoon in May 2019. State Bank of Pakistan Governor Tariq Bajwa was in Islamabad with the IMF mission, flanked by his technical team I led. Unbeknownst to him, his successor was already in the country. By evening, Bajwa had resigned. The finance secretary at the time told me a new governor would be appointed by nightfall: Reza Baqir, the IMF’s resident representative in Egypt, already in town and ready to assume charge. The notification followed the next day, a Saturday. Within 24 hours, over a weekend, a serving IMF official had been installed at the helm of the central bank, as abruptly as Bajwa’s own 2017 appointment had followed a rupee devaluation. Opacity is not the only problem; law compounds it. The 2022 amendments to the SBP Act, while granting welcome operational autonomy, also imposed restrictions found nowhere in the IMF’s own safeguards assessment: an outright bar on dual nationals, and a requirement that a candidate completes a full five-year term before turning 65. Together, these eliminate precisely the cohort best placed to lead the central bank, favouring insiders over competence. Contrast this with how Britain appoints the governor of the Bank of England: open international advertisement, a shortlist, ministerial interviews, and months of public notice before the transition begins. That is the process that produced Andrew Bailey to succeed Mark Carney in March 2020. In Pakistan, favoured candidates are installed within a day, while everyone else waits months, even years, for a vacancy the law says must be filled within 30 days. Mark Carney’s trajectory sharpens the point further. A Canadian citizen who also held Irish citizenship through family ancestry, he was appointed governor of the Bank of England in Nov 2012, and ran UK monetary policy and financial stability for nearly seven years, through the 2016 Brexit shock. In 2018, while still governor, he acquired British citizenship too, holding three nationalities as custodian of Britain’s currency, with no credible case that he favoured Canadian or Irish interests over British ones. Central banking is one of the few institutions where competence, not compliance, should be the only test. Stanley Fischer, author of three of the most influential economics textbooks, and former first deputy managing director of the IMF, served as vice chair of the US Federal Reserve from 2014 to 2017, while holding two citizenships, from age 70 to 74. More recently, the Reserve Bank of New Zealand appointed Dr Anna Breman, a Swedish national, as its first female governor. Dr Breman’s appointment in Aug 2025 followed a rigorous recruitment process, that even involved a global executive search firm. Baqir is the more contested recent case, but the criticism was never really about a passport; it was that an IMF insider negotiating a Pakistani programme sat ‘on both sides of the table’, a concern about institutional capture, not nationality. The consequences have been predictable. The governor’s post fell vacant in May 2022 and took over three months to fill, breaching the SBP Act’s 30-day requirement; a deputy governor’s post vacant since Nov 2024 was filled only in Aug 2026. Leadership has increasingly gone to candidates with modest qualifications, and qualified economists are either sidelined or leaving the SBP, a brain drain that is entirely self-inflicted. Brain drain is estimated to cost Pakistan roughly $4.2 billion annually. Pakistan’s economy, sustained by remittances, external borrowing, real estate speculation, and import-led consumption rather than exports, demands sophisticated leadership, not a bureaucratic placeholder chosen for compliance. Article 63(1)(c) bars dual nationals from parliament since an elected official answers to voters as the sole political principal, and a second citizenship competes with that loyalty. A central bank governor is not elected, but appointed, and constrained by statute, audit, parliamentary review, and a monetary policy committee, an accountability built on expertise, not a mandate. That is why dual nationality has long been lawful for the wider civil service, precisely because competence, not electoral legitimacy, is what those roles select for. A 2025 Senate report counted roughly 2,365 civil servants or their spouses holding dual nationality under that lawful regime. Extending the Article 63 logic to appointed posts applies a rule built for one office to a fundamentally different one. The government’s 2026 policy, read past the headline, already leans this way: the Civil Servants (Disclosure and Regulation of Foreign Nationality) Rules require employees to disclose foreign citizenship annually, treating concealment, not citizenship itself, as misconduct. It is a disclosure regime, not a blanket bar. A second passport is often acquired for reasons of economics and convenience, educating children, or freedom to travel, not disloyalty. The genuine concerns — divided loyalty, foreign leverage, capture by a foreign employer — are real, and better addressed by targeted tools than a passport count: disclosure of foreign assets, recusal where a conflict is shown, and cooling-off periods after work for a foreign government or an institution like the IMF. The US Federal Reserve applies this to its officials. It also catches the single-passport holder who has taken money from a foreign government, someone a citizenship test would wave through. Pakistan need not start from scratch. In 2022, the prime minister notified the ISI as the Special Vetting Agency for public office holders. The mechanism only needs streamlining to run on specific safeguards, not opaque “cleared” or “not cleared” verdicts that strip the federal government’s own statutory appointment authority. It is time to remove the dual-nationality bar and age restrictions on appointments to the governor, deputy governor, and Monetary Policy Committee positions. Central banking is one of the few institutions where competence, not compliance, should be the only test. The question worth asking of any candidate is not how many passports are in their drawer, but whether their record and enforceable safeguards assure they will act in the country’s interest. A passport count answers none of that. Performance, integrity, and disclosure do.
