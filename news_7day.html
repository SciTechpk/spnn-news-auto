Reading Thucydides in the wolf world

The term Thucydides’s Trap has caught on since Professor Graham Allison wrote his 2017 book, Destined for War: Can America and China Escape Thucydides’s Trap? The core concept comes from Greek soldier-historian Thucydides’s History of the Peloponnesian War who identifies the structural stress that occurs when a major rising power is perceived to threaten to displace an established power. My intention here is not to explain Allison’s work or his treatment of the trap but to point to how modern-day scholars and policymakers cherrypick episodes from Thucydides’s work to justify their own approaches. I believe, as the post-World War II international security architecture unravels and we enter what some describe as the wolf world, that it is important to analyse Thucydides’s history in view of the book’s hermeneutics rather than picking up episodes that suit one’s own predilections. Thucydides arrived in the United States policy corridors with Dr Henry Kissinger. He had taught the text at Harvard and was keenly familiar with it. But upon joining Richard Nixon’s administration in the White House, he sought fit to focus on the Melian Dialogue, colouring outside the lines. The one line, “The strong exact what they can; the weak concede what they must,” became the slogan of Cold War strategies. Kissinger knew that Thucydides message was far more nuanced and he was not justifying imperial overreach. But that was for the classroom. The world Kissinger had to tackle was one in which ‘man was wolf to man,’ homo homini lupus of Plautus popularised by Thomas Hobbes. That misrepresentation has generally informed US policymakers since then. Now that cruder minds are ruling America and the precepts of International Law, for whatever they were worth, have been defenestrated and America’s foreign policy has become a Kabuki theatre, a few words about Thucydides’s work are in order. The structural architecture of History of the Peloponnesian War relies on a psychological arc that connects the Plague of Athens, the Melian Dialogue (nearly 14 years later) and the Sicilian Expedition (a year later). Far from putting down a simple chronicle of military manoeuvres, Thucydides tracks the internal rot of the Athenian psyche through the description of the Plague. By tracing this trajectory, the reader discovers that the moral dissolution initiated by the plague directly fostered the intellectual hubris that were to lead to Melos, which inevitably culminated in the material ruin of the Sicilian campaign. The foundational fracture occurs in Book Two with the Plague (430 BCE). Thucydides describes a city where the sudden, indiscriminate terror of death obliterates law (nomos) and religious restraint. Faced with immediate mortality, citizens abandon honour for instant gratification, arguing that the virtuous and the wicked die alike. This lawlessness (anomia) institutionalises a new societal ethos: the abandonment of traditional justice in favour of self-interest and survival. The plague does not merely kill bodies; it extinguishes the moral constraints of the polis. Might and appetite replace civic virtue. The plague, Thucydides tells us, also claimed Pericles and his family. That death, within the hermeneutics of the text, becomes a metaphor for the demise of nomos. The emergence of more populist and aggressive figures, such as Cleon, who prioritised personal popularity and raw emotion over steady strategy, comes to symbolise anomia. This internalised lawlessness manifests later as aggressive foreign policy in the Melian Dialogue (416 BCE) of Book Five. But the seeds of Melos were sown earlier. Two years after Pericles’ death, Athens had the Mytilenian debate between Cleon and Diodotus. Cleon fiercely argued in favour of executing the entire population of Mytilene to send a message of terror. While Diodotus narrowly persuaded the assembly to reverse the decision to execute all citizens, the Athenians still exacted a brutal and bloody punishment on the city. At Melos, the Athenian envoys articulate a chilling political realism, famously declaring that, “the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.” This worldview seems to be the direct philosophical descendant of the plague’s nihilism. Having lost their respect for divine law and cosmic justice during the pestilence, the Athenians now claim that the rule of the stronger is a universal law of nature. The moral decay hardened into intellectual hubris. Athens no longer seeks to justify its empire through justice or leadership, but purely through raw, unyielding power. The immediate narrative pivot to the Sicilian Expedition (415–413 BCE) in Book Six reveals this hubris in action, dramatised through the opposing speeches of Nicias and Alcibiades. Nicias represents the ghost of old Athenian moderation. He desperately warns the assembly against overreach, cautioning that they are leaving enemies behind to chase a massive, unknown island. But the plague-damaged psyche of Athens, now driven entirely by imperial appetite and the illusion of invincibility, cannot tolerate restraint. Instead, they rally behind Alcibiades (notice the shift from the Cleon-Diodotus debate). Alcibiades is brilliant, reckless, and entirely devoid of civic responsibility. He appeals to the city’s insatiable desire for expansion, arguing that an empire must grow or it will wither. Ultimately, Thucydides’s narrative acts as a tragic warning. The lawlessness born in the plague pits became the strategic blindness that was to mock the Melians and finally drove Athens to sail into the Sicilian trap. In Sicily, the inversion is completed. The Athenians are utterly destroyed, reduced to the same desperate reliance on “hope” and suffering that they had ridiculed at Melos. Through this connection Thucydides demonstrates that the real danger of power overreach is not always external opposition but the internal erosion of the moral and intellectual clarity required to understand the limits of power. They inherited the same interim constitution — the Government of India Act, 1935 — which each adapted to fit its own context. Both countries chose a parliamentary form of government. Pakistan adopted a more explicit federal character, while India shied away from calling itself a federation. Instead, it opted to describe itself as a ‘union with some federal characteristics’. Probably, India’s more diluted form of federalism helped it carve an uncomplicated path for creating new provinces: a simple majority in parliament and a non-binding consultation with the concerned states. On the other hand, Pakistan’s 1973 Constitution made the creation of new provinces contingent upon the passage of a resolution in the concerned provincial assembly or assemblies by a two-thirds majority before the bill for new provinces could be tabled in parliament and passed in the two Houses by a two-thirds majority. This set the bar very high for altering the boundaries of the provinces as compared to India. Around the time of Partition, undivided India consisted of 14 provinces and 587 princely states, of which two provinces (Bengal and Punjab) were partitioned. Both India and Pakistan received a part of each province. Three provinces — Sindh, the North-West Frontier Province (now Khyber Pakhtunkhwa) and British Balochistan — joined Pakistan, taking the tally of provinces in India and Pakistan to 11 and five respectively. Thirteen princely states acceded to Pakistan. The rest either voluntarily acceded to India or were coerced into doing so through brute military force. Kashmir has remained a disputed territory ever since. The reorganisation of Indian states was triggered by language riots after a political activist, who had been demanding a separate state from Madras for Telugu-speaking people, died in 1952 following a fast unto death. When the riots spread, the Government of India fast-tracked the creation of the Telugu-speaking Andhra state (later called Andhra Pradesh) out of Madras state. The demands for more states based on language spread. The Indian government constituted a three-member States Reorganisation Commission in December 1953, headed by a former judge of the supreme court. As Pakistan debates new provinces, India’s experience offers relevant insights. The commission, constituted to propose the redrawing of state boundaries, also came to be known as the Justice Fazal Ali Commission after the chairman. Its proceedings continued for about 21 months and it submitted its report in September 1955. Parliament finally passed the States Reorganisation Act in 1956, leading to the redrawing of boundaries and the creation of 14 states and six union territories. Language was a major consideration but not the only one in the creation of the states. The creation of the new states continued even after the enactment of the States Reorganisation Act. Four years later, Bombay state was split into the Gujarati-speaking Gujarat state and the Marathi-speaking Maharashtra. After persistent demands by the tribes, Nagaland was carved out of Assam state in 1963 because of its distinct language and culture. Indian Punjab was split into three parts, with Punjabi-speaking Punjab, Hindi-speaking Haryana and the hilly northern tracts merged into Himachal. In all, 15 new states were created or upgraded from union territories after the enactment of the States Reorganisation Act, 1956. In the meantime, some areas were downgraded to union territories; the latest such move saw the illegal splitting of Indian-occupied Jammu and Kashmir into the two ‘union territories’ of Ladakh and Jammu & Kashmir in 2019. The latest tally is 28 states and eight union territories. As stated earlier, most of the new states and union territories were created on the basis of identity such as language, ethnicity, culture and geography, or the difference in the level of development in two parts of a state. There is hardly a case where a state was created just because of its large population. Uttar Pradesh, with an estimated population of over 243 million, is a case in point. The state’s population almost matches the population of Pakistan. The population of the US has increased 10 times since 1863, but not a single state has been carved out of the existing ones since then. The last state created this way was West Virginia, which had seceded from Virginia in 1863 during the Civil War. What are the lessons from the two-country case studies above? The first is that population on its own is not enough of a reason to split a province into two or more units. Pakistan’s population has increased four times since 1973, but this alone may not justify the creation of more provinces. There may be merit in the proposal to create more provinces, but such reorganisation should be studied in depth, preferably by a commission or even a parliamentary committee as was done when the 18th Constitutional Amendment was being drafted. The concentration of too many powers in the provinces is a key issue that needs to be addressed. Creating more provinces may not be the best solution for this problem as it will not only be an expensive exercise but may also create political instability in one or more provinces. The problem, in my opinion, may be solved by creating a balance of powers among the three tiers of the state — federal, provincial and local. This balance can be effectively achieved by empowering local government through a comprehensive constitutional amendment, as India did in 1993. The LGs already have elaborate infrastructure and staff, unlike the proposed new provinces. The proposed amendment should not only guarantee adequate powers for various tiers of local government but should also ensure the direct transfer of funds to LGs from the National Finance Commission. The beneficiaries of war

WARS are generally assessed through a binary lens. Strategic analysts, policymakers and the media tend to focus on a simple question — who won and who lost? Wars do not merely produce military winners and losers. In the case of the US-Iran conflict, the most obvious beneficiaries may ultimately be neither Washington nor Tehran. The two capitals have both paid a substantial price. They have expended military resources, suffered economic disruption and experienced varying degrees of tactical and strategic pain. The conflict may therefore produce no clear geopolitical victor. Yet several sectors have emerged as unmistakable commercial winners. The foremost beneficiary of the US-Iran conflict has been the defence industry. Modern wars consume extraordinary quantities of missiles, interceptors, drones, precision-guided munitions and other military equipment. Every missile fired has to be replaced. The financial results of major US defence companies illustrate this dynamic. Several sectors have emerged as unmistakable commercial winners in the US-Iran conflict. Lockheed Martin reported earnings of $1.8 billion in the second quarter of 2026, compared with $342 million during the same period in 2025. The conflict between the US and Iran has resultantly become a multi-year procurement cycle for these firms. Although, decisions on war and peace are determined by governments, it is equally difficult to ignore the political economy associated with prolonged warfare. This raises an uncomfortable question: if war creates exponential profits for some industries, would they prefer the settlement of conflicts or their prolonged continuation?
