Govt rebuts claims of cancelling power-generation component of Diamer-Bhasha Dam

The Power Division on Saturday refuted a report that claimed that the government had cancelled the power-generation component of Diamer-Bhasha Dam. The Diamer-Bhasha Dam project is located on the Indus River near Gilgit-Baltistan. It is set to have a gross water storage capacity of 8.1 MAF and an installed power generation capacity of 4,500 megawatts. In a post on X on Saturday, the energy ministry’s Power Division termed the report by Brief, a reader-supported publication, as “fake news”. It affirmed that the “Diamer Bhasha Dam generation component is a committed project in both existing and future IGCEP (Indicative Generation Capacity Expansion Plan) documents”. Brief cited what it claimed was a leaked government document from the Economic Affairs Division dated early this year. The outlet quoted a source and claimed that the government’s decision was based on “lobbying” by independent power producers (IPPs) who got “vast sums even when their plants produce little or no power”. “Pakistan already has too much generating capacity”, the report cited the document as saying, adding the dam’s 4,500-megawatt hydropower complex could be “built at some unspecified later date while Diamer-Bhasha can be built up as a water reservoir for now”. In its rebuttal, the Power Division clarified that the “new IPPs business has permanently closed in Pakistan after the establishment of a free electricity market by the present government”. “Any attempt to link IPPs with this project and sensationalise it is based on nothing but fake assumptions,” the division stated.

There are three things in life that are certain: death, taxes, and English skies waiting for Test cricket to release the worst of their wrath. In the middle of England’s hottest summer on record, this felt like tradition reimposing itself; some things are sacred and rain during cricket is one of them. It takes real conviction to be as gloriously underwhelming as Pakistan have been this entire tour of England so far, but day two in London might be the most abysmal performance we have seen from the Green Shirts in a long time. There is nothing new left to add in these routine assessments of Pakistan’s losses, and most who have watched the team over most of the last few years can articulate the reasons off the top of their head. The management needs a reset, the domestic system needs an overhaul, the batting needs work, the bowling needs fire and discipline, and the fielding, before anything, needs to show up. But, with the sheer obstinance that only watching Pakistan play cricket can grant, I am going to try anyway. The bowling was slightly more incisive than the first Test. England’s quartet of Ollie Robinson, Jofra Archer, Josh Tongue and Gus Atkinson, proved to be matchless. The side’s persistent issues of getting the tail out persisted, with the last wicket partnership amounting to 47 runs. CBAM trade barriers

WHILE Pakistan struggles with the accumulated cost of climate inaction and finds international climate finance elusive, it must now confront an emerging challenge: trade barriers priced according to the carbon embedded in each exported product. The false comfort that this does not touch our key exports to the EU and UK has deferred reforms, with the cost growing steeper by the day. Contradictory policy, misusing carbon levies instead of building a domestic Emissions Trading System (ETS), has made trade even harder. Since January 2026, EU importers of steel, cement, aluminium, fertilisers and electricity have had to buy certificates for the carbon embedded in their imports, with the first annual declarations due next year. Direct exposure under these sectors is small for Pakistan — roughly 1.2 per cent of total exports, typically a percentage easy to ignore. CBAM (Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism), however, is a serious warning. On March 9, 2023, my column on ‘Trade and climate adaptation’ in this paper flagged that Pakistan would need to build carbon labelling and export competitiveness around it, not wait it out. As I wrote then, “they are expected to shift demand towards less carbon-intensive products”. Some three years later, in January 2026, my argument turned from a warning to heed to an opening to seize, urging that CBAM “should be seen as an incentive to accelerate our own decarbonisation”. Two columns, three years apart — but one constant: Pakistan’s institutions have moved slower than the mechanism they were warned about. Pakistan’s exports to the EU reached $8.86 billion in FY25, up from $8.24bn the year before, with textiles, which accounted for the largest share of that total, driving the growth. That concentration has real addresses: major textile houses in Faisalabad, Lahore and Karachi — employing thousands of workers and supplying top brands in the EU — are directly exposed in the coming scope expansion. Pakistan’s economic set-up is structured to fund CBAM via citizens’ fuel bills while getting no credit. In the sectors that CBAM already covers, the exposure affects heavy industry: leading domestic cement makers supply most of that sector’s output, with the steel mills in Karachi making up the remainder. The disproportionately higher share, however, sits in textiles, concentrated in three industrial cities that would absorb the shock first. Pakistan already collects carbon levies. CBAM allows a deduction: a home-country carbon price already paid can be subtracted from the EU’s border levy. It is the mechanism’s one window of opportunity — a domestic carbon price that is real, product-linked and verifiable. Pakistan’s misplaced levies cannot qualify because they aren’t earmarked; the revenue funds the deficit, not the industries that generated it. They are regressive, taxing fuel and transport consumption rather than signalling to producers. And they carry no product-level accounting, so a generalised fuel levy tells Brussels nothing about the carbon intensity of a bale of cotton yarn or a tonne of billet steel. Without that link, CBAM won’t recognise it as a carbon price. The cost will fall twice: first domestically into a levy that does nothing for decarbonisation, and then again at the EU border into a certificate purchase. Pakistan’s economic set-up is structured to fund CBAM out of its own citizens’ fuel bills while getting zero credit for it. This has long been a shortcut for securing lucrative civil service careers, even as compulsory military attachment for civil probationers usefully builds familiarity with the defence forces. The overarching aim must be a civil service that is citizen- and business-centric, and that possesses the political will to finally act on what several commissions have already told us.
