The organisation lost its recognition by France’s sports ministry early this year, while the International Cricket Council (ICC) in June suspended development funding and placed France Cricket on notice for “non-compliance with membership criteria”. This came after scandals such as ‘ghost’ women’s matches and development funding that was unaccounted for. “We must not let cricket die in France, it’s a shame,” Romero, who is president of the Paris regional cricket committee, told AFP. “If we don’t do anything (it will die).” Romero, who at 65 still plays, was previously on France Cricket’s finance committee and is also a director at Paris Universitaire Club (PUC), one of the main cricket clubs in France. Others, though, simply want Balane to resign so they can resolve the governance issues that saw them lose recognition from the ICC and the sports ministry. Afghanistan’s ‘war on women’: How an exiled newsroom finds ways to support journalists under Taliban rule

In Afghanistan, women journalists work covertly and communicate using encrypted platforms with colleagues abroad, finding creative ways to continue reporting under the Taliban’s repressive regime. Despite significant risks, many continue to write under pseudonyms and strict anonymity conditions – sharing stories driven largely by personal experiences of witnessing the sheer violence and diminishing freedoms within their own communities. Zahra Nader, editor-in-chief of exiled media outlet Zan Times, works with such women across various provinces – providing them the opportunity to continue reporting under extremely difficult circumstances. “We set up ourselves as exiled media to be able to cover the stories that could not be covered inside Afghanistan – and that’s the role that we are trying to play. We are doing it as Afghan women journalists. “We are telling our own story and the story of our country and what’s happening there today,” she said at a keynote conference panel on ‘Voices under Pressure: Perspectives on Press Freedom across Asia’  hosted by the Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung (KAS) Media Programme Asia in Chiang Mai, Thailand, on Nov 5. The outlet takes its name from ‘Zan’ – meaning ‘woman’ in the Afghan Dari language – and was founded in 2022, shortly after the Taliban’s takeover a year prior. “Right now in Afghanistan, there is a war on women… When the Taliban took over, women were their first target… Within the first month of the regime, they banned girls from going to school. “By December of 2021, they had banned women from travelling alone. Right now, women in Afghanistan cannot even visit a health clinic by themselves without a mahram [male guardian] or a man accompanying them,” Zahra said. “Women are being arrested on the streets for not properly covering themselves up… There is absolutely no local media in Afghanistan that can report, so that’s why there is a group of us outside Afghanistan doing so,” she added. Yet, despite significant restrictions – Zahra emphasises the unrelenting determination of many Afghan women journalists to keep reporting. Earlier this year, for example, Zan Times launched its first fellowship programme specifically for women within Afghanistan, receiving an unexpectedly overwhelming response. “We had only 12 spots for 12 women inside the country – but only within two weeks of our application being opened, we received 860 applications from women living all across the country,” she said. Alongside virtual training with media experts from across the world, the online fellowship most importantly prioritises the safety and wellbeing of its reporters. “We also talk about mental health and about trauma-informed journalism, and we talk about how they can do this work safely from inside their own homes,” Zahra said. Nevertheless, under circumstances where these women do leave their homes, the precautions taken often include travelling with male chaperones, using burner phones, and preparing ‘cover stories’ in advance, in case they get interrogated by the Taliban. “If our reporters encounter a Taliban checkpoint along the way, she has to have a story of why she’s outside, and where they are going,” Zahra said. Reporters also never carry the smartphones they use for work. Zahra recalls how one colleague always borrows her mother’s phone instead whenever she leaves the house, because “her mother is not talking to anybody that might be dangerous, so it would still be okay if the Taliban ever checks her phone.” These preventative measures are especially vital, given that working with exiled media is completely banned in Afghanistan, and journalists have been arrested simply for communicating with editors and colleagues abroad. If deemed too risky, stories are eventually dropped altogether to protect reporters and their sources. “We try to tell them first that they are in charge of their own security. You have to tell us, this is a story that I cannot work on – and we accept that,” Zahra said. Local journalists who cannot cover a story themselves often pass the information to Zan Times instead. Alongside physical safety, psychological safety also remains paramount to the reporters involved – especially given that the stories investigated by Zan Times journalists often revolve around deeply sensitive and violent matters, such as cases of femicide. Earlier this year, for example, Zan Times had documented 231 killings of women, and the lack of accountability that followed. The findings were later raised at the UN Human Rights Council in Geneva. “We found out that of every four women that was killed in Afghanistan, only one person was arrested – and even then, that one person was released only within hours. Simply, there’s an impunity for femicide, and there are absolutely no consequences for it,” Zahra said. When asked how outsiders could help, she urged international media to continue working with local journalists and exiled newsrooms. Despite foreign journalists facing less intimidation and restrictions than local reporters, Zahra acknowledges that sending foreign reporters into Afghanistan is often also very difficult and costly, and many are also not entirely privy to the nuances of local customs. “They don’t know the context. They often need fixers and translators, for example… Whereas for Afghan journalists, we know the context, we know the language, we know the culture. And our journalists are local to the provinces that we are covering,” she said. “We would be able to tell a much fuller story if the media outlet or the journalists trust us and come to us and work with us. Which, unfortunately, is not happening a lot.” Real support, she added, also means “acknowledging our journalism, by considering us as colleagues” – and making it harder for governments in the region to ‘whitewash’ the Taliban through continued diplomatic relations and collaborations. For Zahra, the work also remains deeply personal, and began long before she established Zan Times in 2022.  As someone who grew up in Kabul and had worked as a journalist in the country prior to the Taliban takeover – even then, she said, Afghan women had rarely been given leadership roles or opportunities equal to their male colleagues. “There were a lot of assumptions about gender, and about what a woman can or cannot do. Once, I was told by my own male colleague who was managing a newsroom of over 2,000 journalists – he said that I can never be a good journalist because I am a woman – because I cannot cover attacks, I cannot stay late in the office, and I cannot travel. “At that point, I had already been working as a journalist for five years – but I had never been asked to do any of those things, because they assumed that I cannot do it. They never gave me a chance,” she said. Hence, Zahra’s work with her colleagues at Zan Times aims to continuously challenge such perceptions, and emphasise the significance of Afghan women and the role they play within society. “Our existence goes against such ideas, and it is to challenge the Taliban’s idea that women should not be part of the society. Because we are very much part of society,” she stressed. For many Afghan women journalists, the work also continues to give their lives meaning. Zahra recalls a message she once received from a colleague who said: “Every day now for five years, I wake up in my home, there’s absolutely nothing I can do. My existence outside my home is illegal. So, to be able to do something for my community, to tell the story of women in my community and be their voice, that is the purpose of my life.” Most importantly, the Afghan women working with Zahra’s team also represent the hope that “one day, there is going to be a day when the Taliban regime will end, and we will all be part of building a new society together”. The pseudonyms and bylines used by Zan Times reporters, Zahra said, are also meant to help the journalists eventually gain public recognition for their work when it is safe to do so. “In my lifetime, five regimes have changed in Afghanistan, and I’m not even that old. So that gives me some hope and that’s why I do this work. “I am training the women journalists with the hope that there is going to come a day that they would be the one leading newsrooms in Afghanistan, telling the story of Afghanistan, and helping shape society’s perceptions about what women can do,” she said. Many of them work in offices run by ruthless developers who, the young architects say, are not interested in good architecture or in the city as a whole — they are only interested in extracting the maximum value out of land. Crucially, this requires developing affordable wall and roof insulation for low-income housing, promoting cross-ventilation, and designing around mature trees to preserve them for posterity. But more important than that is supporting the development of an institution that can “talk and listen to people” and an academia that can make these elements part of its educational system. However, this calls for a culture of continuous learning and not one for simply passing exams and acquiring degrees. A pedestrian bridge covered with advertisement hoardings on Rashid Minhas Road: such hoardings that obscure the view create ideal conditions for harassment — especially of women — rape and muggings ECO-FRIENDLY CONSTRUCTION There are also issues related to the manner of using construction materials and the lack of concern among developers and many architects regarding environmental and economic issues pertaining to them. For example, there are many large office buildings, rapidly increasing in number, with huge glass facades and aluminium surfaces. The glass facades started multiplying in the 1990s. The glass heated the interior, especially when it faced southwest, where the Karachi sun is strongest. This created an extremely uncomfortable interior and excessively high air-conditioning costs. To overcome this problem, many buildings pasted thermopole on the inside surface of the glass, but it was only marginally effective and negated the “aesthetic” reasons for which glass had been used. So something new was introduced: heat-proof glass, which prevents heat from passing through it. This glass can cost up to Rs2,450 per square foot. Large surfaces of this glass can hardly be considered a response to Karachi’s climatic conditions. “But we have no option but to design in this manner,” says a not-so-young architect who worked in my office not so long ago. “The boss wants it, his clients want it.” When I designed the offices of the Sindh Rural Development Organisation in Sukkur with small window openings and large, blank, brick surfaces on the exterior, an unfavourable comment that I received when I made a presentation was, “It looks like Mohenjo Daro.” However, this ‘Mohenjo Daro’ has proved to be remarkably responsive in climatic terms. A flyover on Gulbahar Nazimabad Chowrangi: a lack of clear signage and poor design often results in creating more traffic problems rather than solving them HERITAGE AND CONSERVATION Perhaps the most complex relationship of the built environment is with tangible and intangible heritage and culture as a whole. This is something we have not really sorted out in the design and conservation processes we have practised in Karachi. Where heritage properties are located, land costs are exceptionally high, and so the owners prefer to demolish the building and sell the land for development, and city developers are only too happy to purchase this land. Building bye-laws and zoning regulations permit a much higher land-to-area ratio than exists in most areas where heritage is located. This is a further incentive for the owner of the heritage building to demolish it. But the official custodians of heritage wish to preserve some aspect of the past “glory” of the city, so the facade remains, and a 20-floor building is located behind it. So, how do you deal with an owner who wishes to maximise earnings from his property, a developer who has the money and connections to turn heritage properties into mere facades, and government agencies that are willing to oblige and have the means to ignore the concerns of academia and citizens on the subject? Some aspects are clear, as architect and conservationist Yasmeen Cheema has often told me: “We must move from thinking in terms of buildings to area conservation.” She also feels that, without the establishment of conservation faculties at universities, along with the training of conservation technicians, we will continue to carry out uninformed experimentation and deface our heritage and our history along with it. There is no doubt that area conservation will create a new way of looking at the city, for heritage will become a part of a larger planning exercise, hopefully serving the majority who live in the “area”, promoting intangible culture, activists and communities, and protecting the area, if not the city as a whole, from insensitive projects. NED University’s Department of Architecture and Planning had initiated an area conservation project at Karachi’s Khori Garden. It yielded a lot of knowledge and understanding regarding conservation-related issues in a dense area of the city. Unfortunately, it had to be stopped due to Covid and was never resurrected. However, it showed that the bazaar and its lower- and middle-income communities were interested in heritage and its conservation matters. This has been made obvious by architect Marvi Mazhar’s Pakistan Chowk project. Architects, because of their class bias, normally do not believe this. But there is another important issue that needs to be mentioned here. Most of our heritage buildings and areas are subject to major ownership disputes within the families who own the properties. And then much of the ownership is through the pagrri [tenancy-cum-informal ownership] system. These issues have to be resolved to initiate a conservation process, and this, given Pakistan’s judicial system, takes time, effort and money. The heritage issue needs to be discussed between those who have practised it and those who have been its beneficiaries or victims. Here, what should be decided is what should not be done, rather than what should be done and how. A food street filled with families in Sindhi Muslim Cooperative Housing Society UNDERSTANDING PUBLIC SPACE This brings me to the issue of pedestrianisation of public space. When I first proposed the pedestrianisation of Saddar, there was no reaction. Later, my colleagues Cristoph Polak and Asiya Sadiq worked with me to produce the book Hawkers of Saddar Bazar (2008), which was all about pedestrianisation. The ideas in it interested former Karachi mayor Naimatullah Khan, the Karachi Development Authority Engineering Bureau chief Zahir ul Islam, and students and teachers of architecture, but it did not become a subject for serious discussion, let alone its acceptance. But now pedestrianisation is supported both by officialdom and by the architectural and planning professions. So Burne’s Road was pedestrianised, then de-pedestrianised, and is now being pedestrianised again. A conservation project (tangible and intangible) can be a part of this pedestrianisation process, but it requires considerable research and love and respect for the people who live there, their businesses, their way of life, and their aspirations. You have to get to know the people through a process of osmosis and not only through questionnaires and surveys. But pedestrianisation also needs pavements. Karachi’s pavements have broken surfaces, making it difficult to walk on them. There are trees and billboards by the hundreds on them, forcing people to walk on the roads. In many cases, sidewalks are a foot or even higher than the road surfaces. Why is this so? The answers I have received are interesting. I have been told there is no need to make pavements, since Karachiites, in any case, walk on the roads, and the pavements are a foot high so that motorcycles find it difficult to get on to them and use them as ‘’roads’’. I asked a building contractor who was building pavements how children or elderly persons would get on to the pavements to use them, and he replied, “Koi na koi jugaarr kar hi laingey [They will improvise somehow].” This is also the attitude of those who plan and govern Karachi, as a result of which Karachi remains a city of dirt tracks, garbage-filled open spaces, and unmapped parks that are the refuge of drug addicts, while young men and women are deprived of spaces for sports and recreation. ENTERTAINMENT AND RECREATION Karachi is a big city, with millions of people, so something has to be done to serve their need for creativity and their longing for entertainment and recreation, which state culture in Pakistan has disapproved of since the end of the Zulfikar Ali Bhutto era. With Ahmed Shah sahib as director of the Arts Council Pakistan Karachi and also as a former minister of culture, an attempt to change this has been made. But the Arts Council is an organised culture that the vast majority of young people in Karachi cannot access. Entrepreneurship has found a solution. In Defence Housing Society’s Phase 8, you have a number of open-air chai and paratha dhabas [roadside cafes], which are usually full of young men and women sitting and conversing, moving around, changing venues, and all dolled up. The show begins in the early evening and continues till 1 am. Some of these open-air cafes are considered illegal, but function all the same. There are also indoor cafes that, apart from coffee, have pool tables, book club meetings, small crafts workshops, and mini community get-togethers. In the Nazimabad area, and in almost all middle- and lower-income settlements, a culture of sitting out has emerged, so pavements are full of people sitting, having tea, smoking shisha (which is officially banned), and conversing. But this is an entirely male affair, and it is only now that you occasionally find women sitting outside in open spaces and on pavements. There are also mushairas [poetry recitals], musical evenings, Muharram majlises, milaads and lectures. But these are usually private affairs, though many of them are held in public spaces. The trend is growing, and so is the world of sports. Observations and studies tell us that women are becoming a part of them, especially in the field of cricket. Matches between women’s teams are also held. In certain studies that I was involved in, young women from katchi abadis desired a space to play cricket, practise yoga, and train in martial arts. A new world is being born. A change in gender relations and family structures is taking place around us. Are Karachi’s planners, academia, and civil society willing to cater to and position these realities and aspirations? If not, Karachi will remain in a state of social conflict, and the desire of its young people to migrate to other countries will grow. Pakistan Chowk in Saddar A CHANGING CITY So, I don’t know the city. But the city does not know me either. For 40 years I worked in various low-income and lower-middle-income settlements of the city, and on the housing and land use changes that have taken place. I have recorded the processes by which this has happened and, in the process, developed numerous contacts that I have tried to keep alive. But since people of my age keep dying, there is no way that you can maintain contact with them. Residents of low-income settlements have been my teachers; I have learned to see their problems, their settlements and their social and political culture through their eyes. For instance, the Orangi Pilot Project had three outstanding social organisers, whom its founder, Akhtar Hameed Khan, had trained. Saifee was a plumber, Ramzan was a small-time contractor, and Hafeez Arain was a political activist who earned a living by driving a rickshaw. They were not only my teachers but the teachers of a whole generation of social activists and community organisations all over Pakistan. Then there was Parveen Rehman, who had been my student and who worked with me for 30 years. I saw her grow from someone who had no relationship or understanding of katchi abadis to one who mastered the art and science of their upgrading. She was shot dead. All these people have now gone, including some who have been killed because of their land-related activism. Because of their departure and my old age, knowledge regarding evolving community conditions and processes has become difficult to acquire firsthand. But society has also changed, and the major change has been around women. At a meeting of Orangi residents, major differences around personal and social values emerged. The majority of the younger generation were unmarried, and they were not interested in getting married because they did not want to get married to those their parents wanted them to. They did not wish to live with their parents, but they could not afford a house of their own. Their parents, on the other hand, find it very difficult in today’s expensive world to arrange a marriage function without borrowing money. ‘So how will you get married?’ I asked the young people in the gathering. “TikTok par kar laingey [We will do it on TikTok],” responded one, followed by much laughter and, later, serious discussions about the pros and cons of TikTok’s role in creating relationships and information. Studies also show that the demand for educated women is increasing in the new world of retailing, especially in department stores and malls. This leads to an opening of the mind, public exposure and interaction, and hence a new confidence that can be passed on to younger generations. There can be no equitable planning without catering to the needs and aspirations of women in the new Pakistan that is being born. But we must know what they are aspiring to or urgently need — such as clean, decent public toilets adjacent to every market and every office. My experiences, books and papers that inform this essay are in no way conclusive. But the main factor that has emerged is that Karachi, for many decades, has been planned (directly or indirectly) and managed by uncultured mobs who have no sense of aesthetics and social values. They have no love and respect for the citizens of the city, its history, and no concept of well-being for its future generations. Everything is “chalta hai” [it’ll do] and “do minute mein ho jaega” [It’ll be done in two minutes]. Some changes are visible, but to challenge and change such a firmly established culture and system will require not only effort but a lot of time, and people with ethics who can place the well-being of the city and its inhabitants over themselves, their clans and ethnicities. But I still feel happy when I travel along the road where the walls between the open spaces and the roads have been removed by our mayor. There is a sense of openness, freedom, and a feeling of well-being. I am horrified by cramped high-rises lacking play space, frustrated young architects, and money-driven professionals. Master plans and studies reveal a persistent clash between reality and planners/politicians, leading to flawed assumptions that misguide planning decisions. I see wrong assumptions and misread context determining the shape of planning. Even in elite areas, parks and open spaces, which elite privileges have created, are being taken over illegally by real estate developers. As in the case of the commercialisation of the Nehr-i-Khayyam in Clifton. There is also the case of the KPT-Malir expressway, an elevated transitway that will pass through the heart of Defence Society, the most elite area of the city, destroying not only Defence Society but the entire city of Karachi. So, the elite are now protesting, though they have seldom protested against the injustice that the lower- and lower-middle-income residents have had to suffer. Maybe it is time for a larger movement against a planning process that perpetuates a violation of the city’s ecology and results in the suffering of its people.
