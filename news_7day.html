ESA’s Euclid telescope maps over 900 million galaxies

PARIS: The European Space Agency has released its first big collection of data from the orbiting Euclid telescope’s mission to map the universe’s large-scale structure, including observations of more than 900 million galaxies, as scientists investigate the puzzling cosmic components dark matter and dark energy. The data unveiled on Thursday covered a survey of the sky equal to about 9,500 times the size of the moon as viewed from Earth. The mission’s goal is to complete the largest-ever three-dimensional atlas of the cosmos, spanning more than a third of the sky, in 2031. Thursday’s release encompassed around 13 months of Euclid observations, totalling an enormous 1.7 petabytes 1.7 million gigabytes of data. “It marks a major milestone for ESA’s Euclid mission and for everyone involved,” said astrophysicist Valeria Pettorino, ESA’s Euclid project scientist. Euclid, launched from Florida in 2023, is gathering data on how the universe has expanded and how its structure has formed over time while amassing information about the nature of dark energy and dark matter and the role of gravity on large scales. “Euclid will not just take images of galaxies in the sky, in two dimensions, but will also measure their distance, the third dimension. This will tell us where galaxies are in space and time, across 10 billion years, and in all directions beyond our galactic plane,” Pettorino said. “Galaxies cluster along filaments, surrounded by empty space, and form a network that we call the cosmic web. Euclid will reconstruct the cosmic web and how it has grown over time. This is important because the exact configuration of the cosmic web, how many clusters or voids are there, how fast it changed, depend on the properties of dark energy and dark matter,” Pettorino said. The universe’s contents include ordinary matter as well as dark matter and dark energy. Ordinary matter stars, planets, gas, dust and all the familiar stuff on Earth represents about 5pc of the contents. Dark matter, known from the gravitational effects it exerts on galactic scales, is invisible material that may make up about 27pc of the cosmos. Dark energy, thought to be responsible for the accelerated expansion of the universe, may account for 68pc. Dark matter and dark energy do not emit or absorb light and cannot be directly observed. Consumer courts can’t hear insurance policyholder's complaints, LHC holds

LAHORE: The Lahore High Court has ruled that consumer courts lack jurisdiction to entertain complaints filed by policyholders against insurance companies. Justice Jawad Hassan issued the ruling on a petition filed by the United Insurance Company of Pakistan Limited against an order passed by the District Consumer Court in Bhakkar. The consumer court had overruled an objection raised by the insurance firm regarding the maintainability of a complaint filed by a policyholder. The district court had held that the complaint was maintainable simply because the dispute stemmed from the provision of insurance services. Setting aside the consumer court’s order, Justice Hassan observed that the lower forum failed to properly examine the relevant statutory framework, specifically Section 122 of the Insurance Ordinance, 2000. The judge noted that Section 122(3) of the Ordinance explicitly bars any court other than a specialised tribunal from exercising jurisdiction over claims brought by a policyholder against an insurance company in respect of or arising out of an insurance policy. Orders issuance of fresh, reasoned decision on case’s maintainability within a month Referring to constitutional principles and settled judicial precedents, including the landmark Adamjee Insurance Company case, Justice Hassan highlighted that under Article 143 of the Constitution, competent federal legislation takes precedence over provincial enactments. Consequently, he said, where a special statute provides a specific mechanism and forum for dispute resolution, general laws such as consumer protection acts cannot be invoked to displace the jurisdiction of the specialised tribunal. The judge remanded the matter back to the consumer court with directions to re-examine the question of maintainability strictly in accordance with the law and binding judicial precedents. Constrained partnership

“Trust takes years to build, seconds to break, and forever to repair.”Anonymous THIS observation aptly describes Pakistan’s relationship with one of its most important international health partners. Since 2003, the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tu­­berculosis and Malaria has disbursed more than $1.1 billion to Pakistan’s HIV, tuberculosis and malaria programmes. In August 2020, however, it invoked its Additional Safeguard Policy (ASP) on Pakistan. Behind this bureaucratic terminology lies an extraordinary decision: the Global Fund no longer had sufficient confidence in the country’s arrangements for managing its gra­nts and implementing disease-control programmes. More than six years later, this decision deserves serious public scrutiny. Established in 2002, the Global Fund is one of the most successful innovations in international health financing. It mobilises resources to support countries fighting these three diseases and has helped save an estimated 72 million lives globally. Pakistan has been a significant beneficiary. The Global Fund normally operates through a partnership model in which national stakeholders identify priorities, nominate implementing organisations and oversee grants through a Country Coordinating Mechanism (CCM). The underlying philosophy is country ownership: international resources should strengthen national institutions, not substitute for them. The Global Fund’s observations point to serious weaknesses in governance & institutional accountability. In 2004, the Global Fund Board adopted ASP to address situations where normal funding arrangements could not adequately protect its investments. It permits oversight and direct involvement in selecting principal recipients and implementing partners. In effect, decisions normally taken by national institutions can be assumed by the international financing agency. This is an exceptional, temporary arrangement, reflecting serious concerns about governance, institutional capacity, accountability or programme implementation. Since its introduction, it has been invoked in around 30 countries but revoked in only three: Cuba, Myanmar and Côte d’Ivoire. This shows that entering ASP is easier than exiting it. Why was Pakistan placed under ASP? The ans­wer lies in a candid letter dated Aug 6, 2020, from the Global Fund to the Pakistan government. It expressed serious dissatisfaction with Pakistan’s response to HIV and TB, noting stagnating or declining TB notifications and an almost unchecked increase in HIV incidence. Significantly, it stated that public-sector principal recipients had failed to translate domestic and external financing into effective programmes. The letter specifically criticised the performance of the Common Management Unit (CMU), responsible for coordinating the three disease programmes, and identified inadequate leadership in managing challenges arising from devolution. These observations point to serious weaknesses in governance, leadership, coordination and institutional accountability. Consider this: Between 2020 and 2022, Pakistan changed the national coordinator of the CMU 10 times in three years. Many appointments were political. The epidemiological consequences are equally troubling. While many states have reduced new HIV infections, Pakistan has moved in the opposite direction. According to WHO and UNAIDS estimates reported in Dec 2025, annual new infections increased from approximately 16,000 in 2010 to 48,000 in 2024 — a 200pc increase. ASP’s implications extend beyond these programmes. For a country seeking international inv­estment, development assistance and credibility in global institutions, its inability to demonstrate satisfactory governance of externally financed hea­­lth programmes sends a deeply damaging message. But ASP should not be viewed merely as an international embarrassment. Its more serious implication is the erosion of national ownership. When external partners increasingly determine arrangements, domestic institutions risk becoming less accountable for results. Pakistan must therefore develop a credible, time-bound roadmap for exiting ASP. The Aug 2020 letter identified the conditions for its revocation: timely provincial budget disbursements for HIV and TB; satisfactory programme performance and financial risk management; demonstrated political commitment; compliance with audit recommendations, etc. These are not unreasonable demands. Federal and provincial governments must jointly establish a high-level mechanism to monitor progress against each condition through independently verifiable indicators. Civil society and affected communities must also be meaningfully engaged. There is another dimension requiring urgent attention: financial sustainability. For over two decades, Pakistan has depended heavily on the Global Fund for essential components of its HIV, TB and malaria responses. In 2024, 90pc of HIV financing came from the Fund. This assistance has been invaluable. But indefinite dependence on external financing is neither desirable nor realistic, particularly as international development assistance comes under increasing pressure. Pakistan needs a carefully planned transition towards greater domestic financial responsibility. This does not mean abruptly replacing Global Fund resources or imposing unaffordable obligations on provincial governments overnight. The FCDO-financed, Oxford Policy Managem­e­nt-implemented Global Fund Accelerator Prog­ra­mme is providing valuable analytical support and recommendations. Federal and provincial governments must make optimal use of this opportunity. They should negotiate a gradual increase in domestic financing. Public financing should progressively assume responsibility for recurrent expenditures, while increasingly covering medicines and prevention services. Crucially, exiting ASP and transitioning from donor dependence are related but distinct processes. The Global Fund has stood by Pakistan through successive health emergencies and institutional challenges. Its continued engagement, even after invoking ASP, demonstrates that its objective is not to abandon Pakistan but to protect investments and improve results. Pakistan must respond with the seriousness this partnership deserves. Ultimately, the most compelling argument for reform is not the restoration of international prestige. It is the right of millions of Pakistanis to effective prevention, diagnosis and treatment. The real achievement will be transforming a constrained partnership into a confident, accountable and genuinely country-owned response to three preventable and treatable diseases. Ambition without scale?

PAKISTAN’S newly announced Auto Policy 2026-31 promises cheaper vehicles, higher localisation, exports, employment, improved safety and cleaner transportation. These are worthy objectives. But can they be achieved simultaneously in an industry constrained by limited scale, proliferating makes and models, and the absence of competitive upstream steel, petrochemical and aluminium industries? More importantly, does the policy risk undermining one of the few enduring achievements of decades of automotive protection: the engineering capabilities developed by component manufacturers, with applications in agriculture, defence and other industries? The policy deserves credit for recognising that protection has not delivered internationally competitive vehicles. Gradual tariff reductions, export targets and electric-vehicle incentives signal change. But reducing tariffs without addressing high energy, taxation and financing costs risks weakening domestic manufacturers before they become competitive. Previous auto policies have produced an insufficiently recognised benefit: the engineering capabilities developed by component manufacturers. Guided by international assemblers, Pakistani firms have acquired expertise in precision machining, sheet-metal pressing, plastic moulding, rubber products, glass, wiring and numerous other components. Industrial clusters around Port Qasim, Lahore-Sheikhupura and elsewhere demonstrate capabilities extending beyond automobiles. Losing them would erode an industrial foundation that Pakistan needs for manufacturing diversification and import substitution. Competition among brands is desirable, but proliferation of incompatible platforms undermines supplier competitiveness. Support for technological upgrading, testing and certification is welcome. However, indiscriminate tariff reductions could expose these firms to overseas competitors operating at vastly greater scale, before domestic cost disadvantages are addressed. Protection should neither be indefinite nor un­­conditional. A differentiated, time-bound appro­ach should preserve engineering capabilities with demonstrable competitive potential, linking support to productivity, quality, scale and exports. Mandatory localisation targets require scruti­ny. Components manufactured locally using im­­por­ted steel, polymers, machinery and tooling cannot reasonably be counted as entirely indigenous. Pakistan lacks integrated automotive-grade steel, petrochemical and aluminium supply chains. The appropriate measure is domestic value addition after deducting imported inputs, including those embedded in locally purchased components. Higher local procurement does not necessarily deliver equivalent foreign-exchange savings. Policy must measure genuine indigenous value addition, technological upgrading and net foreign-exchange contribution separately. Pakistan’s limited vehicle market is increasingly divided among manufacturers, models and variants. Each additional platform can require separate moulds, dies and tooling. Small production runs raise costs and discourage sophisticated manufacturing. Competition among brands is desirable, but proliferation of incompatible platforms undermines supplier competitiveness. Common components, platform sharing, contract manufacturing and supplier consolidation should be encouraged. Pakistan’s motorcycle industry offers an instructive parallel. Standardised designs, large volumes, extensive vendor networks and a substantial replacement market have enabled component manufacturers to develop capabilities while making transportation accessible to millions. Cars require more demanding standards and higher tooling investment, but the lessons of affordability, standardisation and scale remain relevant. Pakistan should encourage one or more safe, fuel-efficient, affordable small-car platforms through collaboration with established international manufacturers. Shared components and higher volumes could support meaningful domestic value addition, strengthen vendors and eventually create export opportunities. Encouraging electric vehicles is appropriate, particularly motorcycles, three-wheelers, small cars and public transport. However, concessional imports of completely built-up vehicles and completely knocked-down kits may accelerate adoption without developing domestic engineering capabilities. The transition should include a roadmap enabling existing component manufacturers to enter EV supply chains. The policy envisages approximately $6 billion in cumulative automotive exports over five years. But who will buy these vehicles? Complete-vehicle exports depend on foreign principals allocating production and overseas markets to Pakistani operations. Such decisions are scale-dependent and cannot be mandated. Potential African and other markets present additional challenges. Pakistan generally lacks preferential trade agreements with these destinations, leaving its vehicles exposed to tariffs while competing against established Asian manufacturers. Export incentives introduce another risk. WTO rules restrict subsidies contingent on export performance, while importing countries can impose countervailing duties following investigations establishing subsidisation and injury. Export targets need identifiable customers, commercial commitments, market access and demonstrated net foreign-exchange earnings. The proposed Automotive Development Levy, expected to raise Rs232bn over five years, raises another question. Pakistan’s experience with the Export Development Fund offers a cautionary lesson. Financed through a levy on exports, it supported various initiatives, but its effectiveness in delivering sustained competitiveness remains debatable. The government’s subsequent decision to review its utilisation, audit its accounts and abolish the surcharge raises legitimate questions about establishing another dedicated levy. Public funding for shared testing facilities, technology transfer and skills development has merit. But taxing consumers to subsidise exports that remain uncompetitive risks replacing tariff protection with another permanent support mechanism. Ultimately, Pakistan must distinguish between assembling vehicles, manufacturing competitive components and providing affordable mobility. These are related but different objectives. Climate challenges

THE poisonous smog blanketing Punjab is an immediate threat to public health, but it also points to a larger environmental crisis that Pakistan can no longer address through piecemeal measures. Punjab’s government says better monitoring and enforcement have brought improvements over previous years. That is welcome, but the air remains dangerous. While pollution from India compounds the problem, Pakistan must also confront domestic emissions. Moreover, smog is not the country’s only warning. Our climate crises are deeply interconnected. Repeated floods, punishing heatwaves and mounting water stress show how exposed Pakistan is to climate shocks, and how urgently it needs lasting solutions. At COP31, PM Shehbaz Sharif outlined a cleaner energy future that deserves support. He pointed to nearly 22 gigawatts of rooftop solar capacity added in two years, much of it driven by households seeking relief from expensive electricity. Pakistan has backed a pledge to increase electricity’s share of energy use to 35pc by 2035 and aims for electric vehicles to account for 30pc of new vehicle sales by 2030. These shifts could help reduce fossil-fuel dependence and, especially through cleaner transport, cut harmful emissions. But an energy transition is not, by itself, a climate policy. Pakistan also needs to protect communities from floods and extreme heat, safeguard water supplies and adapt agriculture to changing conditions. The PM rightly called for concessional finance and affordable technology. Rich industrialised countries bear much of the responsibility for global warming and must help vulnerable nations cope. Yet appeals for international climate justice must run alongside serious action at home. That action requires more than ambitious targets or seasonal restrictions. Authorities must enforce vehicle and industrial emission standards, expand public transport, support farmers in ending crop burning and make clean technology accessible beyond affluent households. Cities must be better prepared for floods and extreme weather, while rural communities need help coping with rising temperatures and water shortages. Federal and provincial governments must work together and ensure that environmental regulations are properly enforced.
