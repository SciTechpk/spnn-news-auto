Pakistan’s global role

THE nature of Pakistan’s role in global politics is once again the subject of much debate. Several officials are ferrying messages between Iran and the US for an end to the conflict, having helped organise a short-lived peace agreement earlier in the year. Based on these and past events, there are a series of judgement calls that observers make about the Pakistani state and its position in the world order. One set of views sees Pakistan as a client state of the US, that is, one that’s subservient to America’s imperial interests and its immediate allies in the region, the Gulf monarchies. This view is shared by sections of the left — globally, in the region, and within Pakistan — and disgruntled opponents of the current regime, who furnish as evidence for it the country’s role in global anti-communist politics in the 1950s and 1960s, its partnership with the US against the Soviets in Afghanistan, its role during the ‘war on terror’ between 2001 and 2013, and the proximity of ties with the Trump administration in recent months. Ironically, conservatives and Islamists also cite the 1950s/1960s and the experience of US-Pakistan relations during the war on terror and especially in the last few years, as evidence of successive regimes siding with global secularist interests in subjugating both Islamic movements in the region and Muslim political identity at home. The other view, held in the same time-space continuum, sees the country as ideologically fundamentalist, and simultaneously, a client of China and an unpredictable ally of the US — one that is capable of undermining the global hegemon on its own or in collaboration with the Chinese. This view is shared by much of the American and Indian mainstream, who cite CPEC and close military relations as an example of Chinese clientelism, previous support to the Taliban during the invasion of Afghanistan as an example of duplicity, and its approach to Kashmir, its recent diplomatic support for Iran, and the broader sociocultural and ideational life of its citizens, as an example of (religious) fundamentalism. As with most sweeping observations, many may detect a kernel of truth in these otherwise clearly contradictory positions. Close proximity to American imperial interests has characterised many passages in Pakistani political history. Similarly, the relationship with China remains remarkably consistent since the 1970s. However, in an effort to account for Pakistan’s global and regional behaviour, these observations lapse back to the rather broad and imprecise categories of ‘client state’ or ‘fundamentalist state’. The reality, just on the basis of the actions listed above, is that the state’s geopolitical and ideational positioning has varied considerably, which makes it hard to pin it down as a US ‘client’ of the same ilk as Japan/ South Korea or the Gulf monarchies, or as a Chinese client like North Korea. There are a series of judgement calls that observers make about the state’s position in the world order. What is consistent though is the subordinate role that the state here plays in global affairs, not just in terms of geopolitics but also — and more fundamentally — in terms of international economic processes. How does ‘subordinate’ differ from ‘client’? Beyond mere semantics, a substantive difference is that a subordinate actor may have limited agency, but it retains the capability of understanding and developing its own interests. A client, on the other hand, would, by and large replicate the interests of its patrons. On the evidence of the last seven decades, it would be incorrect to assert that the Pakistani state elite has not developed a sense of its own interests, even though some may disagree with the content of those interests or how those interests were developed. There is, for example, a long-standing recognition of being in a ‘tough’ geography, with insecure borders on at least three sides. There is also a persisting notion that it faces a periodically existential threat from a much more powerful eastern neighbour and that this threat should be countered by a military deterrent. This in turn can only be obtained by forging reciprocal relations with more powerful states. The presence of these interests/ ideas does not invalidate the fact that the state’s own actions and responses may have hastened insecurities in various ways. Nor can it paper over the fact that the processes through which the state recognises its context and develops its response are heavily skewed towards a largely insulated military-bureaucratic state apparatus. Both, in turn, lead to other domestic problems, including frequent civil-military political conflict and law and order insecurity in the peripheries. However, while some reduce Pakistan’s subordination to its geography or the original conditions of its creation, one can make the more substantive case that it remains in that place by the nature of the global economic order since the 1970s and due to its own economic dysfunction. With basic processes of capital accumulation stagnating or being rendered highly inefficient, the dollar requirement to ensure subsistence-level growth under global conditions of economic liberalisation can only be met through wilful subordination to varied benefactors. Periodic balance-of-payment crises since the 1980s have precipitated this pattern, and geopolitical posturing to extract IMF concessions or rents in other forms has cemented it. Each successive crisis of the last four decades has found resolution via subordination in the geopolitical domain: favour garnered with the Gulf monarchies via military assistance or labour provision in exchange for reserve deposits; providing the US with regional geopolitical support in exchange for IMF approval, or allowing Chinese capital to make infrastructure investments to kickstart (fleeting) economic growth. Ultimately, each temporary staving off of an economic crisis has pinned down the state’s subordinate position. Season of protests

A WAVE of protests is sweeping across the world in a season of unrest. From West to East, the street is confronting the state. Led predominantly by students and young people, protests have been widening to encompass other disaffected groups in society. In some cases, they are morphing into a nationwide movement. On almost every continent, governments are being put on notice that there will be a price to pay for misgovernance and being indifferent to public demands. India’s Gen Z has been in the streets in anti-government protests against the controversial revision of electoral rolls. Students in France began protesting against deteriorating school conditions with other groups, including trade unions, joining demonstrations over high living costs and inequality. In Italy, students, farmers and truckers took to the streets in protests driven by education costs, economic grievances, and anti-war sentiment. In Brussels, students marched over education funding. Spain saw protests over a housing crisis. In Albania, student-led demonstrations occurred over living costs and a controversial tourism project linked to Jared Kushner, President Donald Trump’s son-in-law. In Portugal, protests erupted over housing and falling living standards. There were education protests across Greece. In Brazil, thousands joined a student march against the election bid of far-right candidate Flávio Bolsonaro. Tunisia’s capital witnessed protests demanding the president’s resignation and democratic rights. The triggers vary, as do the underlying causes. Each protest is rooted in its own political context. But there are common themes. All reject the status quo and indicate disenchantment with traditional political parties. All voice anger against unresponsive governments and the state failing them on their socioeconomic demands. The context for most protests is a cost-of-living crisis and deterioration or cuts in public services compounded by the state’s inability to deliver on education and jobs. Economic grievances and resentment of inequality are a driving force of the protests. A sense of disenfranchisement is another common thread. The heavy-handed response by governments to the protests and draconian crackdowns further fuelled the unrest and sparked violent clashes in several places. But it energised demonstrators to keep returning to the street. Unfortunately, in France protests degenerated into looting and rioting. Protests in Europe and India share a defining feature. They are propelled by a wave of student activism. At their core is youth frustration in a generation that sees broken promises and an uncertain future. It feels betrayed by the lack of educational and economic opportunities. Protes­ters have also been venting rage against political leaders who have ignored public needs and are seen as venal. Initiated by young people, protests have expanded into broader movements as other disaffected groups, with their own set of demands, joined the demonstrations. Social media has been a key mobilisation tool in all the protests. Governments have been put on notice that misrule and ignoring public needs will have consequences. None of the protests or movements have been party-led. Political parties have largely been sidelined, even as they tried to leverage the demonstrations; France’s left aligned itself with the protesters. In several European countries, protests are playing out in an environment of deep polarisation, which parties of both the left and right are trying to exploit. This is especially because elections are due next year in many countries including France, Spain, and Italy. In India, the youth ‘Cockroach’ movement has spearheaded the protests and mounted the most significant challenge to Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s government so far. The ongoing protests came against the backdrop of student protests led by the Cockroach party over the leak of exam papers in May, which forced the resignation of the education minister. This time, the catalyst was the revision of voter lists by India’s election commission, which apparently involved the removal of around 130 million voters. The government and the election commission’s claim that this was aimed at eliminating duplicate and ineligible entries was widely rejected. It was instead seen as a step towards mass disenfranchisement. It became the trigger for youth protests, demanding the resignation of the chief election commissioner and scrapping the revision. India’s opposition parties also joined the demonstrations and decried the move as a politically motivated purge to benefit the ruling BJP. The Cockroach Janta Party used the opportunity to broaden its campaign. The government’s strong-arm tactics to suppress the popular upsurge only inflamed the demonstrators who accused Modi of “stealing democracy”. While these protests did not pose an existential threat to Modi, his jittery and increasingly autocratic government behaved as if they did — with arrests and a harsh clampdown. The scale of the protests and the government’s repressive response eroded Modi’s authority, weakened his grip on power, and dented his credibility and image. The mass protests signified much more than opposition to the voter roll overhaul. They have been about democratic rights and democratic ac­­c­o­­untability and against pervasive injustice. As Indian academic Sudhir Kumar Suthar has arg­ued, they have been driven by the quest for dignity as much as for rights. The message from the young is clear: “they deserve a more dignified life than what they have received from the system so far.” The central question is what will be the outcome of mass protests and what will they achieve? A definitive assessment has to wait until the protests play themselves out as most are still unfolding. But one unintended consequence of the current wave of discontent in some European countries, especially France but to a degree also in Spain and Portugal, may be to strengthen the far-right. The far-right has led none of these protests but it stands to benefit from their fallout. In a political landscape where the political centre is being hollowed out and centrist parties have lost touch with young people, unrest advantages the most organised opposition force that promises order. In France, that force is Marine Le Pen’s Rassemblement National. Where the hard-right is in power the advantage works differently. It enables governments to cast the protests as a law-and-order issue fuelled by migrants to justify harsher measures. More generally, youth-led protests may force governments to take more seriously the needs and aspirations of young people which have rarely been a priority. What the streets are signalling is a breakdown of trust that can no longer be brushed aside. For now, the protests have put governments on notice: misrule has consequences. Wheat price dilemma

THE announcement of an indicative wheat price of Rs4,500 per 40 kilograms may have come too late for Sindh farmers, but it is welcome as far as it goes. That is not far. The figure is Rs1,000 higher than last year’s and, by the government’s account, reflects rising production and fuel costs. It is also Rs500 short of what farmers demanded. And there is no word on who will buy the grain or when. An indicative price is a signal, not a guarantee. Since April 2025, when the government dropped the support price and suspended large-scale procurement, farmers have been promised a market-based system that would serve them better. It has not. The crop now being sown is the third to go into the ground without a floor. Punjab’s experience this April shows what happens when a procurement price is announced without a mechanism to enforce it. The province tried to revive buying through bank-backed private aggregators. The banks doubted the scheme’s viability, and the aggregators bought only 160,000 tonnes against a target of 2.5m tonnes. Prices fell anyway. A price no buyer is obliged or financially equipped to pay is only a number. Growers seem to have grasped this before the government did. Consumers, meanwhile, are not being spared. Over five years the price of wheat has gone up by 150pc, and that rise goes straight to the price of flour. Punjab has used the price as its only tool instead of working on the cost of farm inputs, which the federal government’s policies have kept high. Flour in cities is selling for Rs160-190 per kilogram, which is hard on poor households. Even the government’s stocks are expensive. Sindh began selling government wheat this month at Rs12,500 per 100kg, or Rs5,000 per 40kg. That is more than the prime minister has offered farmers. The test of wheat reform is not whether the government can step out of the market. It is whether the system that replaces government buying shares the risk fairly among farmers, traders, millers, banks, and the state, keeps flour affordable, and protects the country’s food supply. For now, farmers carry most of the risk while everyone else looks after their own interests. With sowing already underway, the government should say in plain terms what it will buy, through which agency, with what money, and by when. It should adapt its imports to that plan. It should look at the cost of fertiliser and other inputs, instead of relying on price alone. And it should help poor households directly, instead of keeping flour cheap for all by squeezing the grower. VIEWPOINT: Auto policy’s tax paradox

Pakistan’s new auto policy was meant to take effect on July 1. It is yet to be finalised. Meanwhile, the automotive industry continues pricing vehicles, opening letters of credit, and signing dealer agreements in the dark — unaware of the tax burden those cars will carry next year. The latest bottleneck arose during the International Monetary Fund’s fourth review of the Extended Fund Facility. In late September, the Ministry of Industries and Production presented its draft Auto Policy 2026-31 to the IMF mission, but talks concluded without an agreement. The primary sticking point is the proposed one per cent sales tax on new energy vehicles. The Fund wants the standard 18pc rate. On a Rs10 million car, that single disagreement represents the difference between a tax of Rs100,000 and Rs1.8mn. Low tax rates matter, but the rate itself is only half the story. The crucial second half is defining what this policy is actually for. In FY26, Pakistan spent $16.9 billion on petroleum imports, a major portion of which was burnt on its roads. The government aims for 30pc of new vehicle sales to be electric by 2030, a transition estimated to save about $1bn annually in foreign exchange, 2bn litres of fuel, and 4.5mn tonnes of carbon emissions. For a nation recovering from devastating floods and choking on severe winter smog every year, these are not side benefits. They are the entire point. Low tax rates matter, but defining the policy is also crucial Both sides of the current argument are partly right. The Fund has watched Pakistan repeatedly carve up a narrow tax base through special rates and Statutory Regulatory Orders (SROs), knowing temporary concessions almost invariably become permanent. The ministry is equally right that electric vehicles cost more upfront, charging infrastructure remains thin, and buyers harbour deep anxieties about battery life and resale value. Imposing the standard tax rate overnight would reduce the 30pc EV target to a line on paper. However, the draft policy has a glaring weakness of its own. Customs duties on conventional cars are scheduled to fall sharply, but reports suggest this gap will simply be refilled through higher Federal Excise Duty and floor prices on imported vehicles – essentially building the same protective wall with different bricks. In the meanwhile, tax rates keep moving by notification. The concessional 8.5pc sales tax on most hybrids expired on June 30 with the expiry of the last auto industry development and export policy, causing taxes on locally made hybrids to jump to 25pc. Then, in September, the Federal Board of Revenue issued SRO 1525(I)/2026, bringing locally manufactured hybrids up to 2,000cc back down to 18pc. The relief was welcome, but the same car underwent three distinct tax rates in as many months, and the final adjustment came through SRO rather than any transparent policy. An investor deciding whether to establish an assembly plant does not mainly ask whether the tax is 1pc or 18pc. They ask what the tax will be in 2029, and whether that answer will survive the next budget cycle. A better structure would rest on five sensible pillars: First, agree on 18pc as the ultimate destination and legislate a clear roadmap to get there. Keep the 1pc tax rate until June 2027, when current concessions lapse, then step it up incrementally each year to reach the standard rate by FY2030. Low taxes in the early years build the market: China exempted electric cars from purchase tax for nearly a decade, and Norway waived VAT before both began phased rollbacks. Support should be generous at the start, but bound by a firm expiration date. Second, target the relief. A Rs30mn luxury import does not need the same fiscal cushion as a compact car, a taxi, or a rickshaw. Capping concessions by price or battery size concentrates public money where the public benefit actually lies. Third, set a clear tech ladder. The combined burden of duty and tax should be highest on conventional petrol cars, lower on hybrids, lower still on plug-in hybrids, and lowest on fully electric vehicles. Battery electric vehicles and range-extended EVs should share one tax structure at the bottom because their powertrain is fundamentally the same. Fourth, follow the National Tariff Policy honestly. Bring duties on fully built imports down as announced, eliminate additional and regulatory duties by FY30, and do not rebuild protectionism through excise duty. Maintain a moderate, transparent gap between duties on built-up imports and kits for local assembly, fixed for the life of the policy. Fifth, reward localisation through component tariffs, not sales tax exemptions. Offering low duties on battery packs, electric motors, and controllers for a fixed period from the start of local production is a far cleaner incentive that directly rewards genuine investment. The last important point is that established assemblers and vendor networks support over a million livelihoods, as per PAAPAM, and their fear of a uniform tariff cut is legitimate: localisation that took decades to build can be dismantled in a year. But ‘raw’ protectionism alone has failed to deliver scale or competitiveness. Local content in Pakistani passenger cars averages about 58pc, compared to roughly 80pc in India. The electric transition is a chance to rewrite that story.
