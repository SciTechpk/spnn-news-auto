After the emergency

THE much-anticipated, and somewhat dreaded, emergency in KP was finally proclaimed by the president on Oct 11. The federal cabinet’s recommendation, the summary of the prime minister’s advice to the president, and the proclamation — all three momentous developments — occurred on the same day. The subsequent actions like taking the chief minister — non-functional but still the elected chief minister of the province — into preventive custody by his subordinate police from within the chief minister’s official residence along with many other senior elected officials; transfer and posting of senior and mid-level Civil Service officials; formation of an advisory council to help the governor in his newly acquired functions; the advisory council’s first meeting and the convening of the meeting of the long-neglected provincial apex committee took place within 48 hours of the proclamation, indicating that the federal government had done its homework in anticipation of the proclamation. Besides debating whether the emergency would be imposed or not, the media had also been speculating about the specific article in Part X ‘Emergency Provisions’ of the Constitution that would be finally invoked. Articles 232 and 234 were the ‘favourites’ on TV talk shows and, in the end, the federal government did not disappoint either group betting on the choice of articles, as the proclamation invoked both Article 232 and 234. It seems that the government was not taking any chances. While Article 232 was apparently favoured because it does not set a limit on the period of emergency, it contains a ‘flaw’ in the federal government’s view, as it does not bar the provincial assembly from functioning during the period of emergency under Article 232. The federal government simultaneously invoked Article 234 apparently to render the provincial assembly non-functional during the emergency and to vest all legislative powers in parliament. Invoking both articles simultaneously appears to be unprecedented, although an emergency has been proclaimed about seven times in Pakistan since the adoption of the 1973 Constitution. This manifests the hard line with which the federal government wants to implement the proclamation of emergency. In most media reports, Article 232 has been frequently referred to as the instrument to proclaim emergency, in contrast to Article 234 that, according to these reports, has been used to proclaim governor’s rule. A plain reading of the Constitution, however, does not bear out this distinction. Both articles concern the proclamation of an emergency, and both give the federal government the option to either exercise executive powers directly in the province or designate the provincial governor to do so on its behalf. Article 234 is invoked either on a report from the provincial governor to the president, or after the two Houses of parliament separately pass a resolution asking the president to proclaim an emergency under Article 234. The president may or may not act based on the governor’s report. Still, if the two Houses pass a resolution to this effect, the president must invoke Article 234. Both articles lay down different requirements for parliamentary approval of the proclamation. Article 232 requires that the proclamation be laid before the two Houses for approval by each within 10 days of the proclamation if the latter has been made by the president on his own (which is the case for the emergency proclaimed on Oct 11), and not based on a resolution passed by the provincial assembly. The federal government easily fulfilled this requirement on Oct 14, despite the fiery speeches and the opposition’s subsequent boycott of the proceedings. Article 232 also provides that the proclamation issued under the article be laid before a joint sitting of the two Houses within 30 days. This formality was also fulfilled. The government might survive constitutional challenges, but can face political risks. Article 234 simply requires that the proclamation issued under the article be laid before a joint sitting of parliament. The proclamation will cease to be valid at the end of two months unless the joint sitting extends the validity by another two months. The joint sitting is authorised to grant up to two extensions of two months each, meaning the proclamation of emergency under this article can continue for a maximum of six months. Given the clear majority of the ruling coalition and the PPP, continuing the proclamation of emergency under this article for six months doesn’t seem to pose a challenge for the federal government. The provincial assembly will become functional after six months, but, as seen in the past, federal governments manage to engineer such a change within the assembly during this period that it doesn’t pose a major challenge to the centre. This is more likely because the provincial government will remain non-functional indefinitely under Article 232. While the federal government may be able to surmount constitutional challenges, it might still face serious political difficulties. Although contrary to the warnings of the non-functional chief minister of KP and other PTI leaders, no serious agitation has been witnessed in the country or even in KP so far, it should cause some discomfort to the federal government that none of the political parties other than those in the ruling coalition has supported the proclamation. Articles 232 and 234 do not restrain the high court’s powers, but a serious judicial challenge appears unlikely. However, the majority of proclamations of emergency challenged in the courts previously could not be sustained. Last but probably not least, the federal government and the security establishment have put their prestige at stake by taking on total responsibility to combat and defeat terrorism in the province. After the proclamation of emergency, there is no longer a provincial government to blame for the rising arc of terrorism. North Korea says tested AI-assisted torpedo system

North Korea has tested a “new-type” precision-guided torpedo system that uses artificial intelligence for target detection and guidance, state media reported Saturday, as the nuclear-armed country steps up weapons testing. The announcement came days after Pyongyang said it had test-fired two types of cruise missiles from a submarine after weeks of friction with Seoul over a landmine explosion along their border. Analysts say North Korea could be rapidly advancing its missile and weapons technology with support from Russia received in exchange for sending troops to support its war against Ukraine. The underwater test-fire of the “super-speed” Zam-2.0 torpedo system took place on Thursday “to confirm the successful technical updating” of the weapon, the Korean Central News Agency said. “AI technology was applied to the target detection and the guidance function of the new-type super-speed precisely guided torpedo,” KCNA said. The test proved “the core technology for raising the efficiency of propulsion and the performance of rocket propulsion device”, it said, adding it had completed “more than 180 rounds of different tests over the past five years”. Earlier this month, North Korea announced the AI-assisted launch of an intermediate-range hypersonic missile. Pyongyang said at the time there was “no way to avoid” the hypersonic missile, which was “capable of changing its flight orbit at a low altitude”. Torpedoes are designed to strike enemy targets underwater at high speed, but the stated introduction of AI could also allow North Korea to carry out evasive manoeuvres and make autonomous decisions to confuse an adversary, analysts say. “South Korea and the United States deploy decoy targets to divert torpedoes, but AI could potentially help distinguish real targets from decoys,” Lim Eul-chul, a professor at Kyungnam University, told AFP. “From torpedoes to submarine-launched weapons, Pyongyang is pouring resources into demonstrating that US strategic assets can be rendered ineffective.” In September, Pyongyang’s leader Kim Jong Un emphasised the need to raise “the level of artificial intelligence” in the country’s weapons systems. ‘Arms race’ Saturday’s announcement came as the two Koreas appear to be intensifying an arms race over hypersonic missiles. Hypersonic missiles travel at more than five times the speed of sound and can manoeuvre mid-flight, making them significantly harder to track and intercept. Days after North Korea announced it had tested its AI-assisted intermediate-range hypersonic missile, Seoul test-fired its own weapon – a hypersonic glide vehicle it called a “key strategic asset capable of changing the course of the battlefield”. North Korea this week cast doubt on Seoul’s test, saying it may have “failed to achieve hypersonic flight”, a claim South Korea rejected a day later. The latest weapons tests come amid growing speculation over a possible meeting between US President Donald Trump and North Korean leader Kim, as world leaders prepare to gather for November’s APEC summit in China. After several overtures met with silence from Pyongyang, Trump said again this week he “may” meet North Korean leader Kim during his Asia tour next month. Ahead of a possible North Korea-US summit, Pyongyang appears to be “playing down South Korea’s hypersonic missile programme to portray itself as ahead of Seoul — Washington’s ally — in the arms race”, Yang Moo-jin, former president of the University of North Korean Studies in Seoul, told AFP. But North Korea may need more time to advance its torpedo system, particularly in reducing the noise it generates underwater, he said. “At high speeds underwater, a torpedo generates significant noise, making it easier to detect,” Yang said. “Reducing that noise is a major technical challenge requiring advanced technology — and North Korea does not yet appear to have reached that level”.

The department released the seismic data through its Islamabad office. In August, an earthquake of magnitude 4.8 jolted parts of Balochistan, including Zhob and Chaman. Anthropic proposes law regulating AI to US lawmakers

Anthropic, the creator of the AI assistant Claude, confirmed on Friday it is circulating a draft bill among US lawmakers aimed at regulating artificial intelligence to address the lack of federal legislation on the risks posed by the new technology. According to the draft, first reported by Politico on Friday, Washington could block a model’s release or use if there is an imminent risk of killing or seriously injuring more than 50 people, or causing more than $1 billion in damages, but only with a judge’s approval. A company spokesperson told AFP that the proposal “grants the federal government the ability to block or deter dangerous deployments of the most powerful models.” “It requires frontier developers to test the most powerful models, be transparent to the public about their findings, submit them to independent evaluation, and maintain a robust security program,” the spokesperson said. AI policy in the US under President Donald Trump has relied primarily on voluntary industry pledges and executive orders intended to sustain an AI boom that tech leaders say is driving the country’s economy. Congress, politically divided and heavily lobbied by Big Tech, has yet to pass a federal AI law, and is out of session until after next month’s midterm elections. Security became a hot topic after recent revelations of security incidents caused by AI agents — autonomous software that operates without constant human supervision — that escaped the control of their developers. Anthropic, which was banned from the Pentagon by the Trump administration in February, was forced by Washington in June to suspend its most advanced models for nearly three weeks.
