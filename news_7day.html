History and leadership

HISTORY is never linear or monolithic. It may progress in leaps and bounds, or falter and regress. It may appear calm and seamless, but be restive and roiling beneath the surface. No wonder, a small trigger can lead to a cataclysm. Historians have long debated whether historical dialectics shape human destiny, or human agency lights up the darkest epochs of history. There’s no definite answer as yet. But there is a broad consensus among them on two requisites of a historical ‘development’ — ripe ‘objective’ conditions, and an intelligent leadership that can transform a conducive environment into, at the very least, meaningful change. Interestingly, the modern era manifests both trends. We see countries and societies long affected by broken governance, endemic corruption, political instability, economic meltdown, civil strife, and regional and global conflagrations. Significant parts of the Middle East, North Africa, and South and West Asia are a dismal reflection of failed or failing states ruled by autocrats or absolutist dynasties — largely without popular mandate and without accountability. Yet there are countries once dismissed as ‘basket-cases’ that have turned themselves around — such as Singapore, Malaysia, Vietnam, Turkiye, South Korea, and China. Over the years, they have taken tremendous strides in terms of political stability, socioeconomic progress, scientific and technological advancement, and human-resource development. They have carved out a large share for themselves in international supply chains and productivity. Much of the credit for their phenomenal achievements goes to their visionary, upright, and courageous political and institutional leadership. True, some of them did not begin as ideal democracies. Yet they built robust, efficient institutions, along with enabling infrastructure, to ensure continuity, stability, peace, and development, and to facilitate a gradual transition to democracy. In contrast, our state has been stuck in a time warp for decades. Every generation has suffered gross ‘elite capture’ characterised by authoritarianism, disenfranchisement, misgovernance, instability, inequality, impoverishment, and never-ending conflicts. In dialectical terms, although the objective conditions for radical sociopolitical change have long been ripe, the desired agency of change — the political and institutional leadership — has remained elusive. No wonder the state continues to exist and function in colonial and neocolonial moulds. Authoritarian governance, a compromised and complex legal order, an extractive and iniquitous social structure, the dominance of the security and administrative apparatuses, and a pro-elite neoliberal policy framework remain among its key features. Our state has been stuck in a time warp for decades. But has this state worked? Never. The founding ideal of a democratic republic itself has been upended. Instead, a security and administrative juggernaut has been forged on the pretext of guarding national security and ‘ideology’. In fact, the state has been virtually detached from its constitutional and democratic controls. It seeks to draw its power, legitimacy, and even sustenance not from the people’s mandate, but from its coercive powers and capacity to control. Hence, it does not need to worry about citizens’ rights or welfare. However, this mindset has drawn an ever-widening wedge between the state — controlled and exploited by a small club of security, bureaucratic, political, landed, industrial, and financial elites — and the people, more than half of whom are classified as ‘poor’ or ‘very poor’. Yet, instead of correcting this perilous course, already scarce resources are being further drained for security obligations — arguably, not so much at home as abroad. This raises a disturbing question: for how long can this behemoth, guided by colonial notions of governance and built around failed politico-legal machinery, sustain itself on the proverbial feet of clay — an ever-brittle economy compounded by worsening security and governance failure? This question has arisen in every post-colonial state where elites have captured power, stolen resources, disenfranchised people, and eventually pushed society into chaos and conflict. But in many instances, upright, visionary, and courageous leadership provided the answers — convincingly — by steering the country out of the woods. Do we have such leadership that can also provide convincing answers — by transforming public angst and alienation towards dysfunctional state structures into a force for building state and political institutions that are democratic, equitable, accountable, and, especially, peace-loving? Ironically, much of the mainstream political leadership — the PPP, PML-N, PTI, and others — has long forsaken its distinct agenda, and even its identity. Rhetoric aside, they have offered little in the way of ‘answers’ to the many problems afflicting the state and governance. Instead, over the decades, they have tended to outrun one another in their efforts to be more ‘compatible’ with the demands of an extractive state toeing the colonial model. The PPP-PML-N duo’s joining hands with non-political elements to weaken the judiciary, election commission, and media, and the PTI’s eagerness to mend fences with the powers that be instead of the release of its leader are some recent instances of crass political opportunism and historical compromises on the part of our ‘popular’ leaders. So, the causes of our recurrent sociopolitical tragedies and the decline of democracy should not be traced to history’s apathy in the face of ominous lengthening shadows. It is the frayed moral fabric of much of our leadership — political and institutional — that gives preference to personal, political, and institutional interests over democracy and the good of the people — the real masters. Under these hopeless circumstances, a new class of self-led movers of history — a sort of Gen-Z — is needed to change our collective destiny. External sector turning points

THE slight increase in workers’ remittances reported for October prompted a host of questions directed to me. Here are the responses. The rise is too small to matter. In fact, it falls far short of what it needs to be to serve its purpose: helping plug the trade deficit. There is no reason in the world for celebration based on one data outturn. What matters is the trend that a series of data points describe; most often, that trend is intelligible only when seen in its proper context. So what is the proper context for remittances? First, remittances are traditionally how Pakis­tan’s economy finances its trade deficits. The trend is cyclical and reveals itself when you take a 12-month average of the total trade balance plus remittances, then plot that on a month-to-month basis. You will notice something. A wavy line emerges. From a low point in 2015, it rises slowly until it briefly turns positive — around $40 million — in November 2015. Then it plunges back below the X-axis, before bottoming out in July 2018 at around negative $1.45 billion. This is trade balance plus remittances, mind you, so a negative value means remittances can’t plug the trade deficit, and the balance is being financed from reserves or borrowing. If you don’t see the reserves plunging by the same amount in that month, then you can be sure the balance is being financed by new borrowing, and that data will show up in the balance-of-payments whenever the next release comes along. A screengrab from Nadra’s succession website. — succession.nadra.gov.pk The website makes it clear that Nadra can issue a succession certificate or letter of administration only in uncontested cases, where all legal heirs agree and have no dispute among them, no heir is a minor or mentally disabled, and the matter is neither pending before a court nor already decided by one. The deceased must be in Nadra’s records and the heirs must be registered in the Family Registration Certificate (FRC), while the applicant must hold a valid CNIC, Nadra card or Pakistan Origin Card (POC), as the old manual NIC is no longer accepted. A legal heir has to visit a Nadra centre in person, except in Sindh and Balochistan, where a lawyer can appear on his behalf. If any condition is not fulfilled, Nadra does not issue the certificate and the family has to approach the court. For an application to be accepted, the family has to produce the death certificate of the deceased issued by the relevant union council, municipal authority or cantonment board, along with the receipt of cancellation of the deceased’s CNIC, FRC, and copies of the CNICs of all legal heirs. They also need a Schedule-I letter of authorisation attested by an oath commissioner, and the original property documents, as photocopies are not accepted. In some cases, additional documents are required. For immovable property in Sindh, a search certificate has to be obtained, while in Sindh and Balochistan, a wakalatnama (power of attorney) is also required if the application is filed through a lawyer. If the deceased was a government or private employee, a letter from the concerned department about pension or service dues is also needed. The fee for a succession certificate or letter of administration is Rs10,000 where total assets are less than Rs100,000, and Rs20,000 where assets are Rs100,000 or more, except in Sindh where it is Rs22,000. A duplicate certificate costs Rs5,000.
