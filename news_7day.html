Why the world still needs World Trade Organisation

The World Trade Organisation has spent much of its recent life being told it is obsolete. In its World Trade Report 2026 launched last month, the Geneva-based body tells us what would happen if the rules are simply allowed to ‘wear away.’ It says the outcome would be quite expensive for the global economy. It models a world that breaks into rival geopolitical blocs and finds global GDP would be 5.1 per cent lower by 2050 than it would otherwise be, with exports dropping by 18.6pc. In a harsher scenario, where multilateral cooperation collapses, GDP falls 6.9pc and exports by nearly 27pc. Set against that, a reinforced system could add about 2.9pc to global output and lift exports by almost 18pc. That is roughly three trillion dollars. The report is the WTO’s flagship study and looks back over 80 years of trade rules. It is also a warning: reform the system or risk a slide into fragmentation. WTO Chief Economist Rob Staiger has told Reuters that the trading system is at a “critical juncture”. The report names four pressures: economic power is spread more widely than when the rules were written; governments intervene in their economies far more; digitalisation and global value chains have changed what trade actually is; and political friction between major powers keeps rising. Weaker WTO means riskier world for trade None of this is new to anyone who follows trade. What the report adds is a price tag, and some uncomfortable detail. About 72pc of global merchandise trade still moves on WTO most-favoured-nation (MFN) terms, meaning without special preferences for particular partners. Four years ago the figure was roughly 80pc. Staiger called the slide a “worrisome trend”, and it is the sort of change that does not announce itself. Nobody declares the end of a rules-based order. Trade simply migrates, one exemption and one bilateral deal at a time, until the exception becomes the norm. The WTO says trade policy uncertainty has reached unprecedented levels as governments reach for tariffs, subsidies, export controls and industrial policy. Regional and sector-specific deals are multiplying in response. Without a strong multilateral framework, such arrangements divert trade towards preferred partners rather than the most efficient producers, and encourage blocs to raise barriers against outsiders. The WTO rules restrain discrimination against non-members and shape how free trade agreements are built. Take that discipline away and the incentive to build walls grows. The Trump factor: Erosion of the trading order in recent years cannot be discussed without naming the biggest single force behind it. The protectionism led by the United States under two non-consecutive Trump administrations has been a decisive rupture with the multilateralism that shaped the economy in the last eight decades. What began in 2018 as retaliation against steel and aluminium imports into the US has hardened into a tariff doctrine, revived and widened in Trump’s second term from 2025. It is wrapped in the language of national security and economic sovereignty. However, the logic is strained. Tariffs on allies’ steel were not a response to a security threat, and blanket duties on nearly everything cannot be defended as one. The language of security has become a licence for unilateralism, and it undermines the assumption on which the WTO rests: integration and reciprocity would discipline the strong as well as the weak. The costs are not hypothetical. Studies by the IMF and the US Federal Reserve in the last few years have documented real income losses, disrupted supply chains and weaker investment from earlier rounds of tariffs. Costs for US consumers and manufacturers who depend on imported components have been substantial. The 2025 programme with its blanket tariffs and punitive duties on Chinese goods and electric vehicles, moves US policy from rules-based liberalism towards transactional nationalism, in which access to the American market is a favour to be bargained for. Even at home, the approach has run into legal limits. In May 2025, the US Court of International Trade ruled that the president had exceeded his authority under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act when imposing a universal 10pc tariff; the appeals court upheld that decision. The rulings are a reminder that policy built on emergency powers is fragile, and that trading partners are being asked to reorder their economies around measures that may not survive scrutiny. When the largest economy treats the rulebook it helped write as optional, others retaliate. Some hedge by building their own blocs and some conclude the rules were only ever meant for others. The report’s most striking finding is about distribution. Least-developed countries would gain the most from stronger multilateral cooperation, and lose the most from fragmentation. Pakistan has experienced this. Its exporters have had to absorb tariff shocks and renegotiate access. Rules do not guarantee fairness, but they make the terms of competition predictable, and predictability is what small economies cannot afford to lose. WTO director-general Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala struck a more hopeful note at the report’s launch. The system has been repaired and refreshed before, she said, and it can happen again. “Let’s not be fearful,” she said. The report does not argue that the system is working as designed. Its argument is that the alternative to imperfect rules is worse, and that the choice is not between the old order and a better one, but between repair and decline. The bottom line is plain. Trade rules are not an administrative distraction. The choice between renewing them and letting them lapse is worth trillions of dollars, especially for countries with the least say in whether the rules survive. At UNGA debate, Pakistan warns against adding more members to Security Council, extending veto privileges

Pakistan has warned that adding more permanent members to the United Nations Security Council (UNSC) and extending veto privileges would “deepen existing inequalities, perpetuate historical advantages and increase the risk of Council paralysis”. The counsellor at the Pakistan Mission at UN, Gul Qaiser Sarwani, presented these views at the UN General Assembly during a debate on the use of the veto. He said Pakistan called for “comprehensive, democratic and equitable reform of the UN Security Council” and stressed that the misuse of veto power undermined UNSC’s effectiveness, weakened the credibility of the UN and perpetuated inequalities within the international system. “Strategic rivalries and narrow interests among the Council’s permanent members had repeatedly impeded collective action on international peace and security,” he said, emphasising the “unique legitimacy” of the UNGA, whose universal membership and democratic decision-making enabled it to respond when the UNSC unable to act, including due to the exercise of the veto. The Pakistani delegate opposed the creation of any new permanent seats in an expanded Security Council. On proposals to regulate the use of the veto, he said Pakistan favoured its abolition or, at a minimum, severe restrictions on its use. He emphasised that the veto must not be expanded and reaffirmed Pakistan’s support for General Assembly resolution 76/262, which provides for a UNGA debate following the exercise of a veto to promote greater accountability. Sarwani stressed that any measures to restrain the veto must remain consistent with the UN Charter. He called for a balanced approach combining restrictions on veto use with enhanced representation and a stronger role for elected members of the UNSC, making its decision-making “more democratic and providing an effective check on the exercise of veto power”. The delegate further emphasised that the question of the veto must be addressed alongside the other four interrelated clusters under the Intergovernmental Negotiations on Security Council reform, as part of a comprehensive reform package. Concluding his remarks, he reiterated “Pakistan’s strong commitment to reform that corrects existing imbalances, strengthens accountability and serves the collective interests of the entire UN membership rather than a select few”. “Reform for all. Privilege for None!” he said. A recent UN report has also warned that the veto power of the five permanent members of the Security Council is one of the reasons multilateral mechanisms have been unable to prevent or stop the growing number of state-based conflicts. The resulting paralysis has weakened decision-making processes, prompting the UN secretary general to call for Security Council reforms, including an expansion of its membership to ensure fairer representation, particularly for Africa, Latin America and the Caribbean, and the Asia-Pacific region, it says. “All these voices are underrepresented despite bearing much of the burden of peacemaking in an increasingly multipolar world,” the ‘World Social Report 2026’ notes.

Whither terrorism

THE future of Khyber Pakhtun­khwa remains unclear, whether it’s as a single entity or as a democratic one. PTI, we are told, has been made PNG — once again. And the south of KP is reportedly being carved out rapidly to be a new province or to simply reverse the amendment that made the former Fata part of KP. No one is really sure what the coming days hold. Amid this confusion, a newly appointed member of the Area Representative and Development Council (ARDC) for the special emergency-hit districts of the south has created another storm in a teacup. In a video statement he spoke of reaching out to the Afghan Taliban to bring peace to Pakistan. Considering that this preference for dialogue over conflict with the fitna-across-the-border ostensibly has been the reason the PTI government was sent home, a similar message of love from the new advisory council just wasn’t cricket. But before we could get our breath back at this audacity, the new spokesman of the region, Mehmood Jan Babar, added to the mess by not denying the idea altogether. Eventually Samar Bilour, who represents the Peshawar-led advisory council, had to enter the fray to diss the notion of talks. As an aside, we now have a Peshawar council and a Southern council. Be that as it may, the point is these statements have simply added to the confusion surrounding the province. And terrorism, despite the frequency of the attacks and the statements of the government, has taken a backseat. The focus is more on what the grand plan for KP is. Is it simply to close the doors on PTI? Is it to conduct an experiment on the efficacy of newer, smaller provinces? Or is it simply to reverse the Fata merger? Or perhaps it was to give the governor the chance to clear encroachments, a favourite problem for most non-elected officials these days. As the Urdu saying goes, there are as many rumours as there are mouths. We appear to be using the same old playbook put together in the post-Musharraf period. But none of these rumours seem to be highlighting the basic issue — how to address the decades-old problem of militancy in these regions. Other than vague promises of fighting the problem, there is little that has been shared with the people to explain what the new government is doing or planning to do. And this is where the problem lies. Militancy is going from bad to worse. The intensity of the attacks is worrying and impacts the entire country, especially one looking for more foreign investment. But while the state is clear on its decision to fight its way to victory, it doesn’t seem to have a clear strategy or path for this — or so it appears to those on the outside. Any strategy to succeed must take into account the current environment. However, we appear to be using the same old playbook put together in the post-Musharraf period when the TTP was controlling large swathes of land in what is now former Fata. In order to flush the Taliban out, military operations were conducted after the ordinary residents were persuaded to leave their homes and villages before the clearing out was done. And once the area/s were cleared, the displaced people were allowed to return to their homes and businesses. However, nearly two decades later, the TTP is no longer controlling areas; it seems to appear only to conduct attacks or block roads, or carry out other activities and then melt away. And yet, it seems, our strategy remains the same — of wanting to clear out areas, be it at the level of villages and so on. But if the TTP is not controlling these areas and the plan is to clear out the residents before launching operations, surely the TTP will also vacate the areas along with the residents and simply return later. How is this effective? And this is all the more difficult now because 20 years earlier, the TTP escaped to an Afghanistan run by a hostile government, which is no longer the case. But these questions are really not being discussed. The intelligence-based operations (IBOs) are also offered as an alternative. Though once again, there is little clarity on how effective these are; they only become public when they lead to deaths of troops, which is tragic. But once again, there is little information available on why there is even one fatality during an IBO. And if they are effective, then why has the problem simply grown? The second problem with this strategy is the public sentiment, which is against military operations because of the displacement and suffering that comes in the wake of this. But more than that, the displacements were not accompanied by the compensation the people had been promised — for the damage to their property and business. This has been a major reason for their reluctance to support operations this time around. And yet, this also does not seem to have led to any kind of brainstorming about what to do differently. More worrying still is the distrust or alienation of the people in general from the state as well as the TTP. In many anecdotal accounts, it is suggested the local residents of the affected areas see the activities of the TTP and the state as alienating. These gaps with the state, ideally, should be bridged before operations are launched. It is rather obvious that kinetic operations cannot succeed without the people’s support. And yet the state seems to be doing little to earn this support or trust. And along with all this comes the larger issue of why the kinetic operations, whenever they have succeeded, are not followed by a successful handover to strong functioning civilian institutions which can then govern effectively. In other words, the build-and-transfer part has eluded us for decades. Indeed, the reduction of the debate over terrorism to a simple one of using force or not is a very one-dimensional one.
