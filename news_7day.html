Lahore traffic police going beyond the road for recovery of e-challan dues

LAHORE: Chief Traffic Officer (CTO) Syed Abdul Raheem Shirazi has sent recommendations to the higher authorities to link the recovery of pending digital traffic challans to the core services of computerised national identity cards (CNICs), passports, sale/purchase of vehicles and police services, a move being considered ‘most controversial in the history of the traffic police’. The proposal is said to be a blatant violation of the constitutional rights of citizens and the judgments of the Supreme Court. Through an official letter, the CTO has suggested to the Punjab DIG traffic police to make a move to stop the issuance of duplicate CNICs, renewal of passports, vehicle transfer (sale/purchase transactions) and certain police services to the citizens/defaulters at the police Khidmat centres across the city, an uncalled for proposal that has raised eyebrows among the police circles and legal fraternity. Citing the CTO’s recommendations as an attempt to infringe upon the constitutional rights of citizens for generating revenue or recovery of pending payments, a law firm is all set to challenge the ‘executive order of an individual officer’ in the Lahore High Court (LHC). Propose making CNIC, passport issuance, vehicles sale/purchase conditional to clearance of e-challan dues; legal expert terms the move illegal A spokesperson for the City Traffic Police confirmed to Dawn that CTO Shirazi had proposed a comprehensive mechanism for the recovery of pending payments on e-challans. He said the decision was made to tighten the net around e-challan defaulters by making police, CNIC and passport services conditional to the digital challan clearance. “A formal communication in this regard has been sent to the DIG Traffic Punjab and the Punjab Information Technology Department (PITB),” said the spokesperson while quoting the CTO’s letter. The CTO has proposed that, as in the case of e-challan default, the provision of certain services at Police Khidmat Markaz should also be linked to the clearance of outstanding digital challan dues. The spokesperson added that timely recovery of outstanding digital challans would also help expedite the disposal of cases pending before courts. “It would increase government revenue while reducing the administrative efforts and expenditure involved in the recovery process,” he added. A senior lawyer, Omais Younis, declared the proposal of the CTO Lahore childish and funny, saying that the law does not allow such exercises/practices as the SC has protected the rights of the citizens in many reported judgements. “The Supreme Court has held that even a court executing a money decree cannot block a CNIC where the governing law does not authorise that mode of execution,” the lawyer said and added that an unadjudicated or disputed traffic challan provided an even weaker legal basis for such a deprivation. “Article 10-A guarantees fair trial and due process in the determination of civil rights and obligations and criminal accusations. A digital challan begins as an allegation; it is not necessarily a final adjudication merely because it appears in a government database,” said Mr Younis. “Withholding a CNIC for a traffic fine is not a traffic-specific inconvenience,” said the lawyer, adding that it might disable large areas of a person’s legal, economic and social life and might adversely affect family members. “The same is the case with the issuance of the passport which is a legal document that allows the citizen for free and safe travel,” the lawyer added. Referring to a judgement of the Supreme Court given on Feb 18, 2026, Omais said Supreme Court of Pakistan declared that even an executing court cannot block a judgment-debtor’s Computerised National Identity Card (CNIC) to enforce a money decree or demand security, ruling the practice illegal and beyond the jurisdiction of civil courts. “The court held that section 51 of the Code of Civil Procedure provides recognised modes of execution but does not authorise CNIC blockage”, he said, quoting the SC verdict. He added the SC had also stressed that a CNIC is not a luxury or a mere statutory formality. “We are moving the court of law against the unlawful and unconstitutional proposal of the CTO for the sake of protecting the rights vested under the laws to citizens,” said Omais. Unlearnt lessons of history

TODAY, the country’s hybrid government is fixated on altering Pakistan’s constitutionally mandated provincial structure and multiplying administrative units. To sell the idea, establishment apologists have rolled out some disingenuous arguments. They claim this will improve governance and miraculously solve the country’s problems overnight. A debate has been generated in the media about the pros and cons of administrative changes and breaking provinces into more units. Parliament has yet to discuss this. That is unsurprising. Deliberation is not this legislature’s strong suit. It has rushed through constitutional amendments in record time, once in the dead of night and without meaningful debate. Meanwhile, speculation is mounting that another constitutional amendment is being prepared to push through ‘administrative changes’. Absent from the establishment’s consideration of these sweeping changes are lessons from Pakistan’s history. Over the decades, the country’s rulers have experimented with parliamentary, presidential, semi-presidential, quasi-parliamentary systems, direct military rule, and more recently a hybrid model, which involves informal power-sharing between the military and civilians, with the former as dominant partner. Pakistan has tried them all. Yet each switch only contributed to political instability. The only system to command legitimacy and broad support is parliamentary democracy, despite the fact that parliamentary supremacy over the executive has never been realised. Linked to these experiments has been the status and rights of provinces in a federal system. As the provincial configuration and federating units largely coincided with the ethnic composition of people of each province, the need was always felt to make federalism a reality, not just a constitutional precept to ensure the people of all four provinces felt they were part of an inclusive and fair system. Pakistan’s troubled history offers a cautionary lesson against altering the provincial structure. But the federal structure and provincial rights have always been contested issues in Pakistan’s history. Contested on many counts. Smaller provinces have long resented Punjab’s political hegemony, sought greater powers and autonomy and felt the distribution of resources has been unfair. Unelected institutions have always preferred a centralised polity while military rule meant the country functioned as a virtual unitary state. Long periods of unrepresentative rule fuelled alienation among the smaller provinces by marginalising them, suppressing regional identities, concentrating power at the centre and weakening federalism. The creation of One Unit in the 1950s, which merged all the provinces of what was then West Pakistan into a single unit, was aimed at creating ‘parity’ with the more populous eastern wing of the country to undercut its numerical majority. This politically motivated project imposed a unitary system and all but destroyed the country’s federal character. It was justified by totally false and frivolous arguments. They included claims it would end “provincial prejudices”, reduce adm­i­nistrative expenses and help develop backward areas. The move had disastrous consequences. It fanned the flames of nationalist and regionalist sentiment in the smaller provinces of West Pak­istan and strengthened centrifugal forces across the country. It contributed to the sense of discrimination among the people of East Pakistan that, along with other factors, found expression in the Bengali nationalist movement and ultimately led to the break-up of Pakistan in 1971. The issue of provincial rights continued to be a source of tensions and contestation. The 1973 Constitution struck a delicate balance between the centre and provinces in a multiethnic state, assured provincial autonomy and enjoined reciprocal obligations between the federating units. Unfortunately, this was observed more in its breach than in its adherence. Opposition-run provincial governments were ousted in the 1970s by prime minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto. This reinforced a political tradition that began in the early years after independence when provincial governments were summarily dismissed by the central government. This seriously undermined national unity. These and other actions widened interprovincial disparities, deepened alienation among smaller provinces and led to the insurgency in Balochistan in the 1970s. This intensified during the 2000s but their antecedents reached back to the 1950s. The insurgency continues today as a result of monumental mishandling of the province and Baloch sentiment by successive governments. Authoritarian measures also gave a fillip to Pakhtun nationalism, even though over time this waned with the increasing representation of KP in the country’s power elite and bure­aucracy. The troubled history of federalism and provincial tensions in Pakistan offers a cautionary lesson against altering the country’s four ethnically distinct provinces. Past attempts to do that or erode constitutionally guaranteed provincial rights only widened fault lines and created new ones. These efforts were never about better governance or a stronger administrative system. They were about control. They reflected the interests of a ruling elite seeking to entrench political dominance, with little regard for national unity and cohesion. The same logic applies to those in the current hybrid government pushing to create new provinces and fragment existing ones. This is about control, not reform. True ethnic minorities in some provinces, such as Seraiki speakers in southern Punjab, have long wanted their own province. Some political parties have actively supported this. But the challenge is how to square this with carving out a new unit that could undermine the delicate constitutional balance, spark controversy and encourage others to demand new provinces. The 18th Constitutional Amendment, backed by all parties in 2010, opened a new chapter in provincial empowerment. Over a dozen ministries were devolved from the federal level to the provinces. But this also raised capacity issues and whether provinces could handle the increased fiscal power and resources devolved to them. The fiscal aspect evoked the most criticism. Because with the 2009 National Finance Commission award, the substantial transfer of resources to the provinces left the federal government unable to meet its financial obligations. While this tussle over revenue needs to be resolved and provinces should mobilise their own resources, creating smaller provinces or rolling back the amendment is not the answer. In fact, the rationale for redrawing provincial boundaries remains opaque. This lack of clarity reinforces the impression that the motive is to increase control, not to improve governance. Women’s leadership

PAKISTAN doesn’t fare well on indices related to gender gap and women’s empowerment. The damning statistics include the number of girls who are out of school, the wide gender digital divide, high rates of violence against women, and women’s low representation in private and public leadership positions. However, over time, there have been significant advances for Pakistani women leaders. Mohammad Ali Jinnah’s sister, Fatima Jinnah, was a key figure in Pakistan’s independence movement. She later became a presidential candidate, derided by the dictator Gen Ayub Khan. When Benazir Bhutto was elected prime minister in 1988, Pakistan became the first Muslim-majority country to be led by a woman. Pakistani women have led movements against dictators, including generals Ayub, Zia and Musharraf. Asma Jahangir was the first woman to be elected president of the Supreme Court Bar Association. Begum Naseem Wali Khan was the first woman to win a general election seat in the country. Justice Ayesha Malik is the first woman Supreme Court judge. Currently, Maryam Nawaz is the first woman chief minister in Pakistan. Trans women have also made their mark, with Aisha Mughal being the first to represent a country at a UN session. Recently, Nayyab Ali was nominated for a civilian award by the government. But despite these significant positives, the prosecution of political women in Pakistan is at an all-time high. Several women leaders of the PTI remain behind bars, including the ageing cancer survivor, Dr Yasmin Rashid, who has health issues. Since January, award-winning human rights lawyer Imaan Mazari Hazir has been in jail serving a 17-year sentence for mere tweets. Baloch women have now joined numerous men who have been forcibly disappeared; several remain in jail in politically motivated cases. The prosecution of political women in the country is at an all-time high. Do such incarcerations, cases and disappearances send an encouraging message to young girls across Pakistan? Does it motivate reluctant families to send girls to school so they can get an education and start careers? Does it motivate young lawyers to take up cases of the voiceless pro bono? Does it encourage young women to enter politics and do good for the people? The incarcerations reflect a deep-rooted patriarchy that plagues the entire system. Take, for instance, gender-based attacks against women during election campaigns. The Digital Rights Foundation documented at least 163 social media posts in the run-up to the 2024 election that included gendered disinformation against women politicians, and at least 47 posts targeting “journalists covering the elections, subjecting them to gendered insults and online trolling campaigns that included online threats of physical assault”. Deepfakes targeting women human rights defenders have become commonplace, with dehumanising content being disseminated online of women that are critical of state policies, with little to no consequences for such actions — despite Section 22 of the Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act that makes edited and non-consensual imagery a crime. Since the 2025 amendment to Peca, the state is an eager complainant against journalists who are critical of it. Whereas the new ability of the state to file cybercrime cases against activists, journalists and politicians is to be condemned and should be reversed; it is worth noting that the state doesn’t take cognisance of instances of harassment and vitriol on social media that lead to crimes against women and children. This includes routine cases of men mercilessly killing women for rejecting their advances and proposals. The victims include women who are public figures on social media platforms as content creators. On Aug 20, former professor and motivational speaker Faisal Asghar Imam was named in the murder case of his wife in their home, and social media was rife with testimonies of him making his students uncomfortable in class and online. The link between online misogyny and physical harm to life must not be taken lightly. When Peca was being promulgated, those of us who opposed it were labelled anti-women for opposing the law. Today, we see how women are harassed, jailed, and punished including for naming their harassers by the same law meant to protect them. The abuse of the law is not restricted to targeting women who dare to speak truth to power. Human rights lawyer Hadi Ali Chattha is currently serving a 17-year sentence for supporting his wife Imaan Mazari Hazir, with his punishment framed for the “crime” of reposting her tweets. Seemingly, men who support women related to them, they too will be punished. In our geopolitical context, targeting women in the public sphere is particularly alarming. Girls in Afghanistan are not allowed to go to school after Class 6; women are not allowed to leave their house for employment, and they cannot even sing or be heard in public. In Iran, women have been killed for simply opposing the state’s enforcement of head covering rules. In such an environment, it would be a powerful message to the neighbourhood if Pakistan has women in positions of power. Sadly, the current women’s leadership in Pakistan is hardly representative of half the population. There are no women federal ministers, and only one woman minister of state. In Sindh, the PPP that brought us a woman prime minister has only two women cabinet members; while the party’s cabinet in Balochistan has only one woman minister. In KP, the PTI has no women in the cabinet. A woman chief minister leads Punjab, but her cabinet has only two women ministers, with Marriyum Aurangzeb holding six portfolios. As a pluralist federal democracy, at least on paper, Pakistan must move in line with Article 25 of the Constitution that empowers the government to make special provisions to protect women and children, apart from ensuring equal treatment for them. Women political prisoners, along with men, must be released; and women being half the population must be given half of the governing share. We owe this to every young girl in Pakistan. ‘Grand dialogue’

THE country’s top civil and military leaders are absolutely right when they say that dialogue and development are needed to address Balochistan’s crises. But perhaps one should recall that many earlier attempts by the state to bring peace to the province were stalled or remain unimplemented. In order to truly stabilise Balochistan and bring lasting peace to this troubled province, a credible political process must begin, with the state guaranteeing that it will fully implement previous promises, as well as new agreements. While speaking at the Balochistan National Workshop in Islamabad recently, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif called for a “grand, frank and meaningful dialogue” to address the province’s issues. He added that the province’s people had first right over its resources. In a similar vein the chief of defence forces stressed the need for empowering Balochistan’s youth. It is certainly welcome that the rulers see the need to tackle Balochistan’s political problems through dialogue. While kinetic measures are needed to deal with immediate terrorist threats, the underlying factors fuelling discontent can only be handled through a political process. The state must keep in mind previous attempts to address the province’s problems. For example, the PPP government had in 2009 launched the Aghaz-i-Huqooq-i-Balochistan package. Despite the passage of 17 years, many of the package’s key points remain unimplemented. For example, it had proposed releasing political workers, except those involved in heinous crimes. However, over the past few months, we have seen peaceful activists rounded up and thrown in jail. Similarly, the 2009 package had sought to address the province’s socioeconomic woes; suffice it to say, social indicators in Balochistan remain miserable. The PML-N had launched a package of its own in 2015. Much of that scheme also remains unfulfilled. CPEC was similarly supposed to transform the province’s fortunes. It has not. CORPORATE WINDOW: The crossroads of collapse

Five years after the Taliban returned to Kabul, Afghanistan’s economic relationship with Pakistan is undergoing a structural shift. For Khyber-Pakhtunkhwa, particularly Peshawar, this is not simply a bilateral trade issue. It is increasingly a question of industrial resilience and competitiveness. The clearest evidence is Afghanistan’s transit trade through Pakistan. Container traffic, which had risen to nearly 89,000 containers worth about $5 billion before the Taliban returned to power, briefly climbed to a record 102,886 containers valued at $6.7bn in FY23. It then went into reverse. Volumes fell to 54,114 containers in FY24 and 42,959 in FY25, worth $1.36bn. In FY26, they collapsed to just 11,592 containers valued at $367 million, according to customs data cited in a recent Dawn report. The numbers matter because they show that Pakistan’s October 2025 border restrictions did not create the decline; they accelerated a process that was already under way. As Afghanistan shifts imports towards Iran and Central Asia, Pakistan risks losing not only transit trade but also the industrial and commercial ecosystem built around it Kabul has been deliberately diversifying its trade routes, with Iran emerging as the most important alternative. The World Bank’s Afghanistan Economic Monitor reports that the Iranian corridor has become the backbone of Afghanistan’s import supply chain.
