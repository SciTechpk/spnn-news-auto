Breaking the cycle

THE World Bank’s latest Pakistan Development Update sets out what faster reforms could deliver for the economy. If policies and institutions matched those of an average middle-income country, it estimates, annual growth could reach 6.2-6.9pc, enough to reach Vietnam’s current income level, and incomes would triple by 2050. The distance from here is long. Real GDP growth rose to 3.65pc in FY26 and the primary surplus reached 2.9pc of GDP. But per capita income is currently growing at only about 1.6pc, which means the economy has been put in better financial order without making most people better off. The country’s return to macroeconomic stability is widely appreciated by international lenders and credit agencies. However, it is not the same thing as a solid foundation for rapid growth. Much of this stability has come from holding the economy down. High interest rates, fiscal tightening, and costly energy have dampened demand and squeezed imports, easing pressure on the external account. Workers’ remittances have helped the current account and rebuilt reserves, and continued external borrowing has bought time. But none of this builds capacity. Borrowing defers the bill, remittances bring in dollars without adding to domestic output, and compressed imports can fix the external balance while leaving exports no more competitive. The stability that results depends on inflows continuing and on the government keeping up its restraint. That is why any real push for growth can be risky. It would mean more imported machinery, fuel, industrial inputs, and consumer goods. If exports and productivity do not keep pace, the current account slips, reserves fall, and policymakers enforce tightening again. We have been through this cycle many times. Breaking it means changing what the economy produces and sells, not just how fast it is allowed to grow. The obstacles are well known: unreliable and costly energy, a narrow tax base, cumbersome regulation, weak competition, and a poorly skilled workforce. Policy keeps changing, which deters long-term investment. Tax reform must bring undertaxed sectors into the net, instead of increasing the burden on those already compliant. Energy reform has to cut system costs rather than shift them between consumers and the budget. And exports need a policy that works beyond incentives. Macroeconomic stability cannot carry the economy much further without better productivity. The World Bank’s numbers are best read as a reform target. Reaching Vietnam’s income level will take sustained spending on education, health, infrastructure, and technology, and institutions that can be trusted. It will also take growth that creates more than just better headline figures. If the government does not use the present breathing space to raise productivity, boost exports, and complete lasting energy and tax reforms, the next growth push could bring back the crisis Pakistan has just come out of. ECP proposes AI rules for parties, media, election observers

ISLAMABAD: The Election Commission of Pakistan (ECP) has proposed amendments to the codes of conduct for political parties and contesting candidates, media and election observers to protect the electoral process from the harmful effects of artificial intelligence (AI). The commission has shared the draft amendments with stakeholders and sought their feedback by Nov 20. According to a draft notification issued on Friday, the ECP cited its constitutional duty under Article 218(3) to ensure honest, just and fair elections. “Therefore, it is the core responsibility of the commission to take all necessary steps to guard against corrupt practices, which also include manipulation of the electoral process through misleading content generated using AI,” the notification said. Draft bars digitally manipulated ‘defamatory content’; feedback sought by Nov 20 Under the proposed rules, political parties and contesting candidates would be required to clearly disclose all AI-generated campaign material, including deepfake images, audio, video and synthetic voices. Robocalls and voter outreach messages using cloned voices would have to disclose their AI-generated nature at the outset. The draft prohibits AI-generated content intended to deceive voters or misrepresent individuals. It also bans false, derogatory or defamatory deepfake statements targeting candidates, party leaders, supporters, election officials, the judiciary, state functionaries or the armed forces. Political parties would be barred from using automated bots or acco­unts to artificially inflate likes, comments, shares or followers to create a false impression of public support. They would also be required to retain logs of all AI-assisted campaign content and submit them to the ECP upon request. Print, electronic and digital media outlets, as well as social media influencers, would be required to explicitly label AI-altered election content. Media houses would remain legally responsible for the factual accuracy of content produced using AI tools for translation, anchoring or analysis. The Pakistan Electronic Media Regulatory Authority, the Social Media Protection and Regulatory Authority and the National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency will actively monitor online platforms. They will submit compliance reports to the ECP, which retains the authority to order immediate content takedowns or re-labelling. Election observers would be required to identify and promptly report AI-generated deepfakes and disinformation campaigns directly to the ECP without further spreading the misleading material.
