Constitutional liability

EVERY out-of-school child is a living reminder to the state of its constitutional commitment, and these reminders have been increasing since the inception of Pakistan. Now these living reminders have crossed 25 million. Seventy-nine years after independence, Pakistan is still struggling to fulfil one of the most fundamental responsibilities of a modern state, ensuring that every child receives an education. A nuclear state has produced generations without an education system capable of accommodating them. Pakistan’s failure to educate its children is not a recent phenomenon, nor did the constitutional responsibility of the state begin with Article 25-A in 2010. The commitment dates back to the country’s early constitutional history. The 1956 Constitution explicitly recognised the state’s responsibility to eliminate illiteracy and provide free and compulsory primary education. The 1962 Constitution retained this commitment. The 1973 Constitution went further through Article 37(b), requiring the state to remove illiteracy and provide free and compulsory secondary education within the minimum possible period. The insertion of Article 25-A through the 18th Constitutional Amendment in 2010 was therefore not the beginning of the state’s educational responsibility. It was an attempt to transform a longstanding constitutional aspiration into an enforceable fundamental right. Yet, decades of constitutional commitments have failed to produce universal educational access. From the promise of compulsory primary education in 1956 to the guarantee of education for every child aged 5-16 in 2010, the state has repeatedly renewed its commitment without ensuring its fulfilment. Every child denied education represents lost human capital. The question is why, despite repeated constitutional commitments, successive governments have failed to ensure that every child has access to a school. It seems every government has also failed to realise that every child denied education represents lost human capital and another potential victim of intergenerational poverty. A country cannot build a knowledge economy, strengthen democratic institutions, or achieve sustainable development while leaving millions of its children disconnected from education and the opportunities that accompany it. The children are not the liability. The liability is created by a system that allows millions of children to grow up without education, skills, opportunities, or meaningful participation in society. Unesco data indicates that Pakistan has a substantially higher proportion of primary-school-age children out of school compared with India, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, the Maldives, and Sri Lanka. While these countries have made considerable progress in expanding educational access, Pakistan continues to struggle with millions of children excluded from the education system. This regional disparity suggests that the crisis is not merely a consequence of poverty or population growth, but also reflects persistent failures of educational planning, governance, investment and political commitment. Bringing over 25m out-of-school children into the education system is not merely a matter of an enrolment campaign. It requires an enormous expansion of educational infrastructure, human resources and recurring public expenditure. Assuming 40 students per classroom, Pakistan would need approximately 628,750 classrooms to accommodate these children. At an illustrative staffing ratio of 1.2 teachers per classroom, the country would require approximately 754,500 additional teachers. If these teachers were recruited at the initial BPS-14 pay of Rs27,060 per mon­th, the annual salary bill alone would be roughly Rs245 billion. This estim­ate covers basic salaries only, excludes allowances, pensi­o­­ns, and teacher tr­­a­ining. The capital cost of constructing schools with basic amenities, as well as recurring administrative expenditure, would certainly be a substantially higher fiscal commitment. The scale of the challenge is enormous, so is the cost of inaction. Pakistan’s reported education expenditure of Rs962bn in FY2024-25 was already insufficient to meet the needs of its existing education system. The question is therefore not simply how much it would cost to educate the excluded children, but why a country that has constitutionally guaranteed education to every child has failed to develop a sustainable financing mechanism to fulfil that guarantee. A constitutional right cannot remain confined to the pages of a constitution, nor can the state continue to measure its educational achievements through policies announced, schools inaugurated, or budgets allocated. The real measure of its commitment must be the child sitting in a classroom, learning to read, write, think, and participate in society. The writer is a social development practitioner. More credit, fewer women

IMAGINE Sakina, up at the crack of dawn to go see her precious Billo, a fine cow her father had given her. This would be Sakina’s 30th day selling Billo’s milk to a nearby farm owner who wanted good milk for his young children. She was excited to get paid. She got ready to head out, feeling like a businesswoman. Three years later, Sakina has four animals. She feeds them, looks after them and gets the milk ready for collection. She knows when a cow is giving less milk, when feed becomes heavier on the pocket and when it makes sense to buy another animal. Her 40-plus customers know how rich the milk is. But ask them where it comes from and they’ll tell you it’s from Kareem bhai, the doodh wala. Kareem is Sakina’s husband. He delivers the milk and his name is also the one on the ledger that she has maintained all these years. Sakina is sure she could manage 10 animals. She decides to start with two more cows and goes to a bank for a loan. That’s when the name in the ledger becomes a problem. She has years of milk sales to show the bank. She’s kept the records herself but they are in Kareem’s name. The bank decides against giving her a loan. Pakistan’s agricultural lending ambitions must look beyond the size of the portfolio. The State Bank of Pakistan’s Banking on Equality figures show that agricultural financing to women rose from Rs44 billion in 2021 to Rs105bn in 2025 in nominal terms. In those same years, the number of women agricultural borrowers fell from 436,000 to 377,000, a decline of about 14 per cent. The data doesn’t show why fewer women borrowed or who received the additional financing. Yet the amounts lent more than doubled while the number of women borrowers fell. That matters for Banking on Equality 2026. The policy asks banks and development finance institutions to aim to double their financing portfolios for women and qualifying women-owned or women-led businesses across priority sectors by the end of 2028. A bank can grow its loan book by lending more to people it already knows. Reaching a woman like Sakina takes a closer look at who’s been running the business all these years. Agriculture accounts for roughly 23pc of Pakistan’s GDP, and livestock makes up nearly two-thirds of the sector’s value added. The Labour Force Survey 2024-25 tells us that about six in 10 employed women work in agriculture, forestry or fishing. What’s harder to see is who gets paid for that work and who decides how the money is spent. Take three women who look after cattle. One is paid a wage. Another tends the family’s animals and earns no income of her own. The third runs a dairy business like Sakina’s. To decide if a loan makes sense, a bank needs to understand how each woman earns her living and how much of that income she can use to repay it. Sakina’s milk delivery records could give the bank a place to start. Then come the questions she already asks herself. How much does feed cost? What happens to her income when a cow stops producing milk? What does she already owe? Banking on Equality encourages banks to consider evidence like this when assessing women-owned and women-led businesses. There’s one question the loan officer needs to ask a woman like Sakina directly. When the buyer pays for the milk, who can use that money? A buyer could put the supplier account in her name tomorrow and still hand the cash to Kareem. If the loan is in her name, the bank will expect her to pay it back. Can she decide where to sell the milk and set aside enough for the next instalment? In Kenya, CGAP documented how Agrilife and New Kenya Cooperative Creameries used digital delivery records to help dairy suppliers get advances against milk they’d already delivered. A separate study of two Kenyan dairy cooperatives found that farmers short of cash or facing uninsured health expenses shifted sales towards buyers who paid on the spot. Financing a cow means working out what it might produce in the future. And records kept in a farmer group’s name may tell a bank very little about what one woman sold. Could we use the records buyers already keep to show what women like Sakina actually sell and earn? A provincial livestock department could bring milk buyers and banks together to work with women who already run dairy businesses and want to expand. Ask buyers to record what each woman delivers and what she’s paid. If she wants to be paid into an account, let her choose one she can operate herself. If the money is paid in cash or through several buyers, the department maintains a record. With her consent, the bank can use those records alongside its usual checks and see whether they change its decision. After a year, we should know whether banks approved applications they would previously have declined and whether more women received payments directly. We can follow the loan cycles for longer too. Could the women keep up with repayments? After feed, veterinary bills and instalments, did they have more income left? Could they decide what to do with it? SBP can do something now. Put the amount lent and the number of individual women who borrowed on the same page. Show how many took out a formal loan for the first time and where borrower numbers are falling. Sakina knows the business and what it will take to grow it. She went to the bank to get support but came back without a loan or a clear way forward. When the banks report back in 2028, we should look at how many women like Sakina got far enough to have someone sit down with them and assess their potential for growth. Sakina may still look at the instalment and decide to wait. But that should be her decision to make. The writer leads the women’s economic and digital inclusion portfolio at Karandaaz Pakistan. The Islamabad meeting is also expected to focus on finalising standard operating procedures (SOPs), establishing an implementation mechanism and defining coordination between the three countries’ political, defence and military institutions, a Pakistani official said. Saudi Arabia has faced repeated attacks by Yemen’s Ansar Allah movement, commonly known as the Houthis, targeting cities, oil facilities and shipping since fighting in Yemen’s civil war intensified in July. “Any threat to Saudi Arabia is a threat to Pakistan and Turkiye according to the pact, which is absolutely clear,” Asif said. The alliance The three countries signed the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement on Aug 7. The pact provides that an armed attack on any one member will be considered an attack on all, obliging the other two to extend assistance through available means, including military support. The SPDC, which brings together the foreign and defence ministers and chiefs of defence staff of the three countries, is the alliance’s principal decision-making forum. A permanent secretariat has been established in Riyadh to coordinate its work, with Pakistan’s retired Lt Gen Nauman Mahmood appointed as its first secretary general for a three-year term. A working committee comprising officials from member countries is also functioning. Turkiye’s ratification of the agreement has completed the accession process for all three member states, but the alliance’s institutional and operational framework remains at what Asif described as a “nascent stage”. “Initially, we need to have frequent meetings to steer what is being formulated, in a way that in the next couple of months we have everything in place so that we can move forward,” the defence minister said. The alliance has yet to publicly define its doctrine, the composition of forces, command arrangements or procedures governing a collective military response. An emergency SPDC meeting in Riyadh on Oct 5 had called for moving towards practical implementation of collective defence commitments, including arrangements for the rapid deployment of agreed forces and capabilities, subject to national laws. Wider regional tensions The alliance’s formation also comes against the backdrop of wider regional tensions involving Iran, the United States and Israel. While the three members share an interest in strengthening regional deterrence, their differing strategic priorities make it important to define the alliance’s scope without allowing it to become an instrument for wider military confrontation. Asif declined to comment on how the alliance would respond to a direct Iranian attack on Saudi Arabia, but identified Israel as a possible threat to other countries in the region. “The possibility of some conflict, or aggression from the Israeli side exists, so we need to close our ranks, the countries of this region,” he said. However, the truce collapsed, with Washington and Tehran accusing each other of violating the agreement, prompting months of shuttle diplomacy led principally by Qatar and Pakistan.
