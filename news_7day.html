‘China model’ fallacies

ECONOMIC discourse in this country, especi­al­­ly with folks in governance circles, can throw up some very interesting — as well as disturbing — insights. A recent conversation brought up the int­e­­resting case of a preference for the ‘China model’. What is their conception of this model? Briefly, the belief is in an all-powerful centre that forces its economic commands through long-term plans across the entire country, whips up economic growth through major government interventions, and leaves a limited role for the lower tiers (local) of governance. It is worth contemplating whether these views align with ground realities. A good place to start is the recent paper by Fengming Lu and Xiao Ma, ‘The rise of China’s electric vehicle industry’, which helps demolish several myths. Champion EV firms like BYD and NIO are all private firms that did not get a single penny in state grants, which, instead, went to SOEs for EV research. Even more astonishing, these firms rose in cities the state apparatus did not identify for EV cluster development. How, then, did these firms accomplish this feat? The short answer: mayors of the cities that were left out teamed up with entrepreneurs, helping them usher in the latest achievement of China’s stellar development story. Notice that city mayors have the authority to act as they please within their locality. In this case, their initiative and actions undermined the SOEs. Yet, the central government never intervened to prevent it, thus demolishing the myth of an all-powerful centre that micromanages everything and does not tolerate local authority. The Chinese state mainly acts as a facilitator for development in any way it can. In Pakistan, it’s the opposite. China has a highly competitive and well-established local tier system. Passage to the higher echelons of the central party is possible only through performance at this level. Unlike in Pakistan, there is no chance of attaining a prominent party position merely by being a scion or progeny of a party head. (Interested readers can look up President Xi Jinping’s road to the presidency.) Second, it demonstrates that the state is willing to tolerate ‘creative destruction’, a term coined by economist Joseph Schumpeter, to describe how competition and creativity can render some businesses useless. This is classic capitalism, not socialism or communism. In the race for EVs, private competitors left state-run SOEs far behind, but the centre never intervened to prevent their slide. In fact, competition is so intense that it has led to price wars, with some firms expected to fail (one county reportedly lost 6.6 billion yuan on a carmaker it supported). Similar misconceptions exist around its long-term plans. These actually contain insights of tremendous depth, coming from top-tier experts. Plans are contemplated and debated at length rather than rushed through. The economic debates preceding the 14th party congress offer an apt illustration. In the wake of the Soviet Union’s collapse, between October and December 1991 (known as ‘eleven half-days’), the debate centred on why the USSR’s economy collapsed and what could be done to prevent the same from happening to China. The end result was the adoption of the famous ‘modern enterprise system’, approved by the party in November 1993, which enunciated “clear property rights, clearly defined rights and responsibilities, separation of government and enterprise, and scientific management”, that would prove to be a critical moment in China’s march to prosperity. The gist is that prevailing conceptions of China as a centrally administered economy with a benevolent dictator at the helm directing its stellar rise are misplaced. In fact, it is arguably the most decentralised governance system in the world, with more than 80 per cent of public expenditure at the local level, and mayors who enjoy independence in their fiscal and administrative decision-making. The centre is still very powerful, but not in a manner that we understand. It has some red lines that it enforces. The rich, for example, are not allowed to attain influence to an extent that they are in a position to manipulate the state apparatus by tailoring it in their favour to the detriment of the country and its people. This explains, for example, why Communist Party members are banned from holding golf club memberships. The underlying philosophy is simple: facilitate capital creation and growth, but prevent it from working against the public good and the country. The state mainly acts as a facilitator for development in any way it can. In Pakistan, it’s the complete opposite. Big capital dictates and manipulates policies, whether through tax exemptions or trillions doled out in public-sector contracts. The sugar mafia is a pertinent example, whose powerful members, sitting in the legislature, sway policies and regulations in their favour. (Note: the above should not be taken as advocacy for anti-capital formation policies. Instead, it should be viewed in terms of regulating capital’s negative spillovers.) And lest we forget, the corrupt in China are handed the death penalty. Try naming a few cases in Pakistan where a similar predicament befell the corrupt. In essence, China’s remarkable transformation to where it stands today constitutes a long, arduous, and messy process that began with Deng Xiaoping’s 1978 reforms, with the above merely offering a glimpse of that long journey. Why then clamour for this ‘model’ that no one in our power halls would be willing to implement? After all, that would be hara-kiri for a corrupt, compromised elite which thrives on central control and illegally accrued rents via this system. Well, more than four years into the current experiment, the economy is still struggling to get out of the stagflationary rut in which it has been stuck since 2022; the average GDP growth has barely surpassed population growth, and all the ‘game changers’ (SIFC, $6 trillion mineral wealth, corporate farming, crypto, and geopolitical rents) have fizzled out. Expectedly, in comes yet another game changer — the ‘China model’. I cannot speak for others, but if it were up to me, I’d start implementing this model tomorrow. I assume, though, that once the enthusiastic folks in power circles come to grips with what it really implies, they’ll discard it immediately. The writer is an economist. Politics of agitation

SEPTEMBER emerged as a politically charged month. The PTI and Jamaat-i-Islami announced their plans for long marches on Islamabad. The simultaneous mobilisation by the two parties had raised concerns about potentially prolonged street protests and disruptions in the capital. However, both parties postponed their marches (although the PTI has indicated it would begin its protest over the weekend), which temporarily eased the immediate concern of large-scale agitation. But, despite the postponements, the episode has once again highlighted the recurring pattern of opposition parties resorting to street agitation. It has raised several questions regarding the rationale of this politics of agitation. Why do some political parties in Pakistan consistently resort to street agitation, road blockades, and prolonged sit-ins, while others continue to place their faith in parliamentary procedures and institutional mechanisms? What does this divergence reveal about the country’s political cultu­re? These questions lie at the heart of understanding the recurring cycle of protests that has come to define much of Pakistan’s contemporary politics. In Pakistan’s political culture, agitation has recently served as a potent instrument for opposition parties to convert street power into tangible political gains through the ballot box. By orchestrating prolonged protests and sit-ins, parties have succeeded in mobilising their grassroots workers, reinvigorating cadre loyalty, and projecting an im­­­age of resistance against the status quo. Ex­­ces­sive media coverage provides publicity that often elevates relatively smaller actors to the level of na­­tional relevance, while the narrative of standing up for the ‘common man’ against an unresponsive system helps cultivate a saviour-like persona. This dynamic was most visibly demonstrated by the PTI, whose 2014 Islamabad dharna and 2016 ‘lockdown’ agitation not only kept the party in the headlines for months but also laid the organisational and perceptual groundwork for its dramatic electoral expansion in 2018. Through this strategy, the PTI secured phenomenal electoral gains, from a modest 34 National Assembly seats in 2013 to 120 seats in 2018. Since 2012, the state’s response to political agitation has undergone a noticeable evolution. Similar patterns are evident at the regional levels. The TLP registered itself as a political party in 2016 and after its long march on Islamabad in November 2017, it remarkably secured 2.2 million votes in the 2018 elections. Maulana Hidayatur Rehman’s sustained agitation in Gwadar transformed him from a marginal local political figure into a formidable voice of Gwadar, while Hafiz Naeemur Rehman’s high-visibility protests in Karachi have allowed the Jamaat-i-Islami to reclaim political space in urban Sindh. It is precisely this proven formula of converting agitation into both visibility and voter sympathy that appears to have encouraged the current Jamaat chief to attempt replicating the strategy on a national scale. At its core, however, the recurring resort to agitation also reflects a deep lack of trust in the government, legitimacy of the electoral system, and the taxation system, compounded by the widespread perception of corruption. When citizens believe that taxes are routinely misappropriated rather than spent on public welfare, protests become the default language of political expression, as institutional channels are viewed as ineffective or compromised. However, this strategy of political agitation also carries significant socioeconomic costs. Prolonged protests and road blockades not only disrupt daily economic activities, they also impose heavy financial burdens on businesses, trade, and ordinary citizens. The PTI’s 2014 Islamabad sit-ins, which stretched for 126 days, and the subsequent waves of agitation in later years, created extended periods of uncertainty, including around 152 days of political and economic instability within a span of two years. As per a recent statement by the finance minister, every single day of protest may inflict an economic loss of around Rs120 billion on Pakistan’s economy. When this figure is applied to the 152 days of protests witnessed over the past decade, the cumulative financial damage is staggering and exposes the severe economic cost of sustained political agitation. These disruptions led to substantial losses in the trade, transport, and retail sectors, while simultaneously damaging market sentiment and eroding investor confidence, as prolonged street unrest signals political instability and policy unpredictability. Since 2012, the state’s response to political agitation has undergone a noticeable evolution. In the earlier phase, the government largely avoided direct confrontation. Instead, it opted for dialogue and negotiation. Federal ministers were frequently nominated by the prime minister of the time to engage with protest leaders, as seen during Tahirul Qadri’s 2012-13 sit-ins in Islamabad, the PTI’s prolonged 2014 dharna, and the TLP’s 2017 long march. These episodes reflected the government’s preference for political management over force. From 2012 to 2017, the state apparatus largely attempted to defuse tensions through concessions or mediated settlements rather than immediate crackdowns. Over time, however, this approach has shifted towards greater repression. Successive governments have increasingly relied on heavy-handed tactics, including the use of force, communication blackout, mass arrests, and restrictions on movement to disperse protests. While this strategy has, to some extent, discouraged repeated large-scale national-level agitations by raising the cost of mobilisation, it has simultaneously deepened public resentment towards state institutions. The perception of an increasingly authoritarian response has further eroded public trust in the system, and reinforced the very sense of institutional alienation that often fuels street protests in the first place. Although both the Jamaat and PTI postponed their long marches to Islamabad, the underlying tension between the opposition’s inclination towards street agitation and the government’s readiness to suppress such mobilisations remains. This recurring pattern continues to raise important questions: Can Pakistan continue to afford the repeated economic and institutional costs of agitation and repression? Or will the state and political actors eventually recognise the need to strengthen formal institutions such as the judiciary, parliament, and the standing committees of the National Assembly and Senate so that grievances can be resolved through dialogue and established democratic mechanisms rather than through the streets? COMMENT: Will Afghanistan Change?

THE evolution of the Pakistan-Afghanistan relationship has always been inconsistent, and rooted in deep mistrust, blame games, propaganda, conspiracy theories, perpetual external interference and repeated cycles of violence. The bilateral equation has experienced many irritants, each having abundant potential for a complete breakdown. Despite these complexities and substantial investments by external players, the relationship has somehow survived every dilemma, only to experience new and more challenging negative developments. The current state of bilateral tensions – though one of the most damaging – is nothing new and should be seen in context. Since August 1947, there has never been a decade in which Afghanistan did not make a deliberate effort to export insurgencies and unrest to Pakistan, mostly by exploiting internal socio-political grievances within segments of Pakistani society, or by capitalising on ethnic and linguistic affiliations. These offensive trends were initiated by Afghanistan in the pursuit of enduring objectives such as Lar-O-Bar Yaw Afghan, Pakhtunistan, historical claims and, at times, on the behest of third parties pursuing inimical motives towards the integrity and sovereignty of Pakistan. The concept of ‘strategic depth’ was relevant till the conclusion of World War II. Given its hostile conduct since Aug 15, 1947, no sane person could have imagined such ‘depth’ for Pakistan on Afghan territory On the other hand, Pakistan persistently claimed to be working for a stable, peaceful, and friendly Afghanistan, objectives that to this day are interpreted as a quest for ‘strategic depth’. Irrespective of the past statements by a couple of leaders, it is naïve to consider that Pakistan was pursuing ‘strategic depth’ in Afghanistan. In the security domain, the concept of ‘strategic depth’ was relevant till the conclusion of World War II, and was generally understood as a mutually-agreed arrangement under which one nation-state could place its most precious military hardware and forces in a neighbouring state, while facing military aggression from an enemy. Given Afghanistan’s very hostile conduct since August 15, 1947, no sane person could have imagined such ‘strategic depth’ for Pakistan on Afghan territory while facing a military threat from its arch-rival, India. Successive military leaders, the intelligence community and political forces envisioned a stable and friendly relationship with Afghanistan that would afford them an environment to concentrate their entire military potential on the eastern border with India, without being distracted by the defence of western frontiers. During previous crises in Afghanistan, successive Pakistani governments have opted for alignments with important factions primarily for post-crisis normalcy. This was primarily done to help dominant factions or external players create a stable government in Kabul. Losing the north? The much-maligned support for the Taliban’s rise in 1996 also had the same objective. Most of these efforts by Pakistan, however, were not viewed favourably by the people of Afghanistan, particularly by ethnic communities in the north of Hindu Kush range. One can conclude that while supporting dominant players in the Pakhtun belt of Afghanistan, Pakistan lost positive relevance with the people of the north. Capitalising on the resultant opportunity to hurt Pakistan’s interests, India strongly aligned itself with communities of the north throughout the period of Afghan Jihad and for years afterwards. As of today, however, the whole paradigm appears to have undergone a tectonic shift. While people of the north appear willing to engage with Pakistan, the Pakhtun belt under the Afghan Taliban regime is accepting transformative influence and a strong relationship with India. Those interested in history can trace out the reasons behind this shift, some of which are even shaping the current conflict. Back in 2011, a Pakistani delegation visited one of the important Central Asian Republics. The delegation had an engagement to discuss Afghanistan’s situation with the defence minister of that country, who had also served in Afghanistan as a regimental commander for years during Soviet invasion. After a lengthy presentation by our delegation, when it was the hosts’ turn to present their views, the defence minister summed up Afghanistan in one sentence. He said, “Gentlemen, Afghanistan will remain Afghanistan”. This was a profound statement, as he attempted to predict Afghanistan’s future, which he thought would remain hostage to violence, tribal feuds, external influence, proxy wars, and perpetual instability. His opinion has, thus far, withstood the test of time. We should all, however, pray and hope that he is proven wrong, sooner rather than later. Perpetual instability ’Starting with the First Afghan War of 1839, repeated British invasions of Afghanistan kept the country at war until the close of the 19th century. Afghanistan’s geography, history, and culture had always shaped and sustained ethnic, linguistic, and tribal divisions for centuries. Throughout the many Afghan wars, the British capitalised on these divisions, only to further exacerbate social and political fault lines. From the beginning of the 20th century until the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, there were intervals of stability, progress, and the evolution of peculiar governance structures that relied on accommodating tribal and ethnic interests to keep various regions aligned with the government in Kabul. These limited cycles of stability, however, could never lead to enduring internal cohesion, participatory democracy, and overarching inclusivity. It will take a collective effort by the people of Afghanistan to enforce a moderate interpretation of Islam and an inclusive government on the Afghan Taliban The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979 provided an opportunity for the US to orchestrate yet another clash of political ideologies in a third country, which by that time had become a trend in the international order. The consequent so-called containment of communism through proxy warfare was a globally-shared geopolitical objective, fully backed by the US-led West and many influential nation-states in the Muslim world. Pakistan, being geographically contiguous, became the most important enabler for both logistics and training infrastructure for the Mujahideen, whose rank and file were drawn mostly from Afghanistan’s southern Pakhtun belt. Pakistan joined the effort primarily to safeguard its own security interests in the face of the Soviet military presence in Afghanistan and the wider regional consequences of the conflict. But this participation further alienated the ethnicities of the north, as well as the Pakhtun factions who later suffered at the hands of the Taliban during the 1990s. When the Soviets withdrew in 1989, Pakistan had two choices: one, leave Afghanistan to the Afghans, as was done by every country involved during the Afghan Jihad. Two, attempt reconciliation among warring factions and support most dominant group with the hope for stabilising Afghanistan’s internal dynamics. When reconciliation efforts did not work and Taliban rose as most dominant and powerful interest group, Pakistan opted to exercise second option, which later proved very costly. ‘War on terror’ At the start of 21st century, when Afghanistan was once again invaded under the garb of the ‘war on terror’, Pakistan made every possible effort to prevent the invasion. Having been unable to convince Taliban over the future of Osama bin Laden, Pakistan opted to join the US-led ‘war on terror’, in a support role, under compulsions of mandatory UN resolutions and clearly stated global priorities. During the run-up to the US exit in 2021, Pakistan’s standing among Taliban-aligned constituencies in Afghanistan, particularly in the Pakhtun belt, had also eroded as Islamabad was increasingly viewed as having sided with the US-led intervention. Paradoxically, throughout the same two decades, successive Afghan governments, Isaf, Nato, and US officials repeatedly accused Pakistan of complicity with the Taliban. Afghan Presidents Hamid Karzai and Ashraf Ghani, along with successive US political and military leaders, consistently pressed Pakistan to militarily confront the Taliban as part of the wider war effort. Islamabad, though, resisted such a course, fearing that direct confrontation could trigger serious internal instability and security blowback. At the same time, successive Pakistani governments firmly rejected allegations of state-sponsored material or financial support to the Taliban and repeatedly called for evidence establishing such institutional complicity. Despite years of accusations, no publicly disclosed evidence conclusively established direct sponsorship of the Taliban by Pakistani state institutions. Yet, an insurgency capable of sustaining a two-decade war against the world’s most powerful military alliance clearly required dependable financial, logistical and external support. Pakistani assessments have consistently maintained that significant elements of such support originated elsewhere in the region. The larger point is that allegations against Pakistan became a convenient explanation for the failures of the Afghan war, while the wider regional networks that sustained the insurgency received far less scrutiny. What Afghans must do Looking back at almost two centuries of Afghanistan’s evolution, one can conclude that Afghans have been victims of ruthless pursuit of superpowers’ geostrategic interests under the garb of the illusionary ‘Great Game’ or the global ‘war on terror’. Resultantly, generations of proud Afghans have lived through perpetual violence and bloodshed. This has led to emergence of a mindset, particularly in the Pakhtun belt in which violence, mercenaries, and proxies are viewed as a routine affair. As successive wars and insurgencies were forced upon the Afghan people by external powers, it is a moral obligation of the international community to help rebuild the country for a stable, peaceful, and prosperous future for its freedom-loving and proud people. For this to happen, Afghanistan has to move away from exclusivity and realign its statecraft with globally acceptable norms and external behaviour. Not only its external conduct; sooner or later, Afghanistan will also have to re-orientate its internal dynamics towards internationally accepted practices and norms. The Afghan Taliban regime steering such a realignment appears to be a distant possibility. It will call for a collective effort by the people of Afghan­istan to enforce a moderate interpretation of Islam and an inclusive government on the Afghan Taliban. It is time for the people of Afghanistan to understand that integration with the international political, economic, and financial order in today’s globalised world is not a matter of choice but a compulsion. They also need to understand that such integration does not call for giving up the unique ethos, values, religious sentiment, or cultural imperatives of a nation-state. The Afghan Taliban regime does not accept this publicly, but in their internal discourse, its policy towards Pakistan is driven by three overarching objectives. Firstly, they want status quo ante on the Pakistan-Afghanistan border, allowing unhindered movement of people and materials without any oversight mechanisms. Secondly, they want Afghan transit trade to continue without any new measures imposed by Pakistan. Thirdly, and most importantly, the Afghan Taliban regime has a deep desire to see a government in Pakistan that follows its interpretation of Sharia. Having defeated Isaf and Nato, the Afghan Taliban regime feels confident that its allies like Tehreek-i-Taliban Pakistan, Baloch Liberation Army and others can achi­­eve similar success against Pakistan. Alternatives Pakistan’s intelligence community has convincing and abundant evidence related to these objectives, not only to the extent of internal discussions within the Afghan Taliban regime but also reg­arding their future plans and support for terrorist organisations working tow­ards such an agenda against Pakistan. Pakistan has made every possible effort to convince the Afghan Taliban regime to abandon such objectives and work towards peaceful coexistence for realisation of benefits of geo-economic connectivity. Since 2021, there have been repeated engagements by Pakistan on political, diplomatic, intelligence, and military tracks with Afghan counterparts but every engagement has led to an impetus both in the frequency and lethality of terrorist attacks emanating from Afghan soil. Against these misplaced ambitions, Pakistan has been offering them three alternatives. First, Pakistani interlocutors have been pleading with the Afghan Taliban regime to select any border management arrangement with any of their neighbours as a model and enforce the same on Pakistan-Afghanistan border through reciprocal efforts. Second, they have always asked the Afghan Taliban regime leadership to double the volume of transit trade but align it with existing mechanisms used in any of transit-trade models for landlocked countries around the world. Third, they have consistently argued that Pakistani society is inherently moderate, despite the marginal representation of far-right political forces our people envision their future in participatory democracy. Hence, they must walk away from the shared vision of terrorist organisations seeking to enforce a government based on extreme interpretation of Sharia. Publicly and through mediations, Pakistan has been consistently asking Afghan Taliban regime to come good on commitments made at Doha in preventing use of Afghan soil by terrorist organisations operating against Pakistan. The Afghan Taliban regime continues to hide behind capacity issues and obligations under ‘Pakhtunwali’ as hindrances to controlling terrorist organisations, despite accepting their presence on Afghan soil in closed-door meetings with Pakistani counterparts. Having made every possible and conceivable effort within the realm of soft power, Pakistan recently embarked upon the use of hard power to convince the Afghan Taliban regime to abandon its dangerous objectives against Pakistan. Debate over kinetic actions Having exhausted political, diplomatic and other non-kinetic avenues over an extended period, Pakistan has increasingly resorted to kinetic measures that it describes as necessary acts of self-defence against terrorism emanating from Afghan soil. Few states confronted with sustained cross-border terrorism would indefinitely exclude the use of force. Nevertheless, some political forces within Pakistan have expressed reservations over the use of hard power and continue to advocate greater engagement with Kabul. Such differences are part of Pakistan’s political discourse, but they can also be exploited by the Afghan Taliban to misread Pakistan’s resolve or expect divisions within its polity to constrain a sustained response. For the policy shift to remain effective and nationally sustainable, these differences should be addr­es­sed through structured political consultation and the sharing of credible information regarding the nature and scale of the threat. Pakistan should therefore maintain consistency in its security policy while keeping open every credible opportunity for direct, bilateral or mediated engagement with Kabul. The Afghan Taliban regime also needs to understand that terrorist organisations or insurgents usually succeed when they operate against a state apparatus that is not defended by strong national armed forces. Their own triumphs in 1996 and 2021 were primarily because of the absence of strong Afghan national armed forces. The Afghan National Army, raised, funded, and trained by Isaf, had yet to transform into a truly national army; hence, the Taliban succeeded in taking over Kabul without any resistance. No matter how many proxies operate from Afghanistan, they will not be able to experience such a walk in the park against Pakistan. It will not happen even in the decades ahead. If Afghanistan chooses not to carry the burdens of its past into its future, it can unlock immense opportunities for its own people, and for the wider region. The responsibility now rests with the Afghan Taliban regime to recognise that governing a state demands a fundamentally different mindset from fighting an insurgency. Afghanistan’s future will depend on whether the Taliban can make that transition, from a guerrilla movement shaped by decades of war into a responsible leadership capable of pursuing peace, stability, economic integration and constructive relations with its neighbours. One can only hope that this transformation begins before another generation of Afghans is condemned to suffer the consequences of repeating the cycles of the past. Disaster was averted on flight FZ1073 from Dubai after the wounded captain unlocked the cockpit door, allowing passengers to subdue the attacker. Passengers described ru­­shing the cockpit after the flight suddenly nosedived, losing nearly 14,000 feet of altitude in less than 30 seconds over Saudi Arabia. Israel says initial assessment points to co-pilot acting alone; mental health concerns not ruled out The airline stated that an off-duty Dubai Reserve crew member travelling on the flight diverted and safely landed the aircraft in Tabuk. Both pilots were hospitalised after sustaining injuries. The UAE’s chief prosecutor said an investigation has been launched to dete­rmine circumstances and motives, including whe­th­­er the incident was linked to terrorist activity or inv­olved prior planning. UAE judicial authorities have jurisdiction because the aircraft flies under its flag. Flydubai suspended flig­­hts to Israel during the inv­estigation and warned aga­inst premature speculation. Two Israeli security officials said on Thursday that Israel’s initial assessment is that the co-pilot, identified by an Israeli official as an Omani national, likely acted alone. Israel’s envoy to the UAE, Yossi Shelley, cautioned that no motive had been determined. Netanyahu called the event a serious security incident, but told CBS News that mental health could not be ruled out. “He’ll be, I assume, moved very quickly to Dubai,” Netanyahu spoke of the alleged attacker, who was being questioned in Saudi Arabia. The report stated that while it PTA acknowledged the importance of protecting children from online harms, the relief sought by the petitioner — specifically a universal minimum age for social media accounts — travelled beyond its existing legal mandate. In its report, the PTA highlighted a major transition in the regulatry landscape. “With the promulgation of the Prevention of Electronic Crimes (Amendment) Act, 2025, a new body — the Social Media Protection and Regulatory Authority (SMPRA) — is set to replace the PTA regarding the regulation of social media platforms,” it maintained.
