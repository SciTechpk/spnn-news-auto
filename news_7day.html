In the Iraqi oil hub of Basra, maintenance contractor Fawzi spends his days cooling his heels waiting for work that seldom comes, as the Middle East war puts a serious crimp on the once-bustling port town. Basra has long been a key cog in Iraq’s economy but, after months of Iran choking off Gulf oil exports with its blockade in the Strait of Hormuz, the city is suffering. Fuel trucks stand idle and workers face pay cuts and lay-offs. “I haven’t done anything in a month,” Fawzi told AFP. “What are we supposed to maintain?” Many Basra residents have lost their jobs or taken heavy pay cuts as private contractors, as oil fields and the Umm Qasr cargo port scale back operations. Crude oil exports account for almost 90 per cent of Iraq’s foreign revenue, and are primarily shipped through Hormuz via the cluster of facilities around Basra, the country’s only gateway to the Gulf. Fawzi said his company first slashed salaries by 20pc, then by half, before laying off dozens of employees. He now reports for duty just 15 days a month. Basra’s parking areas overflow with oil trucks. Some linger empty, while others wait for weeks with full tanks to load their crude onto ships. Cheap oil Before the war started at the end of February, Iraq produced around four million barrels of crude per day and exported an average of 3.4 million, mostly via Hormuz. But when Iran blockaded Hormuz, onshore storage quickly filled up, forcing Iraq to slash production. Experts have long warned Iraq to introduce different income streams to cushion swings in the energy market. But with a fledgling private sector, poor banking system and dilapidated infrastructure, diversification is difficult. Iraq has sought to calm fears by using alternative routes, including trucks through Syria and a pipeline to Turkey, but they can handle only a fraction of the sea-bound trade. After a sharp drop, Iraqi oil exports through Hormuz came climbing back in the first week of September, data from the maritime tracking firm Kpler showed. However, Iraq sold the oil at “discounted prices”, a government official said. According to authorities, Iraq exported an average of 2.6m bpd so far in September. Iraq relies heavily on foreign currency from oil sales to pay civil servants, finance imports and stabilise the dinar. The collapse in these revenues forced authorities to borrow internally. While the government hopes it can continue borrowing for months, “it’s probably banking on a resolution to the conflict before the end of this year”, economic expert Ali al-Mawlawi said. The recent partial recovery in oil exports bought the government “a little more time”, and allowed it to pay salaries with minor delays. “The imperative is to ensure that it can continue” to do so, Mawlawi warned. State port workers lost their monthly incentives, which are linked to the port’s profits and had supplemented their pay. Pak-US ties: a new chapter for an old friendship

Pakistan-US ties may have witnessed many vicissitudes over the years. But where some saw a bleak future, primarily because they wished it so, a new hope has emerged for a more consistent, balanced and mutually beneficial partnership set on a solid foundation of common aspirations and shared perceptions. The forthcoming talks planned between Pakistani and US leadership on the margins of the UN General Assembly offer both sides a wondrous opportunity to re-envision bilateral ties in a broader spectrum of cooperation: more meaningful, multidimensional, and consistent. Given the expanding scope of engagement between the two countries in recent times, there is now both an opportunity and a compelling rationale to orient the relationship toward a more forward-looking framework — one attuned to the new era in which fast-moving technological, economic and geopolitical shifts carry profound implications. This calls for a reinvigorated partnership, unbound by the exigencies of the moment and grounded instead in the enduring aspirations and interests of both nations. Washington has long recognised that Pakistan matters in its own right, by virtue of its demography, diplomacy, geography, and strategic weight. Its location, at the confluence of South Asia, Central Asia, Middle East and the Arabian Sea, gives it intrinsic relevance to regional and global connectivity, trade and security. A population exceeding 250 million makes it one of the world’s largest potential markets and a vast reservoir of human capital, while its geography offers a natural bridge to resource-rich economies of Central Asia, to say nothing of Pakistan’s own substantial, still under-explored resource endowments. Its diplomatic reach has been on full display this year as Islamabad worked to bring stability to the wider region, most notably through its widely noted mediatory role during the recent US-Iran-Israel conflict. A historic relationship These factors will shape the forward-looking cast of the discussions in New York. But history too will loom over the encounter as both sides survey the possibilities ahead. Pakistan and the United States have cooperated since 1947 at several defining moments in world affairs. Pakistan was a key American ally during the Cold War and instrumental in facilitating the opening between Nixon’s Washington and Mao’s Beijing, arguably one of the most consequential turns in international affairs since World War II. Islamabad also bore enormous human and economic costs standing alongside the United States and the international community during the War on Terror. These episodes point to something essential: the relationship has repeatedly served as a fulcrum for shaping the international system at moments of the greatest strain or change. Yet two other threads run through this history: a lack of continuity and a narrowness of engagement. A forward-looking relationship must reckon with the need for stable, sustained bilateral contact and a broader horizon of connectivity. The avenues for doing so are well known — strategic dialogue, parliamentary exchanges, economic consultations, business-to-business investment, greater multinational presence, educational and scientific partnerships, people-to-people ties and regular engagement between diplomatic, defence and commercial institutions. Such mechanisms have long existed; what has been missing is continuity at meaningful scale. Only through that consistency can a durable architecture emerge, one dynamic enough to evolve as circumstances change. The path ahead With continuity and breadth in mind, the most promising path to a sustainable partnership runs through economics. There is considerable room to expand trade, investment, technology and enterprise. Pakistan’s mineral potential, including critical minerals, dovetails with America’s growing interest in resilient and diversified supply chains. But the real prize lies further downstream, in processing capacity, technology partnerships, industrial value chains, skills development and manufacturing within Pakistan itself. Similar opportunities exist in information technology, artificial intelligence, agriculture, energy, advanced manufacturing, health and digital services. Pakistan’s young population and growing technology sector offer American companies an increasingly attractive platform for new markets, new talent and new production partnerships at a notably competitive cost. Security cooperation remains an important pillar, but it too can take on a broader, more forward-looking character. Pakistan has paid an extraordinary human and economic price to terrorism and continues to face extremist threats in its neighbourhood. Washington and Islamabad have reaffirmed their determination to cooperate against terrorist organisations and to strengthen border security and counterterrorism capacity. But the purpose of that cooperation, described by American military officials as a “phenomenal” partnership just last year — should be to help build a regional environment in which economic development, connectivity, and human security can flourish. A stable Pakistan is not merely a domestic aspiration; it serves the regional and international interest, since stability strengthens the prospects for trade, connectivity, investment and moderation across a region of great strategic significance. As the coming dialogue should underscore, the convergence between Pakistani and American interests runs wider than is sometimes acknowledged. Both countries benefit from a more peaceful South Asia, a more connected Central Asia, and a more integrated regional economy. And a broadening Pakistan- US relationship would help avert ‘Thucydides Trap’ playing out in South Asia. Equally importantly, both countries have a stake in denying terrorism new space — particularly in view of the evolving situation in Afghanistan — and in expanding legitimate commerce and ensuring young populations are drawn toward opportunity rather than instability. Pakistan has repeatedly shown it can serve as a bridge between countries that do not easily communicate with one another — including most recently Iran and the United States. This could add another dimension to the bilateral cooperation framework. The world is passing through a period of transition that demands new pillars of stability and new engines of growth. This is where Pakistan and the United States have the opportunity to write a new chapter — one that can surpass those that came before it, if built on a broad scope of engagement and genuine continuity. What is required now of both sides is an orientation toward enduring possibilities of the relationship: deep enough to withstand occasional differences of perspectives and ambitious enough to help build a more connected, prosperous and peaceful region.

A question of region

“Don’t know much about history, don’t know much biology,Don’t know much about the science book, don’t know much about the French I took…” THIS is an old song, which seems to be an apt description of journalists — especially in Pakistan. Over the decades, the profession has moved on from print to television and now digital but our training remains rooted in old-fashioned ‘generalism’ (if there’s such a word). As with the bureaucrats who run petroleum one day and health the other, we journalists move from commenting on foreign affairs one evening to the economy and healthcare the next. The notion of specialising in issues or studying them in depth is as alien to us as democracy is to Islamabad’s neighbours. Perhaps this is why most in the profession rarely bother with reading and books. Take history — even of Pakistan. Those of us who entered the field at the time of newspapers learnt the country’s history through oral tradition. Late-night chats with senior colleagues, who had survived martial laws and newsroom working hours, told us of tumultuous times such as war, secession and dictatorship. They included those who remembered what it was like working during the censorship of Ziaul Haq’s times, the night Pervez Musharraf removed the government or when an operation was first launched against the MQM in Karachi. The oral history we learnt alongside copy editing was the best counter-narrative to the whitewashed account taught in school books under Pakistan Studies. Our daastango provided us with a fun but patchy account of the land of the pure. Patchy, for it leaves us with more questions than answers — the personal accounts that we learn history from are rooted in Pakistan but are a little sketchy on details of the region. Few journalists have travelled around the region and reported on other countries. As a result, many may point out similarities between Pakistan and Egypt (aren’t they obvious?) but few will note what makes them different and why. For a hack such as myself, the biggest difference is perhaps Pakistan’s impatience with its rulers. Our last dictator spent eight years in power, struggling in the last two. And in Egypt, Anwar Sadat spent 11 years in power, Hosni Mubarak around three decades, while Abdel Fattah el-Sisi has been ruling for 12 and is still going strong. Closer to Pakistan, the Gulf states in general have also enjoyed stability and long reigns without much pressure from below. Evidently, this is due to oil, but can it explain everything? After all, Iran is no less oil-rich and yet it went through a revolution, which perhaps remains unprecedented as a political event in the region. Despite the authoritarianism of both the Shah and the theocracy that followed, Iran has demonstrated that its people believe in resistance. In recent years, they have pushed back multiple times against crackdowns; at times they have even compelled the state to change course. This is a strain missing in Iran’s neighbours to the west. Crowded, noisy and poor, societies in this region struggle with imperfect democracies. But to its east, it’s a different story. Pakistan and the rest of South Asia, in comparison to its western neighbours in the Middle East, are poorer and noisier. Is this why this region has avoided the kind of authoritarianism that marks the Middle East? Crowded, noisy and poor, societies in this region continue to struggle with flawed and imperfect democracies; there is flirtation with authoritarianism but on paper most of them adhere or aspire to the framework left by the British. This is true of Pakistan where martial law dictators have also worked hard to prove their democratic credentials by holding elections. And with it comes the inability of rulers in the region to lord it over their citizens for long periods. Indeed, in recent years, Hasina Wajed’s 15 years in power has been quite a feat. In India, Jawaharlal Nehru and Narendra Modi have also enjoyed prime ministerships for a similar period of time. On the other hand, in Pakistan, the longest power stints have been a bit shorter. In a tradition closer to Iran, South Asia has also pushed back against dictators of the civilian (Wajed) and military (Musharraf) type, with street protests forcing them to call it a day. Strongmen such as Modi have also been bested by students armed with their wit and social media, while in Pakistan, authoritarian establishments have struggled to clamp down on criticism on X. This is not entirely new: back in the 1970s, both Indira Gandhi’s imposition of the Emergency and Zulfikar Ali Bhutto’s authoritarian rule came to an end, albeit in very different ways. In one country, the democratic system pushed back while in the other, the military establishment did so. With all our problems, Pakistan’s journalists still sit in talk shows and complain about the censorship they face; their counterparts in nearby regions are perhaps not allowed to whine thus on live television. In most countries with censorship, journalists usually spend their time complaining about absolute freedom. There is an ‘argumentative’ South Asian that shapes this region differently from its western neighbours. And perhaps also its eastern ones. Travel east to Myanmar and here once again, regimes tend to become more authoritarian and more long-lasting. The long detention of Aung San Suu Kyi under a martial law regime illustrates this best. After a short experiment with elections and civilian control, Myanmar has returned to military rule but one in which contestation by the people has turned to civil war. While this may seem alien to Pakistanis, it is worrying that in parts of our country we, too, face a situation where the state appears to only control the cities. But what eventually determines the different state-society relationships in these countries? Why is Myanmar able to sustain military rule while Bangladesh is not? Or why has Pakistan, despite its close ties to the Middle East, not been able to impose a similar system of government? Civil service reform

PAKISTAN cannot become a modern state as long as its bureaucracy continues to be organised around hierarchy, seniority and generalist recruitment. The system has become better at managing careers than solving problems. It rewards position rather than expertise and performance, and protects procedure more readily than outcomes. Moreover, a bureaucracy built primarily for routine administration can hardly meet the ever-growing economic, technological, environmental and social challenges confronting the government. It is against this backdrop that a ‘new’ civil service restructuring plan has been unveiled by the planning minister. The plan proposes professional cadres, seeks changes to the CSS examination, opens up senior posts to wider competition and links performance to rewards. It also proposes a new civil-service training structure. These ideas may not be new but they do make sense. However, the problem is that the plan represents a piecemeal approach. Several reform programmes were proposed in the last couple of decades; none was implemented due to resistance from bureaucracy and lack of political will. This plan could have provided a useful starting point for comprehensive civil service reform had it not cherry-picked proposals from an earlier reform exercise. The report, on which the new proposals draw, was prepared by the Institutional Reforms Committee headed by Dr Ishrat Husain in 2021, and covered recruitment, training, career progression, specialisation, performance management, accountability and the structure of the senior bureaucracy. It explicitly said that civil service reform had to be holistic. Reforming one segment of the system without addressing the others, it warned, could backfire. That raises the more important question: why are these proposals being put forward again, instead of building on the earlier blueprint? The government needs to explain what happened to the previous reform exercise, which of its recommendations were implemented, which were rejected and why. Instead, the government appears to have taken selected recommendations from it. That risks producing another reorganisation plan without actually changing the way the bureaucracy and state function. Civil service reform is not about creating new cadres, changing examination rules or merging training institutions. It is about changing incentives and institutional behaviour. The state needs specialists who can understand increasingly complex problems. It needs managers who can deliver. It needs promotion based on performance rather than seniority alone.
