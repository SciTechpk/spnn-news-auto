Pakistan’s natural resources worth $8-10tr at 'most conservative estimate', says Dar

Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar on Friday said that the estimated worth of minerals, hydrocarbons and other natural resources in Pakistan was between $8 trillion and $10tr. And this was the “most conservative estimate”, he said while addressing a ceremony held in Karachi to mark the arrival of a Turkish seismic research vessel, Oruc Reis. The vessel, which arrived on Thursday, will be used for offshore oil and gas exploration in Pakistan. Dar began his address by welcoming the leadership and professionals of the Turkish Petroleum, as well as the captain, crew and seismic research team of Oruc Reis, and termed the occasion a “historic moment”. He noted that Oruc Reis brought “Turkish scientific expertise together with the experience and capabilities of our national energy companies to advance exploration in Pakistan’s offshore domain”. Dar added, “Every successful journey of exploration begins with knowledge. The data gathered by this vessel may deepen our understanding of the geological structures beneath the seabed and provide a stronger basis for Pakistan’s future exploration plan.” He described offshore exploration as “complex, capital-intensive and inherently uncertain”. And a seismic survey could not guarantee a commercial discovery, he continued. “But no discovery can be made without seismic and exploration,” he stressed, adding that today, Pakistan was taking that step with “confidence, responsibility and the prayers of our people”. He said Pakistan sought a long-term energy partnership with Turkiye, encompassing offshore exploration, minerals and renewable energy. “You all have witnessed three memorandums of understanding signed between the relevant Turkish and Pakistani agencies. “We want Pakistani geologists and engineers to work very closely with their Turkish counterparts. We want our universities and research institutions to develop partnerships in marine, geoscience and offshore engineering,” he said. He assured “our Turkish partners and brothers” that the government of Pakistan would extend every appropriate facilitation to the “very important mission” to be undertaken by Oruc Reis. “All offshore activities must observe the highest standards of maritime safety and environmental protection. Our seas are an economic asset, but they are also a national trust. Development and environmental stewardship must proceed together,” he stressed. Dar further said that the Turkish energy minister told him that the seismic mission would take around five to six months. “I said, you won’t be allowed to leave unless you give us good news of a real discovery,” he added. He said he would come across people who described Pakistan as a country in a debt trap. He went on to say the estimated value of the country’s natural resources, including mines and minerals, hydrocarbons, gemstones and rare earth materials, far exceeded its debt burden. “Sometimes people talk about Pakistan being in debt, being a heavily indebted country, or being in a debt-trap; I always negate that,” he said, adding that Pakistan was endowed with abundant natural resources. He thanked the Turkish government and people for standing alongside Pakistan and described the arrival of the Oruc Reis as another example of the two countries’ partnership. The arrival of the Oruç Reis vessel follows the government’s revival of offshore exploration efforts after a gap of nearly two decades. The Offshore Bid Round 2025 was launched in January last year, offering 40 offshore blocks in the Indus and Makran basins for petroleum exploration. Turkish Petroleum Offshore Company (TPOC), a subsidiary of Turkish Petroleum Corporation, has partnered with local state-owned energy companies, including Oil and Gas Development Company Limited (OGDCL), Mari Energies and Pakistan Petroleum Limited (PPL), for exploration activities in three blocks. It holds a 25 per cent interest and operatorship in the Eastern Offshore Indus-C block in the Arabian Sea, alongside stakes in two onshore blocks. Pakistan’s offshore zone covers around 300,000 square kilometres, but only 18 wells have been drilled there since independence in 1947. A basin study by US firm DeGolyer and MacNaughton has identified significant untapped hydrocarbon potential in the country’s offshore basins.

A criminal case has been registered at the NCCIA Police Station in Karachi under Sections 3 (unauthorised access to an information system or data), 4 (unauthorised copying or transmission of data), 13 (electronic forgery), 14 (electronic fraud) and 16 (unauthorised use of identity information) of the Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act (PECA), 2016, as amended in 2025. The investigation revealed that the suspect operated under the online alias “DP BRUCE” and allegedly used the cybercriminal platform LeakBase and Telegram to advertise and sell stolen databases containing sensitive information. “The compromised data allegedly included information relating to prominent US government institutions, including the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), Central Intelligence Agency (CIA), US Department of State, Department of Defence, and various US police departments, along with data associated with private businesses,” the press release stated. According to the agency, further investigations revealed that the suspect allegedly received payments for the stolen data through cryptocurrency transactions, including Bitcoin. The press release added that, acting on “credible information”, an NCCIA Karachi team conducted a raid in Karachi’s Manzoor Colony area and arrested the suspect on November 11. During the investigation, a laptop belonging to the suspect was also seized. “Preliminary technical examination revealed multiple databases and files apparently containing sensitive information associated with US government agencies, law enforcement departments, and other organisations,” the agency said. Digital evidence also pointed to the use of online platforms for the alleged sale and distribution of stolen data. “NCCIA Karachi remains committed to combating cybercrime, dismantling networks involved in the illegal trade of stolen data, and taking strict legal action against individuals involved in national and transnational cybercriminal activities,” the agency said. A day earlier, the agency claimed to have uncovered an extensive financial network allegedly involved in illegal online betting and gambling, fraudulent business operations and suspected money laundering, with an estimated transaction exposure of up to Rs1 trillion. Once unthinkable, Trump-Xi-Putin summit makes Russia and Beijing the biggest winners

SHENZHEN: In a capacious reception room within a sprawling hillside resort in the southern Chinese tech hub Shenzhen, the leaders of the world’s three biggest powers sat at a round table and spoke for 90 minutes in a meeting that was once unthinkable. The historic trilateral talks between Chinese President Xi Jinping, US President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit yielded a joint “peace declaration” and effusive assessments from the three leaders. But while some believe the success of the meeting could have a stabilising effect on an international order in turmoil, the broad consensus among analysts is that this first-of-its-kind meeting worked more as a confidence-building exercise between the rival nations rather than an immediate, concrete cure to the world’s ills. According to a joint statement released after the Nov 19 meeting, which started two hours later than scheduled and came after a separate bilateral summit between Xi and Trump, the three leaders had an “in-depth exchange of views on the current international situation” and committed themselves to maintaining strategic stability and international security. They agreed on four points, including a commitment to reform the United Nations and plans to increase the supply of refined petroleum products. The leaders also said they would hold a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing the proliferation of nuclear weapons, and agreed that the International Criminal Court (ICC) and similar institutions “must not abuse their jurisdiction”. In his opening remarks at the meeting, Xi noted the responsibilities that lie on the shoulders of major powers. “Their positive interaction is not optional, but a must,” he said, adding later that the three countries should maintain regular high-level engagement, keep channels of dialogue open at all levels and establish crisis communication and prevention mechanisms. Trump, meanwhile, continued a long-running call for a reduction in nuclear weapon stockpiles and railed against “rogue globalist organisations”, pointing the finger at the ICC, which his administration has been working to dismantle. Trump also defended his enthusiasm for the three-way talks with China and Russia, which would traditionally have been considered anathema for a US president. “No peace was ever forged by declining opportunities for engagement. Together, we are choosing a different course,” he said. Like the other two leaders, Putin noted a shared responsibility for maintaining global peace and security and spoke of shared interests in tackling issues like transnational crime. But top of his agenda was the war in Ukraine, which Putin said he had discussed when he met Xi and Trump separately. Win-win-win? Analysts told The Straits Times that all three leaders appeared to have gotten something out of the trilateral meeting, with Beijing and Russia the biggest winners. For China, being the host country meant it could shape the agenda and spotlight the country’s centrality within the global order, said Nanyang Technological University associate professor Hoo Tiang Boon, who studies China’s foreign policy. Wu Xinbo, dean of the Institute of International Studies at Fudan University in Shanghai, said the success of the meeting has had a positive effect on China’s international status and influence, and it also reflects Beijing’s diplomatic power in being able to bring the three parties together. For Putin, getting a seat at the table with China and the US is a sign to the world that Russia is a peer power, despite its growing disparities with the other two. “It’s another opportunity for Russia to break out of global isolation,” said Hoo. As for Trump, Hoo believes there is a desire on Trump’s part to reassert US global leadership after heavy midterm losses have left him a lame duck at home. “The thing about Trump is that he seems to crave attention-seeking events… He likes the optics of doing unconventional stuff that no other American leader has done and a three-way meeting is good for the cameras.” These views have been given some validation by the leaders in their comments after the meeting. In concluding remarks, Xi described the talks as “positive, constructive and fruitful” and in footage released by the Kremlin, Putin told Xi: “I don’t think anyone even expected everything to go so well.” Putin later told a press conference that the meeting was “open” and “very useful”. Symbolism over substance However, doubts remain over whether the trilateral meet will be more than a one-off photo opportunity, given the lack of concrete progress on thorny issues, including the wars in Ukraine and Iran. Hoo said the joint statement from the three leaders was a minor achievement, but a low-hanging fruit. “The meeting was certainly more symbolism than substance. But perceptions and optics matter to some extent in international politics,” he added. Others said having the meeting itself is significant as it sends out important signals that the three major powers have the capacity to manage their differences and competition through dialogue. Fudan’s Wu said: “This summit is not for negotiations… In the end, you still need the parties directly involved to resolve the issues themselves. For example, Russia and Ukraine, or the United States and Iran. That is their business. So this summit cannot directly resolve these issues.” Zhang Yun, an international relations professor at Nanjing University, made a similar point, noting that the trilateral talks can create positive momentum. “This summit is definitely a good thing as it sends a reassuring and positive signal for an uncertain world… But we should not expect one summit meeting to resolve all problems.” Cui Hongjian from Beijing Foreign Studies University said one potential benefit of the trilateral meeting is that it can help the three countries boost mutual trust, which in turn can help to stabilise the current international situation. “It’s a bit like how we understand traditional Chinese medicine. It doesn’t directly target the symptoms. It aims to improve the overall condition of the body and its constitution, working on the root cause,” added the director of the Centre for the European Union and Regional Development Studies at the university. Alarm bells for smaller countries Given the relative success of the trilateral meeting, some scholars believe there could be more trilateral interactions in the future. Nanjing University’s Zhang said he believes that Xi’s comments on the need for regular high-level exchanges mean that the trilateral format may be institutionalised. “In multilateral settings like APEC, the possibility of handling trilateral matters is high… Let’s see what happens at the G20 in Miami next month,” he added. But analysts also sounded alarm bells for middle powers and smaller countries which may feel cut out. “You don’t want rules and key decisions to be made only among those with more power,” Hoo said. For Europe, the singling out of the ICC in the leaders’ joint statement will be a concern as it reinforces the assessment that Trump is more aligned with leaders like Putin and Xi than he is with his traditional allies, said Eva Seiwert of the Mercator Institute for China Studies, a German think-tank. “But I think we can be happy as it could have gone much worse. It was all quite vague statements at the end of the day,” said the senior analyst, who studies Sino-Russian ties.

US religious freedom panel says India is targeting minorities in voter roll revisions

India has escalated its targeting of religious minorities during a voter list revision that struck off tens of millions of names nationwide, a US panel on religious freedom said on Thursday. “The Indian govt is targeting Muslim citizens by removing them from voter rolls, calling them ‘foreigners’, and now illegally deporting them,” Asif Mahmood, the chair of the US Commission on International Religious Freedom (USCIRF), said in a statement. India’s voter list revision removed 130 million names, roughly the population of Mexico, in the largest deletion of voters in the country’s history. Data shows the omissions disproportionately affect Muslims, India’s largest minority, who have faced what rights groups describe as exclusionary policies and rising discrimination since Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s Hindu nationalist Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) took power in 2014. India’s Election Commission says it was removing duplicate, deceased, shifted and otherwise ineligible voters and that anyone excluded in error can pursue appeals. The revision has become entangled with a separate drive to identify and expel suspected illegal immigrants, even though removal from the rolls does not itself establish that someone is a foreigner. “The Indian government’s expulsion of its own citizens based on their Muslim faith is a shocking escalation of tactics that target religious minorities,” said Gene Mills, a commissioner on the US panel. Mills urged the State Department to designate India as a “country of particular concern”. USCIRF is a bipartisan body of the US government that monitors religious freedom around the world and makes policy recommendations, which are not binding. Washington has not acted on its recent recommendations on India. India’s foreign ministry has cast USCIRF as biased. New Delhi has repeatedly dismissed such criticism and called it interference in the country’s internal affairs. The Indian embassy in Washington did not respond to a request for comment. The voter roll revisions sparked protests in recent weeks, with opposition groups and student demonstrators saying the revisions affected those unlikely to vote for the Hindu nationalist BJP. Rights experts condemned India’s crackdown on the protests. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said last month the issue was a “domestic political matter” and Washington would not comment on it. UN experts and human rights groups say abuse of minorities has risen under Modi. They point to rising hate speech, a religion-based citizenship law that the UN calls “fundamentally discriminatory,” the demolition of Muslim-owned properties, the removal of Muslim-majority Kashmir’s autonomy and anti-conversion legislation that challenges freedom of belief. India’s government denies being discriminatory and says its policies, including food subsidy programs and electrification drives, benefit all communities. Civil rights advocates have also been critical of the treatment of Muslims in the US while noting a rise in Islamophobia in recent years and documenting escalating anti-Muslim rhetoric from Republicans during election campaigns.

Trump's personalised diplomacy hits its limits with 3-way summit

US President Donald Trump has long revelled in the pomp and ceremony of international summitry but has often come away with little to show for his signature brand of personalised diplomacy. His meeting on Thursday with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin has starkly exposed the limits of that approach, analysts said. Trump went into the unprecedented three-way summit in the southern Chinese city of Shenzhen seeking to reassert himself internationally after being politically weakened at home with his Republican Party’s loss of control of Congress in this month’s midterm elections. He is also still locked in a tense stalemate with Iran in a war that polls show is very unpopular and which has driven down his approval ratings. But while the three leaders agreed on a symbolic “peace declaration” reaffirming that nuclear war should never be fought, they announced no plans for arms control talks long sought by Trump. They also showed no signs of advances on issues such as Iran, Ukraine and Taiwan, or on narrowing gaps over trade and artificial intelligence. Instead, the leaders appeared content to gloss over major differences, with Xi able to burnish his diplomatic credentials by hosting a big-power gathering. Putin was able to present Russia, a comparative economic lightweight, as appearing on par with the world’s two most powerful leaders without making major concessions. US President Donald Trump, China’s President Xi Jinping and Russia’s President Vladimir Putin participate in a trilateral meeting during the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) Economic Leaders’ Meeting in Shenzhen, China on Nov 19, 2026. —Reuters Some analysts said the outcome of the Shenzhen summit may actually diminish Trump’s stature on the world stage as he enters the lame-duck phase of his presidency, while bolstering two of Washington’s main adversaries. “Trump has always loved the pageantry and the sense that he’s sitting down with the most important people in the world, and he got that moment,” said Christopher Preble, a senior fellow at the Stimson Centre think tank in Washington. “But as in other summits, it’s hard to see any substantive progress made on any major issue.” For Trump, it fit the pattern of many of his leader-to-leader encounters since returning to office last year, including earlier meetings with Xi and Putin that did not yield breakthroughs, and his inconclusive first-term summits with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un. Responding to queries from Reuters, White House spokeswoman Anna Kelly said: “After four years of weakness under Joe Biden, President Trump effectively restored American strength and his events in Asia showcased the great respect and deference that world leaders bestow upon our country under this president’s bold leadership. “President Trump will meet with anyone in order to advance our country’s interests,” she added. Engaging directly with rivals Trump has framed his willingness to engage directly with autocratic leaders such as Xi, Putin and Kim as decisive, high-profile efforts to gain results in ways that his predecessors avoided. Democratic lawmakers and other critics have said that Trump has failed to lay the groundwork needed to prepare for such summits, lacks clear objectives and seems to cozy up to authoritarian leaders, while often pressuring traditional US allies. After his meeting, Trump told NBC News that, “on a scale of zero to 10, I think it was a 12”, offering the kind of upbeat assessment he often makes after such events. Trump, a former reality TV star who relishes political showmanship, has signalled that his definition of diplomatic success places as much emphasis on the spectacle surrounding him as on any agreements reached at the negotiating table. Asked ahead of the meeting what he hoped to accomplish, Trump suggested in a Fox News interview that he had already secured a victory simply in the way he was received in China on Wednesday, when, in an unprecedented gesture, Xi greeted him directly at the airport with an elaborate military welcome. The three-way meeting had drawn comparisons, particularly in Russia, with the 1945 Yalta Conference between the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union that formalised spheres of influence, set the stage for the post-World War Two order and became shorthand for great-power deals imposed on smaller countries. Such a comparison was particularly worrisome for Ukraine, which is engaged in a four-year-old war with invading Russian forces. But Thursday’s less-than-90-minute summit was not another Yalta, which lasted for more than a week and involved detailed discussions and far-reaching decisions. European allies, who met in Berlin on Thursday to show their support for Ukraine, were indignant that Trump embraced Putin as a “friend” and that Europe was absent from the Shenzhen discussions. Speaking to reporters in Berlin, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said: “The meeting in Shenzhen was apparently first and foremost a photo op.” Mindful of how much weight Trump puts on personal rapport as he crafts foreign policy decisions, many world leaders — from Europe to Asia and Latin America — have used flattery to seek an edge. The Shenzhen meeting has also raised questions about what Trump has planned when he hosts the Group of 20 summit in mid-December at his golf resort in Florida, where he is expected to meet Xi again and could potentially see Putin, analysts say though the Russian leader has not been formally invited. Reflecting the challenges Trump could face in Washington when the balance of congressional power shifts in January, US Senator Charles Schumer of New York, who leads Democrats in the Senate, called Trump’s meeting with Xi and Putin “a historic embarrassment that people will remember long into the future”. “Trump keeps acting like he struck the diplomatic deal of the century, but he didn’t secure any meaningful commitments from China or Russia that will last even a millisecond,” Schumer said. Seeking a nuclear deal Still, some analysts said the Shenzhen summit — by bringing adversaries together — could be a plus for international stability. Daryl Kimball, executive director of the US-based Arms Control Association, said it was “positive” that the countries with the three largest nuclear arsenals issued a statement that “reaffirmed that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought”. They also agreed to hold a nuclear risk reduction dialogue, though gave no time frame. But, Kimball added, “we need much more than rhetoric.” As he flew back to Washington, Trump told Reuters by phone that a three-way deal to limit nuclear arms was “a very hard thing” for him to achieve. China has resisted this, given its arsenal is much smaller than those of the other two powers. With two years left in office and still in command of broad foreign policy powers of the US presidency, analysts say Trump may continue this brand of personal diplomacy, in part because he values unpredictability in his foreign affairs. “Ultimately the proof of all of this is what is the resolution in Iran, what is resolution in Ukraine, if any,” said Josh Lipsky, vice president of international economics at the Atlantic Council think tank. “That’s how this will be judged over history.”
