In August last year, the UN’s Integrated Food Security Phase Classification said that Gaza was suffering famine conditions. Record El Nino? Depends who's counting

Britain’s Met Office says this year’s El Nino is “unprecedented.” Japan’s weather agency has called it the strongest ever observed. US forecasters say it still trails the biggest events in its record books. How can they all be right? The answer lies in how they measure El Nino’s strength, with meteorological agencies using different methods to assess the climate phenomenon that make direct comparisons difficult. “By any measure, we are looking at a very strong El Nino,” climate scientist Emily Becker of the University of Miami told AFP, adding that only once it is over will scientists be able to assess how big it is and “where it fits in the context of history.” Natural climate driver The Pacific Ocean’s El Nino Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is the planet’s main natural climate driver. Normally, trade winds blow east to west along the equator, pushing warm water from South America toward Asia, while cold water rises from the depths to replace it. El Ninos, which occur every two to seven years on average and last nine to 12 months, disrupt this pattern. The trade winds weaken and warm water is pushed back east toward the Americas. That shift in where ocean heat is released into the atmosphere ripples across global weather, making some regions cooler and wetter and others hotter and drier. A drone view shows the dried-up Guarne Lagoon at Arvi Park amid drought conditions linked to the El Nino phenomenon, in Medellin, Colombia, September 3, 2026. —Reuters In describing the El Nino as unprecedented, the Met Office points to raw temperatures in the Pacific. “Our observations confirm that sea-surface temperatures in the central equatorial Pacific have now surpassed levels recorded during previous El Nino events,” it said in a statement to AFP. But absolute temperatures are just one way of measuring El Nino’s strength. Agencies also look at anomalies — how far temperatures rise above a historic baseline — to assess the event. The Japan Meteorological Agency last week declared this El Nino the strongest on record based on how far sea surface temperatures were above normal in the Nino3 region of the eastern Pacific. By its count, the event beat 1997 in observations going back to 1949. The US watches a neighboring box, Nino3.4, in the east-central Pacific. It declares an El Nino when temperatures there run at least 0.5C above normal and the atmosphere responds, weakening the trade winds and shifting tropical rainfall eastward. This process is known as “ocean-atmosphere coupling.” Accounting for climate change How “normal” is defined has been a matter of significant debate. The US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) and other agencies including the UN’s World Meteorological Organisation long used the Oceanic Nino Index (ONI), which compares temperatures to a 30-year average that is updated every five years, so that the baseline keeps pace with a warming climate. But with climate change speeding up and warming oceans worldwide, NOAA now uses the Relative Oceanic Nino Index (RONI), and the WMO is in the process of switching. RONI also relies on a 30-year average but goes on to measure how warm or cool the east-central Pacific is compared to the rest of the tropical ocean, because it is that difference that drives the weather impacts we care about. “We were finding a mismatch between the temperature of the tropical Pacific and the impacts,” said Becker about the old index. La Nina years, when the central and eastern tropical Pacific is cooler than normal, were producing stronger effects than their rating suggested, and El Ninos weaker ones. A drone view shows a tugboat alongside a cargo barge loaded with metal pipes, stuck on the Kapuas River, as water levels recede amid a prolonged El Nino-induced drought in Sanggau Regency, West Kalimantan province, Indonesia, September 30, 2026. —Reuters NOAA projects this El Nino will be the strongest in its records, which go back to 1950, if the three-month average RONI anomaly reaches 2.5°C or higher, a level that would put it above the 1982-83, 2015-16, and 1997-98 events. The chances are rated “high,” but not yet certain. “Regardless of where we’re ranking or how we’re measuring this event, it’s an incredible opportunity to study and understand this very powerful phenomenon in the context of a warming world,” said Becker.

The data centre and water question

AS plans for large data centres take shape in Pakistan, the debate has centred on jobs and investment, while a more basic concern has received little attention: water. Why would a data centre compete for water? Computing generates heat, and evaporating water is one of the most cost-effective ways to remove it. By 2030, the United Nations University projects that data centres globally will use nearly triple the combined electricity usage of Pakistan, Bangladesh and Nigeria, with an associated water footprint equal to the basic domestic needs of 1.3 billion people. In Pakistan, the per capita water availability had plummeted from 5,600 cubic metres in 1947 to roughly 930 by 2023. Some weeks ago, we argued in these pages for bringing compute to where Pakistan’s surplus electricity sits, thereby monetising capacity that consumers already pay for. Water is integral to this equation, and a deeper analysis is warranted, which we aim to provide here. How data centres keep cool: Data centres are usually cooled through open evaporative systems, which results in most of the water being lost to the atmosphere. However, these systems are comparatively cost-effective and relatively energy-efficient. Other options include closed-loop systems that recirculate water or a coolant through sealed pipes, which can cut freshwater use by up to 70 per cent; and direct-to-chip and immersion cooling, which bring liquid straight to the processor. Additionally, dry cooling uses almost no water but, by Google’s own estimate, about 10pc more energy. So there is a trade-off in cost and energy, but for a country like ours that already pays for idle generation, it is a strategic investment choice. Running the numbers: A 2016 US Department of Energy study puts average on-site data centre water use at 1.8 litres per kWh. At that rate, a 1 GW campus running continuously would consume about 16 million cubic metres a year, roughly 0.01pc of the 175 billion cubic metres the Indus supplies annually. Sky47’s 8.5MW Karakoram-01 would, based on these estimates, use around 134,000 cubic metres a year. Even accounting for Pakistan’s hotter temperatures, these figures remain marginal. Hence, nationally, data centres are not Pakistan’s water problem. Thirsty infrastructure is workable when siting follows the water. However, locally they can be. A 1 GW campus would draw about 10 million gallons a day from a single source, the same volume that Karachi’s new Rs14bn Dumlottee pipeline will add for the Defence and Clifton areas, in a city receiving 550-650 million gallons daily against a demand of around 1,200 million gallons. The burden falls on the nearest communities. Pakistan has faced this question of siting before with its nuclear power plants. Reactors with cooling towers consume about 2.5 litres per kWh, according to a 2012 review by the US National Renewable Energy Laboratory. At 6,755 GWh generated by Karachi’s K-3 unit in 2024, by the Pakistan Atomic Energy Commission’s count, that’s about 17 million cubic metres a year, or about 12 million gallons a day, more than a 1 GW data centre campus. Pakistan avoided that burden through siting: K-2 and K-3 sit on the coast and cool with seawater, while Chashma draws on the Chashma-Jhelum link canal. Thirsty infrastructure is workable when siting follows the water. The problem at the foundation: Pakistan’s water crisis lies elsewhere. More than 93pc of its water goes to agriculture, and over 60pc of that is lost in conveyance and application. Four crops (rice, wheat, sugarcane, and cotton) consume over 80pc of water resources yet contribute about 5pc of GDP; Pakistan generates just $1.4 of GDP per cubic metre withdrawn, the eighth-lowest in the world. The parallel with power is striking: consumers pay for electricity that never reaches them, and the country withdraws water that never reaches a crop. The way forward: Three interventions follow: Immediate: make water a licensing condition, with energy-compute zones mandating closed-loop or dry cooling, public reporting of water use, and siting away from stressed aquifers. Dubai has promoted recycled water in district cooling since 2008, and Qatar has long incentivised non-potable cooling. Short-term: cool with wastewater. Pakistan treats barely 1pc of its collected wastewater. Meanwhile, Chennai’s tertiary treatment plants are designed to recycle a fifth of the city’s sewage for industry, and Amazon, which air-cools its Indian data centres, will use treated sewage in upcoming facilities in Mumbai and Hyderabad. Operators could co-finance similar plants in Karachi and Lahore. Long-term: reform agricultural water use. Punjab’s World Bank-backed irrigation programme improved over 15,000 watercourses while promoting drip irrigation and laser-levelling. Scaling it nationally and shifting acreage from rice and sugarcane towards pulses, vegetables, and sunflower would save water and cut edible oil imports. Saving just 1pc of the Indus water used for irrigation would supply a 1 GW campus for a century. Weighing both sides: Both sides of this debate hold part of the truth. Critics are right that a poorly sited data centre can strain a community; advocates are right that the sector will not exhaust a country. Pakistan has missed too many economic waves to let a misframed debate cost it this one. The power and water are there to support the data centre industry. What remains is the discipline to manage matters both wisely and efficiently. Persisting fault lines

HISTORY, it has famously been said, repeats itself first as tragedy and then as farce. Today, it is repeating itself in Pakistan as both tragedy and farce. Recent events have deepened some of the country’s overlapping fault lines — enduring features that have shaped its political landscape for decades. Together they have contributed to its chronic instability and impeded its progress. It has meant the absence of a calm and predictable environment in which to meet the country’s growing challenges. The country seems caught in a time warp with a power elite ensuring it cannot escape its past. Four fault lines (there are others too) are laid bare by recent political developments: unceasing confrontation between the government and opposition; centre-province conflict resulting in dismissal of elected provincial governments; parliament’s marginal role in the political system; and weak institutions whose evolution is stymied or independence eroded by predatory ruling elites. The ongoing confrontation between the PML-N-led government and PTI may have novel aspects but it reflects a long-standing fault line in the country’s divisive politics. Government-opposition conflicts have been endemic in Pakistan’s history, with political behaviour driven by the ethic of war rather than competition. Political leaders and parties have viewed opponents as enemies to be eliminated from the political arena in a terminal conflict, not rivals to compete with, much less accommodated. Branding opponents as disloyal to the country is a well-worn, unseemly tradition. This is evident today by the disingenuous narrative peddled by federal ministers that the ousted KP government was aligned with terrorist organisations. A culture of compromise, tolerance, and restraint never took root leaving ruling and opposition parties perpetually at loggerheads. This, in turn, opened the door to both direct and indirect interventions by the military, which could always count on support from one political party or another to oust governments and expand its power. Divide and rule strategies pursued by the establishment also fuelled political conflict. Some enduring aspects of the political landscape are holding Pakistan back. There were rare moments of political consensus as for example when the 1973 Constitution was adopted, Charter of Democracy agreed (but never implemented) between Benazir Bhutto and Nawaz Sharif in 2006 and in more recent years, the unanimous passage of the 18th Constitutional Amendment in 2010. Beyond these and some other exceptions, the tradition of intolerance and authoritarian behaviour has persisted. Opposition parties have rarely accepted the legitimacy of governments while governments refused to recognise opposition as a legitimate activity fundamental to democracy. The underlying reason has usually been disputed elections. Pakistan’s history is replete with losing sides contesting results and alleging ballot fraud, often for good reason. This happened most recently in the 2024 election which, despite its controversial nature, still saw PTI winning a plurality in the national legislature. It was blocked from forming the federal government by an alliance between its opponents and the establishment. Its uncompromising conduct today stems from what it sees as a stolen election and political manoeuvring to keep it out of power. While linked to government-opposition conflict, the second fault line is distinct: troubled relations and friction between the centre and provinces, which have punctuated the country’s history and weakened federalism. Intolerance of opposition-controlled provincial governments by parties governing at the centre became a common feature from the very inception of Pakistan. Just seven days after independence, the NWFP government was deposed, setting an unfortunate precedent. Thereafter, every province saw elected governments replaced by governor’s rule at some time or another, undermining the federation and testing the country’s unity. The consequences of intolerance of rivals together with the establishment’s efforts to centralise power and failure to make the federal arrangement work, were writ large in the rise of the Bengali nationalist movement that culminated in the break-up of Pakistan in 1971. But no lesson was learnt and the fault line endured. Fast forward to the 1990s when the regionalised nature of election outcomes meant that parties forming governments at the federal level were often different from those that constituted provincial governments. This should have urged efforts to establish smooth and cooperative centre-province relations. But that did not happen. The notion that reciprocal obligations between the centre and provinces are more than a constitutional precept and actually have to be fulfilled by both sides still finds few takers. A third fault line relates to parliament’s weakness and its inability to play its fundamental role of exercising oversight of executive actions and hold governments to account. Successive governments have viewed parliament principally as a means to maintain themselves in power rather than a forum to initiate and debate laws and policy. The preferred mode of legislating has often been through ordinance or using a brute majority to rush through laws without deliberation. This reduced parliament to little more than a rubber stamp. The most recent examples are the 26th and 27th Constitutional amendments that were steamrolled through parliament in 2024 and 2025 without real debate and without even sharing their texts with members before moving them. There was virtually no debate when the two chambers recently endorsed the imposition of emergency in KP. Few if any governments encouraged parliament to play an active legislative and deliberative role. Instead, they preferred the legislature to be a passive body. This remains the case. Even ruling parties with handsome majorities failed to recognise parliament’s utility as a forum to ventilate, change and share opinion. Whether this reflects lack of confidence in their backbenchers or poor understanding of how parliament should function, the outcome is marginalisation of the legislature’s role. This denudes the political system of democratic accountability. The fourth fault line — weak institutions — is no less consequential. It is rooted in a political culture that has privileged personalities over institutions, and in a ruling elite that has little appetite for strong institutions because they impose checks and balances and place limits on power. Nothing illustrates this better than the judiciary. Its independence has been steadily eroded over the years by both civilian and military governments. The recent constitutional amendments have taken this further, making the superior judiciary subservient to the executive. Political fault lines are not cast in stone. Their persistence doesn’t mean they cannot be overcome especially when their impact on the country is so damaging. But it requires bold, visionary leadership to break from the past. Better PPPs, less fiscal risk

Pakistan needs infrastructure. It also has little fiscal space to pay for it. In FY25, federal development expenditure was about Rs1.05 trillion, compared with current expenditure of more than Rs15.8tr. That combination makes public-private partnerships (PPPs) particularly attractive, and potentially dangerous. When government cannot finance a motorway, hospital, water system or other infrastructure project upfront, bringing in private capital delivers the asset, operates it under an arrangement and earns a return. But financing is not the same as funding. Private capital can finance infrastructure today. It cannot make its cost disappear. Eventually, somebody must pay, users through tariffs and tolls, government through availability payments and subsidies, or taxpayers when guarantees and contingent liabilities materialise. Pakistan’s experience with independent power producers (IPPs) offers an important warning for its next generation of PPPs. Private investment through take or pay contracts, helped build power plants when public funds were limited. The problem was the allocation of risks and payments. In FY25, the National Electric Power Regulatory Authority (Nepra) reported total power purchase costs of about Rs2.94tr. At the same time, the Finance Division reported circular debt of about Rs1.89tr. These burdens sit alongside distribution losses, weak recoveries, theft and tariff pressures. Pakistan learnt an important lesson through this: a bankable project for the investor may still be unaffordable for the government. Private money can turn into public risk; private investment doesn’t eliminate govt’s financial obligations Following the 18th Amendment, powers shifted to provinces, but leadership became more complicated. Responsibilities like health, education, and agriculture moved downward, yet federal spending still operate in these areas, creating overlap and confusion over who pays and who leads. Under the PPP law, some projects are classified as ‘Qualified Projects’ because they need special government backing, such as guarantees or subsidies. These projects face stricter review by the Public Private Partnership Authority (P3A). Other projects that don’t require such support can move ahead with lighter oversight. Recently, the government moved P3A’s administrative control from the Planning Division to the Privatisation Division. None of these arrangements is problematic on its own. The risk comes when fragmented responsibilities make it unclear about who chooses a project, who pays for it, who delivers it, and who carries the risk. Pakistan doesn’t just need more PPP projects; it needs a pathway to decide which ones should qualify as PPPs in the first place. Just because the private sector is involved in a PPP project doesn’t mean the government stops being accountable Every major public investment should answer a few basic questions. Is the project genuinely a responsibility of this level of government? Does it generate sufficient economic and social benefits to justify state involvement? Can government afford its commitments over the project’s entire life cycle? Would conventional procurement offer better value? And if PPP delivery is chosen, what guarantees, payments, or liabilities will remain with the public sector? This distinction matters beyond motorways and power plants. PPPs can support health, education, water, irrigation, housing, logistics and other social and economic infrastructure. Devolution does not mean that provincial governments must necessarily finance, build and operate every service themselves. The government can remain responsible for ensuring a service while allowing the private sector to finance or deliver parts of it. Just because the private sector is involved in a PPP project doesn’t mean the government stops being accountable, and it doesn’t mean the government automatically avoids financial risk. Policymakers should, therefore, resist two extremes. Fiscal constraints should not stop the government from accepting private investment that can deliver infrastructure more efficiently. But at the same time, those limits should not turn PPPs into a way of shifting today’s spending into tomorrow’s debt. The question should not be whether Pakistan needs more PPPs. The coalition said overnight that it intercepted two drones in the Riyadh area and a third over the oil-rich Eastern Province. Saudi authorities had earlier issued a threat alert in the capital. Two ballistic missiles “launched by the terrorist Houthi militia towards Khamis Mushait” were intercepted in the kingdom’s south, the coalition added. On Saturday, the Houthis reported that Saudi strikes killed 12 people in Yemen, including three children.
