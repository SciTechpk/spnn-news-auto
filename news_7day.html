Love at scale

RECENTLY, the second Global Ministerial Conference to End Violence Against Children was held in Manila. Ministers and government officials from all over the world came together to assert their commitment to protecting children, and to make concrete pledges to work towards in the next two years. Among the plenary speakers was Dr Nadine Burke Harris, a paediatrician and former surgeon general of the state of California. Her work has been dedicated to making the link between adverse childhood experiences (ACES) — including neglect, violence, poverty and abuse — and long-term impact on adults. Prolonged exposure to toxic stress has a biological response, with negative impacts on health and life potential. These can range from heart disease to obesity and substance misuse, to impact on brain development, with difficulties in learning or self-regulating. On the other hand, the experience of loving and nurturing support can have a buffering effect for children, tempering the effects of their difficult realities. Especially if support is offered early, it can transform a future. That’s why, she argued before global delegations, when it comes to child protection, “infrastructure is love at scale.” The experience of loving and nurturing support can have a buffering effect for children, tempering the effects of difficult realities. What this means is that if done right, child protection systems would represent a continuum of care. Right from prevention to recognising the signs for when an intervention is needed, to appropriate case management, response and essential follow-up. Allocating enough resources to have effective referral pathways, trained personnel and data management — that would be an act of love by those with power over budgets. In Pakistan, the scale of challenges is both vast and daunting. Where do we even start? From children visibly exposed to insecurity on the streets, to those in distant villages quietly experiencing the impacts of climate change on their households, it seems impossible to address all the structural factors behind violence. Chronic economic stress increases the risk of violence within households, as well as of child labour that sends children out without guardrails. Then there is the plethora of departments that need stitching together — health, education, social welfare, law enforcement. Each one has a role that needs to be clearly understood, so that the doctor or teacher seeing a child in suffering knows how to recognise physical and psychological injury, and who should be handling it next. The minister for human rights, speaking before the world, shared the launch of a National Action Plan and strategy for child protection. It is apt that the oversight of this strategy falls under human rights, which must be guaranteed to every child regardless of geography, ethnic or religious identity. However, what is crucial at this point is to acknowledge the need for inter-ministerial and inter-departmental cooperation, for which the MoHR can act as a conduit. The ministry of education and professional training, for instance, has a key role in ensuring that schools are both safe and enabling, and an important point of connection with children and families. It also has a key role in creating a culture of care for children, with educators beginning to disrupt the normalisation of corporal punishment and violence as a tool for discipline. In that sense, the conference was a unique opportunity to really learn from global experiences of navigating bureaucracy and politics, to get to what matters. All this being said, there are limitations to scale. Social services even in the most “developed” contexts can get it wrong, when mechanically applying standards to children or families under stress. Love at scale cannot mean that children are not seen for their individual circumstances, or are separated from families without working through challenges. If anything, love will be made up of the countless tiny acts and steps taken by the frontline workers who come into contact with vulnerable children, the conversations they are able to have, the kindness they show in response. Again, teachers can represent essential allies in a protective ecosystem. Not only do they know individuals, communities and their challenges, but can be reliable sources of support that children see every day. Within a compassionate classroom, they can create trust, joy, and hope for a positive future. The theme for World Children’s Day this year was “My day, my rights, my future.” This is not the first time we have heard words like this; perhaps that is why it is a time of great reckoning in the sphere of human rights and development, with a tragic deficit of resources and trust in the sector. However, what finally needs to be understood is that civil society alone cannot stop all the gaps, even while being able to offer the state both technical support and insights from community work. Manila was an illustration of how it is possible to make space for high-level official dialogue as well as learning from academic research, and ground-level implementation experience incorporating children’s voices. If this time we truly want love at scale, it will take all of us, working to cover the crevices that children can fall through. Referendum — a non-starter?

THE idea of holding a referendum to ascertain public opinion with regard to the creation of more provinces has been floated by some ministers. The prime minister, on the other hand, remains mum on this highly divisive and potentially destabilising subject. No wonder Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari — who heads a major coalition party that also has a major stake in the current system — is vociferously decrying the government for keeping him in the dark and not taking him into confidence about the ‘reset’ or the referendum. But the visible discord among the coalition partners should not be surprising. It is in the dichotomous nature of the hybrid system. The key policies are made outside the political sphere. But the onerous task of implementation is thrown on the shoulders of politicians, often at the cost of their careers and even constituencies. The tradition of setting critical agendas — whether they are related to constitutional experiments, economic shifts, social policies, institutional restructuring, public accountability, internal security, strategic alliances, or war and peace — in secrecy and away from the public glare, is by now quite old. In many instances, even parliament has come into the picture only when the fig leaf of the law or the Constitution is needed to ‘legitimise’ a dubious plan. The ‘decision’ to hold the referendum is also par for the course. The architecture of the reset has never been elucidated. Its proponents are fully aware that a referendum can never substitute for the mandatory constitutional amendment to alter the boundaries of provinces. They also know the tainted history of referendums, which were called to validate the capture of power by dictators. And they may also not deny the fact, as rightly pointed out by BBZ, that the prevailing sociopolitical and security environment in the country is unsuitable for holding a controversial referendum, that too on such a socio-politically explosive issue. Yet, the advocates of referendum seem adamant to carry it through. Why? No one can conjecture the answer with surety because to this day the architecture of the reset has never been elucidated. All that we know is that ‘reset’ is ‘desirable’ for good governance and devolution of power to the lowest strata. But this ‘truism’ is flawed, conceptually and functionally. Conceptually, good governance and devolution being the offshoots of a broader constitutional democratic framework are antithetical to the hybrid system that is extraconstitutional. And functionally, restructuring the entire state fabric requires not only public mandate but an array of democratic tools — an elected parliament, representative governments, independent judiciary, and an apolitical (military and civilian) bureaucracy. Moreover, the increasingly arbitrary, if not authoritarian, mode of governance also makes the conversation on the imperatives of good governance and devolution rather absurd. This raises a pertinent question: are the proponents of ‘reset’ willing to do away with the hybrid system to make way for a legitimate and functional democracy, fulfilling the ‘intended’ objects of reforming governance and empowering local communities? If the hybridity is to stay, then it would be disastrous to ‘reset’ in the name of good governance or devolution, which are the attributes of a functional democracy. Even the reset on the logic of size or demographics is grossly misleading. Size and demography are only two of the many dimensions of an efficient democratic system. There are many countries where large and populated provinces are known for their excellent governance. In fact, the standards of measuring the success or failure of a political system lie in the degree of its constitutional legitimacy, electoral transparency, institutional capacity, and public administration. Therefore, the key questions probing the performance of our political system should be: is the electoral system credible? Is the public mandate respected? Are wealth and resources distributed fairly? Are public functionaries accountable? Is the justice system cheap, fair, and speedy? Is public safety and order a priority? Is the republic peaceable? And, are the citizens’ fundamental rights protected? I would advise the proponents of the ‘reset’ to consider these questions before calling for a controversial referendum that is bound to pit individuals, groups, communities, and even provinces against one another, adding more fault lines to the already much-riven ethno-cultural landscape. The people have seen enough of the ‘yes-no’ binary questions which were framed in referendums to seek personal legitimacy and institutional empowerment for military dictators, rather than reforming governance or empowering the people. Moreover, none of the referendums has earned a mark of credibility or fairness because they were rigged or state-managed. Therefore, they completely lost their utility or worth in the eyes of the people. BBZ is not too off the mark when he warns that holding the referendum is ‘near impossible’ because of the insecure environment in the country. But also, the people, particularly in Sindh, would not accept the results of a referendum, even if it is somehow carried out. Which brings us to the most critical question: what if one or the other of the provinces or a sizeable ethnic community rejected the results of the referendum? This question is crucial because there is hardly any ‘credible’ or ‘neutral’ forum — political, administrative or judicial — to resolve political disputes. Leave aside the grudges of the battered opposition, even BBZ has now cast doubts on the legitimacy of the 27th Amendment that led to the creation of the Federal Constitutional Court. And the FCC is the court of last resort in matters involving politico-constitutional disputes. Given the thick air of mistrust in public institutions, how would a ‘controversial’ referendum act as a stepping stone towards the constitutional ‘reset’? And more so when, among others, the PPP is resolutely opposing the ‘division’ of Sindh. How would the powers that be garner the requisite numbers to amend the Constitution? It would be tragic to pull off a wholesale restructuring of the state employing the carrot-and-stick method. The multiethnic state of 250 million people is too complex, conflict-ridden and even fragile to sustain yet another forced experiment. It requires meaningful dialogue, consensus-making, and democratic reforms to overhaul its political system, not a non-starter referendum or an arbitrary reset.
