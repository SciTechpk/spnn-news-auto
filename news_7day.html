Islamabad province?

OPPOSITION to it has been largely suppressed, but all is not well within the ruling regime. One way or the other, the proposal to break up existing federating units will ultimately shape the regime’s endgame. Stage one has been completed without major resistance, the PTI disarmed through the imposition of emergency in KP, and a neo-administrative experiment comprising 12 districts in the southern and ex-Fata regions operationalised. The ultimate prize for the establishment is the long-desired separation of Karachi from the rest of Sindh. And this is where the Bhutto-Zardari clan, facing an existential threat, will be forced to play its hand. But before this, there are likely to be other moves expected to trigger less resistance, both within the ruling coalition and outside it. One of these is the plan to make Islamabad into a province. The bureaucracy rules Islamabad without impediment. The only proposal on record, finalised by a committee headed by Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal, has advised a 27-member assembly, headed by an elected chief minister/mayor. The elephant in the room is the viceregal institution which dictates the fates of Islamabad’s three million people — the Capital Development Authority (CDA). Pakistani democracy is, at best, an aspiration rather than a functioning reality connected to the will of the people. Elected institutions operate under pressure, while most who claim to be representatives of the people wage class war daily. Nevertheless, the pretence of democracy remains. The federal capital is a glaring exception. The bureaucracy rules here without impediment, the popular will be damned. There has been one citywide experiment with local government since the capital was created in 1960. The 2016 elections brought the Municipal Corporation of Islamabad (MCI) into existence, under the nominal authority of an elected mayor. The mayor and elected representatives down to the level of union councillor had virtually zero legislative and financial power. Elections never took place again, and the MCI was transformed into yet another bureaucratic feeder to the CDA. So if Islamabad is given a legislative assembly, will the CDA be cut down to size? I choose my words carefully — because those who are poised to ‘give’ Islamabad an assembly have, over the past year or so, made their plans for the city and its environs sufficiently clear. A short list of ‘achievements’ reads as follows: thousands of homes in katchi abadis and historical villages bulldozed; suburban sprawl in the form of land-grabbing real-estate schemes extended; thousands of trees razed and the city’s air poisoned by relentless construction. With the CDA’s Master Plan flagrantly violated by its own commercial projects and big proprietors, another post-facto amendment in zoning regulations was recently approved by the government. As for what is to come, entire chunks of Rawal Lake and many villages around Bhara Kahu will be sacrificed for a 350-acre safari park, while the area adjacent to the Bari Imam shrine — where forced evictions took place earlier this year — is being given over to hotel developers. Will Islamabad’s much-celebrated green spaces, already limited public services, and working-class settlements be left by the time the purported legislative assembly comes into place? Will the dominant imaginary of ‘development’ fronted by the state apparatus — of which the CDA is a poster-child — suddenly give way to a political-economic-environmental arrangement that serves the mass of Islamabad’s people? Only the awfully naïve believe that the plans for an Isla­mabad province — which may ex­­tend into adjacent districts like Mu­­rree and Haripur currently part of Punjab and KP respectively — are driven by democ­r­atic impulses. Seen thus, the Islam-abad case provides the most obvious indication of what the new provinces hullabaloo is all about; an attempt to subjugate the political and economic interests of the mass of the population, and future generations to boot, all in the name of efficient administration. Over the course of the past year, mainstream parties like the PPP and PML-N have made no effort to stand with katchi abadi dwellers and villagers whose homes have been bulldozed in Islamabad. They have not resisted the barrage of ‘development’ projects that have forever destroyed local ecologies. Islamabad was always the preserve of the elite, but working people fought for their rights with some success, mainstream politicians paying heed in exchange for votes. Like in the rest of the country, Islamabad’s working masses now have to exclusively wage their own political fight. They are up against both powerful elements and political entrepreneurs who hunt with the hare and run with the hound. The writer teaches at Quaid-i-Azam University, Islamabad. Helping create paths

ONE child, born in an affluent household, goes to one of the best private, high-fee schools, gets a lot of support from home, appears for international school leaving assessments and then makes her way to the best colleges and universities of the country or of the world. When she enters the job market, she not only has her education to bank on, and the confidence that comes with it and the upbringing she got, she has parental and university networks to support her as well. Another child — and there are millions of children in Pakistan who face these odds — might have struggled to even be in a public or low-fee private school, is likely to drop out well before reaching college, but even if she reaches college, it is likely she will struggle to find a job afterwards. The quality of her education is compromised, she did not have much support from home for her learning, her confidence has not been built up, and she does not have a parental/family network or a university network to support her. The state is supposed to level the playing field amongst children. Education has shown to be an amazing equaliser and the state, in theory at least, can use educational opportunities to level some of the inequalities coming from homes and families. It is a legal and constitutional requirement in the case of basic education (Article 25A of the Constitution as well as many international commitments of our state), but it makes functional sense to do that as well: a merit-based society is likely to bring the best talent forward and that, more than anything else, becomes the engine for economic growth, social well-being and societal harmony. The state of Pakistan has completely failed to deliver the promise of ‘the right to education’. But it has also failed, and quite miserably, to deliver on social outcomes. Twenty-six million-odd children out of school, poor quality of education for most children enrolled in low-fee private and public schools, high dropout rate, small percentage of enrolled children reaching university level (roughly only 10 per cent), poor quality of university education in most private and public universities and very poor opportunities for employment even if you survive in the system and graduate from it with a degree. State policies have, in many instances, helped develop & entrench inequalities. Instead of equalising opportunities and levelling the playing field, state policies have, in many instances, helped develop and entrench inequalities even more. Funding cuts and restrictions for public education, both at school and university level, are a telling example as is the inability and unwillingness of the state to regulate provision of education by private, for-profit providers. Some effort has been made to provide scholarships, on merit, at high school and university level to help students who do well in public examinations, but, compared to the scale of poverty and the escalation in costs of education, all scholarship schemes, together, do not amount to more than a few drops in the ocean. Private not-for-profit initiatives have also been working in the area, but the scale is a big issue here. They have been and can help hundreds of thousands, but they have not been able to reach millions. If experience from other countries is to be of value, the state has to step in and play its role. The Citizen’s Foundation (TCF) is probably the largest non-profit provider of schooling in the country. It has been around for more than three decades and has served hundreds of thousands of students and employed thousands of teachers. But even today, TCF has less than 3,000 odd schools and school units that it runs. Pakistan has some 200,000 schools in operation in the country, and we still have 26m children out of school. How many TCFs do we need? It cannot be done without the state! Equally important is the form of support that is provided. Even when the state has come in it has stepped in to either reduce tuition fees (public schools are free for students and public universities are still subsidised) or provide individual scholarships based on need and/or merit. But a lot more is needed. Again, if you look at examples from other countries young people need support for health and nutrition, academic support (where such support from home is missing), counselling support to ensure better career and occupation choices, scholarships, mentoring support, support for developing non-academic but needed skills in communication, self-presentation, confidence building, and support in making transition to the workplace. These programmes, sadly, are almost completely missing in Pakistan. Even technical education, skills and training providers focus a lot more on specific skills than on issues of confidence, interpersonal skills, self-presentation and so on. But we do need these programmes as well. Here are a couple of examples from small/local initiatives. Amal Academy does fellowships with university students to develop their skills in self-presentation, to improve confidence, to ensure better self-organisation and management. This is done over a few months through mentorship programmes that are quite structured and very interactive. Institute of Lifelong Learning and Development, a new initiative, has a three-pronged strategy. They provide scholarships to ease access, development and mentorship programmes to focus on needed skills and confidence building and a service orientation, right from the start, to ensure benefiting students are ready to pay it forward right from the word go. The point of these examples is to just emphasise that equalising opportunities or levelling the playing field does not get addressed through access issues (tuition fees and subsidies) only; the state needs to do a lot more. We have plenty of examples we could learn from and bodies like TEVTA and PSDF could benefit from scaling some of these programmes. It is hard to see how Pakistan could move on the road to better human development and sustained economic growth without the state stepping in and stepping up to address some of these glaring issues of unequal opportunity sets. In hindsight

JUST as an important partner in the ruling coalition is starting to question the wisdom of the 26th and 27th Amendments, a report issued by the International Commission of Jurists has detailed the damage the two enactments have wrought on Pakistan’s judiciary. Titled Judicial Capture: Independence of the Judiciary in Pakistan after the 26th and 27th Constitutional Amendments, the report concludes that systemic constitutional and legislative changes have placed the country’s highest courts under the control of the political and executive branches. The ICJ comprises judges and lawyers from around the world working to “develop and strengthen national and international justice systems”. The report clarifies: “The ICJ does not take a position on the merits of political disputes between the parties. Its analysis is focused on the institutional framework in which the judiciary operates, and whether it is consistent with Pakistan’s obligations under international law.” The most worrying flag the report raises is that the ruling parties — in what can only be read as their zeal to have these two amendments enacted — have pushed the judiciary to a state where the country’s highest courts no longer “enjoy the institutional guarantees of independence required by Article 14” of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the UN Basic Principles on the Independence of the Judiciary, among other standards. It also notes that the loss of judicial independence has stripped victims of human rights abuses of their right to an effective legal remedy under domestic law and Article 2(3) of the ICCPR. Though the ICJ agrees that prior issues in the Pakistani judiciary, especially its tendency to overreach into parliament’s domain, needed targeted reforms, its secretary-general, Santiago Canton, says the government used those frustrations as a pretext to seize full control of the judiciary rather than enact serious reforms. The detailed report offers a cold examination of the path taken by the Pakistani judiciary since 2024 and brings into fresh relief what may be the roots of some of the new crises that have gripped the country since then. Its timing couldn’t have been better: there is speculation that another amendment is in the works, and those who partnered with the government on getting earlier legislation enacted are now apprehensive of what a new rewrite will entail. As local observers have been warning, and as the ICJ’s findings now bear out, the 26th and 27th Amendments have done serious damage to judicial independence. Part of the reason why is the haste with which they were enacted. Before any further changes are made to the Constitution, it would be wise to debate all proposed amendments at length and enact them only after a careful, deliberate consensus has been built. The PHC, through its Nov 14, 2025 judgement, dismissed 157 petitions challenging the levy and upheld its legality. The judgements of both high courts were subsequently challenged before the FCC.
