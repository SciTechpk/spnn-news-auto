Committee expert and member of the task force for Pakistan, Michael Windfuhr, welcomed the 2025 amendment to the Child Marriage Restraint Act 1929, which raised the minimum age of marriage to 18 for both males and females. Lazhari Bouzid, another committee expert and member of the task force for Pakistan, said bonded labour continued to be prevalent in agriculture and was also found in the informal sector, particularly brick making. Construction companies and government agencies were reportedly complicit in the practice by purchasing bricks produced through debt bondage. He asked how Pakistan had responded to these reports. Mr Windfuhr also asked how the country had coped with the impact of the devastating floods in 2022 and 2025. He inquired whether Pakistan could rebuild housing for affected people and restore their access to land or resources, and how many people remained displaced. ‘Range of measures’ Minister of State for Law and Justice and head of the delegation, Barrister Aqeel Malik, said in his opening remarks that Pakistan had taken a range of legislative, policy and institutional measures since 2017 for the progressive realisation of covenant rights. He said child marriage restraint laws had set the minimum age of marriage at 18 for both boys and girls in Islamabad, Balochistan, Punjab and Sindh, and criminalised child, early and forced marriages. During the ensuing dialogue, the delegation said Pakistan’s Constitution prohibited slavery and all forms of forced labour, while the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1992 had abolished the practice. Labour inspectors had identified more than 2,900 victims of forced labour last year. On flood recovery, the delegation said the government had established a national emergency operations centre focusing on high-risk areas and launched a national flood protection programme. The programme aimed to protect at-risk people, eight million acres of agricultural land and 2.2 million housing units. Each province was also implementing flood protection measures. Committee vice chair and acting chair of the dialogue, Asraf Ally Caunhye, thanked the delegation for its participation in the constructive dialogue and its expression of unwavering commitment to protecting the rights enshrined in the covenant. Evolving climate architecture

PAKISTAN stands at a crossroads as it prepares to attend COP31 in Turkiye next week. Every October, the Emissions Gap Report brings bad news, and every October we read it as a scorecard of how far the world is from Paris. This year, it is not a progress report. It is notice that the Paris benchmark has been breached. The United Nations Environment Programme’s 2026 report concludes that the chance to hold warming below 1.5 degrees Celsius with no or limited overshoot “is gone”. The task now is to “minimise overshoot and return to 1.5°C”. The target survives; its meaning has flipped. No longer a ceiling, 1.5°C is a line we will cross and then try to get back under. Even the “lowest possible” overshoot peaks at 1.7 to 1.8°C. For Pakistan, this is not abstract arithmetic. The gap between 1.5 and 1.8 is more poverty and heat in Jacobabad, wilder monsoons in Sindh, transboundary floods in Punjab, and faster glacial melt in the upper Indus. The question is simple: who pays for the overshoot years? Just as the science matters most, the body that certifies it is going broke. For the first time in its history, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) “might not have the funds to finish an assessment report”, Nature warned recently. Contributions have fallen since 2023, and Washington has walked away. The panel needs more than CHF 20 million ($24m) to survive until 2029, less than many single-donor projects in Islamabad. Nor can it agree on a timetable. In Addis Ababa last month, governments failed for the sixth meeting running to decide when its next reports come out. A small bloc wants the reports on emission cuts and adaptation delayed until after the 2028 global stocktake, the five-yearly review of progress under Paris. The reasons are procedural; the effect is political. For the most vulnerable countries, that delay is betrayal. Yet Pakistan, the self-declared ground zero of climate impacts, stayed away from the forum that certifies the evidence. We need some $47bn a year to 2030 but receive under $2bn. It is not for want of evidence. In August, the Pakistan Meteorological Department (PMD) published How the Climate of Pakistan Has Changed, a 45-year record and the most granular we have. Nights are warming faster than days, and springs across much of the country are up to 2.5°C warmer than in the 1980s and 1990s. Rain has become not scarcer but less dependable: the 2022 monsoon dumped up to 450 per cent above-average rain on the south, and three years later, the winter of 2024-25 brought a 67pc national deficit. That is the overshoot world in miniature. The EU’s Paris Agreement Overshooting: Reversibility, Climate Impacts and Adaptation Needs (PROVIDE project), which studied exactly such risks, had an Islamabad partner, so the research clearly exists. What is missing is the habit of carrying it into the rooms where global science, and therefore, global money, is decided. The empty chair reflects a deeper reflex. Pakistan’s climate diplomacy still treats finance as something to ask for: grants, owed as a moral debt by those who caused the problem. The argument is right on the merits and increasingly beside the point. The COP29 goal of $300 billion a year is being hollowed out by the governments that pledged it, and what grant money remains goes to countries ready to absorb it. On that test, Pakistan fails. We need some $47bn a year to 2030 but receive under $2bn. The gap is not one of vulnerability but readiness. Meanwhile, a new architecture is taking shape elsewhere, and in one corner of it Pakistan already leads. At the World Bank-IMF annual meetings in Bangkok last month, Islamabad took the chair of the Borrowers’ Platform, which gives debtors what creditors have in the Paris Club: a place to pool data, compare terms, and bargain as a bloc. On Nov 1, it also assumed the chair of the G-24, the developing-country caucus at the IMF and World Bank. For a year then, Pakistan speaks for the debtors just as debt has become the main channel of climate finance. Disasters are paid for with borrowing, and each recovery loan makes the next shock harder to absorb. Pakistan, where debt servicing eats 43pc of the federal budget, is itself a textbook case. Of the $10bn pledged at Geneva after the 2022 floods, about $8.7bn came from lenders. We were asked to borrow our way out of a disaster we did little to cause. New instruments attack this chain link by link. Debt-pause clauses suspend repayments after a disaster; Grenada used one after Hurricane Beryl to defer about $30m. The V20, a bloc of finance ministers from the most climate-vulnerable countries, is building a Lifeline Fund that would pool members’ reserves into a $500m emergency facility. Africa’s new rating agency challenges unfair risk premiums; Mia Mottley’s Bridgetown Initiative demands cheaper, longer loans. Each treats climate as a balance sheet problem, and that is where the post-Paris order is taking shape. Pakistan now heads the borrowers’ table and leaves an empty chair at the scientists’ table. The two are linked: a debtor bloc that wants climate risk priced into debt terms needs credible, nationally owned evidence, and the IPCC is where such evidence gains global standing. Four steps follow before Antalya. First, rejoin the science table: pay into the IPCC trust fund, attend the resumed session in December, and back the least developed countries and small islands on a 2028 timeline. Second, put the PMD record to work in AR7, the next Nationally Determined Contributions, and the annual budgets. Third, trade appeals for readiness, with bankable adaptation projects, accredited national entities and climate tagging in the budget. Fourth, use the Borrowers’ Platform chair to make debt-pause clauses standard in sovereign lending and to tie debt and climate together at the stocktake. The world has moved past the Paris target and is moving past COP as the only place where climate is decided. The winds of change are blowing elsewhere. Turning of the American tide

THE election results in America this week portend a sea change, and they have important ramifications for Pakistan. The Democrats have taken both the House and the Senate in a rout that reached Texas and Kansas. Anger over living costs, tariffs, and the war on Iran all played their part. For the remaining two years of his term, President Donald Trump will face a Congress armed with subpoena power and eager to use it. This is no distant affair for us. Much of the world has spent two years trying to cope with Trump. Meanwhile, the leadership in Pakistan flamboyantly embraced him, though let’s not underestimate how well that worked. It got us lower tariffs, a White House lunch, and, above all, a central role when the Iran war needed an exit ramp. The question now is what that embrace will cost us as the man on the receiving end of it begins to lose his grip on Washington. Pakistan has veered too close to Trump, and this might not go down well in the near future. The April ceasefire that we helped broker was a genuine achievement, and I said so at the time. The memorandum that followed in June has since collapsed, but the credit earned in those weeks still stands. Yet all of it could well come to be overshadowed by the single act of having nominated Trump for a Nobel Peace Prize in so conspicuous and overly generous a manner. Showering the man with extravagant praise in highly public settings might have worked to win him over in the short term. But the praise kept coming long after the point had been made. In September, he was telling the UN General Assembly that our prime minister credits him with saving 30 million lives. Last month, on the day the Nobel went to someone else, the prime minister was on X lauding his “historic role” in South Asia. These things do not expire. There is a risk they get tagged onto Pakistan in the years to come, once Americans settle on their verdict of this presidency. What looks clever in one season reads very differently at a hearing in the next. Even more dangerous is the fact that the Trump family’s business dealings might come under congressional investigation. Reuters reported in September that probes could begin as early as February, with Trump-linked crypto firms of particular interest. Senator Richard Blumenthal, who is in line to chair an investigations panel, has promised to use his subpoena power. The same senator was asking questions about World Liberty Financial’s Pakistan deal in May last year. If any ties lead back here, they could tie the country to the affair; the threads are already in public view. In January, our finance minister signed a memorandum with a World Liberty affiliate concerning its USD1 stablecoin. In September, the defence ministry placed a limited order with Powerus, a drone company in which two of Trump’s sons are investors. No wrongdoing has been established. The White House denies any conflict of interest, and as of July the stablecoin deal had produced no known transactions at all. But I have described that memorandum as an instrument of access, and access is exactly what investigators will want to look into. What looks clever in one season reads very differently at a hearing in the next. These risks, however, are rooted more in optics and perceptions than in realpolitik. Pakistan’s thrust to find a place in the new regional order emerging in the Middle East must continue unabated. The Makkah Joint Defence Agreement with Saudi Arabia and Turkiye, and the Tehran huddle I wrote about last week, which Field Marshal Asim Munir himself proposed, have a logic of their own that does not depend on who chairs which committee in Washington. Whatever the fallout for Pakistan, the changing of the political tide in America will be an American affair first and foremost. The most likely outcome is that the Trump administration comes to be consumed by its wrangling with the new Congress and loses its moorings in foreign policy. A White House fighting hearings and subpoenas at home will have little bandwidth for the world. This could produce a hazardous moment. A beleaguered and irrational president might try an adventure abroad to distract from his troubles at home, and the adventure is ready to hand. The blockade of Iran remains in place, reports in September said he saw a fresh bombing campaign as likely after the midterms, and Iran’s Supreme Leader warned on Wednesday that the war’s scope will widen if the attacks are repeated. But that seems unlikely given the stakes. This is the war voters have just punished him for, and on the eve of the vote he himself cast doubt on resuming strikes. What is more likely is an acceleration in the pace with which America withdraws from its commitments to underwrite global order, particularly its military posture in the Middle East. I argued in April that the price of getting out of this war would be a disorderly exit from the region altogether. A Congress that owes its majority partly to anger over this war will be in no hurry to fund the next one. The Gulf monarchies have seen their American protector fail to shield them from Iranian missiles, and they are drawing their own conclusions. All this will spur the search for a new security architecture among the powers of the region, a search that will definitely include Pakistan. Three times in our history, in the 1960s, the 1980s and the 2000s, an American embrace brought dollars and a growth spurt, and each time the money stopped when Washington’s interest moved on. This time it has brought more photographs than dollars. Ironically enough, this turning inward of the United States might just be the push Pakistan needs to learn to stand on its own feet. Analysis: Midterms clip Trump’s wings at home, not abroad

THE world had good reason to watch the US midterm elections with bated breath, because their outcome would not only determine the balance of power in Washington, but also shape the course of major international conflicts — particularly in the Middle East — and establish how much political bandwidth Presi­dent Donald Trump would retain to pursue his foreign policy during the final two years of his presidency. The verdict has been considerably harsher than many had anticipated, chiefly beca­use of the fallout of the Iran war. As the election outcome was influenced by an external issue, the implications will ac­c­ordingly be reflected more on the foreign policy front. For foreign capitals, the significance of the result lies less in the change of congressional control itself but what it means for Trump’s ability, and willingness, to use American power abroad. A midterm defeat does not automatically diminish presidential authority over foreign affairs and military operations. Trump, therefore, retains considerable discretion over military operations and negotiations, as well as executive powers over sanctions and trade. What he has actually lost is political room to manoeuvre as Democrats in the Congress can now investigate, obstruct funding and impose greater scrutiny on his decisions. Compounding that would be the fact that some key Republicans, mindful of their own electoral prospects in 2028, may become less willing to defend his policies that come with a political cost. The resulting situation could constrain the president over time, but it would not necessarily make him more cautious in the immediate term. Iran Iran will be the immediate test of how Trump responds to the Republican rout. The electoral setback has created a paradox for the president because while the war is the key reason for the debacle, many think he may still be tempted to intensify military pressure in the hope of securing a decisive outcome before the political space for doing so narrows further. Christopher Clary, a non-resident fellow at Stimson Center, points out that “the Constitution grants the president enormous latitude on the use of force and the president can escalate despite the new Democratic Congress.” But escalation carries the risk of aggravating the original problem. Iranian retaliation in that case could deepen the economic pressures and make the war an even greater political liability. The indications from Tehran further suggest that resumption of full-scale hostilities could also lead to widening of the conflict. Importantly, Trump has a crucial window of two months before the new Congress takes office. But after the transition, political calculations will change with Democrats making the Iran war a major focus of oversight. They could also pursue War Powers resolutions, requiring congressional authorisation for continued hostilities, in addition to attaching conditions or restrictions to defence appropriations. Mr Clary warned that “the lame duck period before the new Congress is sworn in will be an especially dangerous time.” He emphasised that while there are “no real binding constraints on Trump”, the Democrats can “use their budgetary authority to deny new funds to sustain the operation”. The control of spending will give Democrats additional leverage, although it will not automatically enable them to terminate military operations because Trump retains veto power, which he has hinted that he will use, and Democrats don’t command the two-thirds majority needed to override it. Moreover, Democrats will not either want to be seen as curtailing support for American forces. Quincy Institute Vice President Trita Parsi recalled that “the Vietnam war ended because Congress stopped funding it”, adding, “Democrats can do this to Trump’s illegal Iran war as well. If they don’t, they will become complicit”. Sustaining an open ended campaign will, in any case, become politically more difficult and even Republicans, conscious of the electoral cost of the Iran war, may also become increasingly reluctant to support additional funding without a clearer strategy and exit plan. If things do come to that point sometime next year, Trump can then become inclined towards a combination of military pressure and diplomacy by extracting concessions from Tehran through the threat of further strikes while pursuing an agreement, which he can present as an achievement and a resolution of the conflict. The turnaround in relations under Trump depended heavily on the president and his inner circle, rather than an actual shift in the relationship. Islamabad had moved quickly to cultivate ties with the new administration after Trump returned to office — extraditing the accused in Abbey Gate Kabul bombing; praising the president’s intervention in the May 2025 conflict with India; nominating him for the Nobel Peace prize; and, joining his Board of Peace for Gaza. Critical minerals and cryptocurrency also became prominent areas of engagement, alongside proposals involving Trump family-backed World Liberty Financial, the Roosevelt Hotel and drone manufacturing. A Democratic Congress is unlikely to undo the relationship, but it could subject some of these dealings to greater scrutiny. Questions may be raised about foreign links of the Trump family’s commercial interests, the cooperation with Powerus and Pakistan’s governance and transparency. There will, nevertheless, be areas of bipartisan convergence, particularly counterterrorism, regional stability, critical minerals and Pakistan’s diplomatic role in the Iran conflict. The challenge for Islamabad will be to translate the access secured under Trump into a relationship with a wider institutional base, including engagement with Democrats and a stronger case for durable economic cooperation. The Election Commission of Pakistan (ECP) on Wednesday objected to remarks made by former senior puisne judge of the Supreme Court of Pakistan, Mansoor Ali Shah, regarding polls in KP and Punjab and the reserved seats case pertaining to the PTI. Shah made the remarks at an event organised by the US-based Community Alliance for Peace and Justice on October 29, where he was asked about his dissenting opinion in the 2023 SC ruling on provincial elections in Punjab and KP and whether it was a “turning point” and the beginning of a “division” within the SC. In its 3-2 verdict, the SC, after taking a suo motu notice, directed that elections should be held within 90 days in both provinces. Two judges, including Shah, had dissented from the ruling, saying that the suo motu proceedings initiated by then-chief justice of Pakistan Umar Ata Bandial, were “wholly unjustified”, besides being initiated with “undue haste”. In their detailed reasoning, they explained that the SC by a majority of 4-3 and not 3-2 had dismissed the suo motu hearing on polls delay in Punjab and KP and therefore the same should be considered as the “Order of the Court”. It had resulted in a controversy, with the government igniting a raging debate claiming that the ruling was a 4-3 majority decision in its favour and not a 3-2 verdict against. In response to the question, Shah said that this was the time when the ex-chief justice was forming “like-minded benches”, adding that three or four judges were attending to the majority of the cases. He said that initially the matter was taken by the high courts. He said that the SC judges had gathered in the ante-room and discussed with the chief justice at the time that suo motu notice should not be taken at this point. He said that it was “agreed” to wait but days later a SC bench took up the matter under suo motu powers as he denounced the “one-man show” going on at the time. He said that at the time, two SC judges — Justice Yahya Afridi and Justice Athar Minallah — had dismissed the petitions in the case. He said that the SC bench was later reconstituted, adding that since two judges had decided the matter, the verdict could not be dismissed. He said that this sparked the 4-3, 2-3 debate. Shah also defended the 2024 verdict in the reserved seats case, wherein PTI was declared eligible for seats reserved for women and minorities, in a setback to the ruling coalition. “We said that the PTI was a political party and it was wrong to deprive [them of their symbol] … and illegal,” he said, recalling that this was a decision “that nobody liked”. In a statement on Wednesday, the ECP referred to the remarks made by Shah, saying that they ignored “basic constitutional and legal facts related to the election matters and judicial decisions”. “It is highly inappropriate to present the judicial record for specific purposes through selective quotations, incomplete perspectives and one-sided interpretations,” the statement said. The electoral watchdog said that Shah had claimed that the SC’s majority decision about the elections in 2023 was a 4-3 verdict and that the suo motu notice and relevant petitions were dismissed. The ECP also stated that according to Shah’s claims, the election ruling was a “minority decision” and the commission could not have held polls. Regarding the PTI case, it said that Shah had declared the order for withdrawal of the party’s electoral symbol during the 2024 elections as “illegal”. The commission said that the election symbol was subject to transparent intra-party elections as per Sections 208 and 209 of the Election Act 2017. It added that “till date, PTI has not been able to fulfil the requirement”. The ECP said PTI was supposed to hold intra-party elections by June 30, 2021, and the commission had given the party three chances; however, the party failed to hold intra-party elections. As a result, the commission said that the party’s election symbol was withdrawn, which, the ECP added, was also upheld by the SC in 2025, along with rejecting review petitions filed by the PTI. The ECP further stated that PTI held intra-party elections after five years and its proceedings remained pending before the commission as the “PTI itself has taken a stay order from a high court”. It further stated that as per the commission’s records, the PTI was still enlisted as a political party. The ECP added that only political parties which contested the elections had a right to the reserved seats. “These political parties are required to submit a preferred list of candidates before the elections, while PTI has not submitted any preferred list,” it stated. It further stated that it was mandatory for a political party to win at least one seat and that independent members were not entitled to reserved seats and therefore, the ECP gave the seats to the eligible parties. It said that if the PTI was entitled to the reserved seats, then “Articles 51 and 106 of the Constitution and Section 104 of the Elections Act 2917 become ineffective”. The ECP said that it was “unfortunate” that a retired judge was taking a position against the Constitution and the law. In November 2025, Shah had resigned as an SC judge alongside Minallah, hours after the 27th Constitutional Amendment — which made significant changes to the country’s judicial system and established the Federal Constitutional Court — was signed into law by President Asif Ali Zardari. “By fracturing the unity of the nation’s apex court, it has crippled judicial independence and integrity, pushing the country back by decades,” he wrote in the letter sent to the president. “Staying on would not only amount to silent acquiescence in a constitutional wrong, but would also mean continuing to sit in a court whose constitutional voice has been muted,” he said.
