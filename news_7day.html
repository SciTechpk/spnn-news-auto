FCC sets aside high court rulings upholding infrastructure cess in Sindh, KP

ISLAMABAD: The Federal Constitutional Court (FCC) on Thursday set aside high court rulings upholding the infrastructure cess imposed by the Sindh and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa governments. The case pertained to the Sindh Development and Maintenance of Infrastructure Cess Act, 2017 and the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Infrastructure Development Cess Act, 2022, including amendments introduced through the KP Finance Act, 2024. Headed by FCC Chief Justice Aminuddin Khan, a two-judge bench, also comprising Justice Aamer Farooq, had taken up a batch of 568 petitions by private petitioners challenging decisions by the Sindh High Court (SHC) and the Peshawar High Court (PHC) upholding the cesses. The FCC bench examined the constitutional limits of provincial taxation in relation to imports, exports, customs-related transactions and the movement of goods across provincial boundaries. The Sindh infrastructure cess controversy originated in 1994 and was subsequently governed by the Sindh Development and Maintenance of Infrastructure Cess Act, 2017, which provided for taxes on goods entering or leaving Sindh from or for outside the country through air or sea. On June 4, 2021, the SHC upheld the levy. Similarly, the KP Infrastructure Development Cess Act, 2022, imposed cess on the transportation and movement of goods in the province. Section 14 of the KP Finance Act, 2024, amended Section 3 of the 2022 Act to enhance the cess rate from one to two per cent. The PHC, through its Nov 14, 2025 judgement, dismissed 157 petitions challenging the levy and upheld its legality. The judgements of both high courts were subsequently challenged before the FCC. The petitioners argued that the provinces lacked constitutional authority to impose fiscal levies on imports, exports and goods falling within the federal legislative domain. They maintained that the impugned laws encroached upon Parliament’s exclusive legislative competence. Consequently, the provincial provisions and the high court judgements upholding them could not be sustained. Senior counsel Hafiz Ehsaan Ahmad Khokhar, while representing the secretary of the Revenue Division and the Federal Board of Revenue (FBR), submitted that the impugned provincial levies had to be examined in the light of Articles 141 and 142(a) of the Constitution, read with Entries 27, 43 and 59 of Part I of the Federal Legislative List (FLL) in the Fourth Schedule. He explained that Entry 27 covered imports and exports across customs frontiers, inter-provincial trade and commerce and trade with foreign countries, whereas Entry 43 specifically assigned customs duties, including export duties, to the federation. The counsel contended that under Article 142(a), Parliament had exclusive legislative competence over matters enumerated in the FLL. The constitutional character of a levy, he argued, must be determined by its true nature, taxable event, fiscal incidence, charging provisions and actual operation, not merely by its nomenclature as an infrastructure cess. A provincial levy imposing an additional fiscal burden on imported or exported goods and operating through the customs-clearance mechanism directly engaged the federal legislative domain, he said. He also referred to Article 163, explaining that the provincial power to impose taxes on professions, trades, callings and employments could not be extended to customs duties or other subjects constitutionally assigned to the Parliament. The counsel further argued that a provincial legislature could not acquire legislative competence over a federal subject merely by describing a customs-linked fiscal levy as an infrastructure cess. Meanwhile, the Sindh and KP governments defended their respective laws, arguing that the taxes were provincial levies intended to finance infrastructure development and road facilities. They maintained that it was not a customs or export duty and that the provincial assemblies were competent to legislate on such matters under Article 142(c) of the Constitution and the impugned judgements were legal. The additional attorney general supported the federation’s position, submitting that the provincial governments lacked legislative competence to impose levies falling within the FLL. After hearing the arguments, the FCC set aside the two high court judgements and declared that the legislations were beyond the legislative competence of the provinces.
