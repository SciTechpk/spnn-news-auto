VIEWPOINT: Auto policy’s tax paradox

Pakistan’s new auto policy was meant to take effect on July 1. It is yet to be finalised. Meanwhile, the automotive industry continues pricing vehicles, opening letters of credit, and signing dealer agreements in the dark — unaware of the tax burden those cars will carry next year. The latest bottleneck arose during the International Monetary Fund’s fourth review of the Extended Fund Facility. In late September, the Ministry of Industries and Production presented its draft Auto Policy 2026-31 to the IMF mission, but talks concluded without an agreement. The primary sticking point is the proposed one per cent sales tax on new energy vehicles. The Fund wants the standard 18pc rate. On a Rs10 million car, that single disagreement represents the difference between a tax of Rs100,000 and Rs1.8mn. Low tax rates matter, but the rate itself is only half the story. The crucial second half is defining what this policy is actually for. In FY26, Pakistan spent $16.9 billion on petroleum imports, a major portion of which was burnt on its roads. The government aims for 30pc of new vehicle sales to be electric by 2030, a transition estimated to save about $1bn annually in foreign exchange, 2bn litres of fuel, and 4.5mn tonnes of carbon emissions. For a nation recovering from devastating floods and choking on severe winter smog every year, these are not side benefits. They are the entire point. Low tax rates matter, but defining the policy is also crucial Both sides of the current argument are partly right. The Fund has watched Pakistan repeatedly carve up a narrow tax base through special rates and Statutory Regulatory Orders (SROs), knowing temporary concessions almost invariably become permanent. The ministry is equally right that electric vehicles cost more upfront, charging infrastructure remains thin, and buyers harbour deep anxieties about battery life and resale value. Imposing the standard tax rate overnight would reduce the 30pc EV target to a line on paper. However, the draft policy has a glaring weakness of its own. Customs duties on conventional cars are scheduled to fall sharply, but reports suggest this gap will simply be refilled through higher Federal Excise Duty and floor prices on imported vehicles – essentially building the same protective wall with different bricks. In the meanwhile, tax rates keep moving by notification. The concessional 8.5pc sales tax on most hybrids expired on June 30 with the expiry of the last auto industry development and export policy, causing taxes on locally made hybrids to jump to 25pc. Then, in September, the Federal Board of Revenue issued SRO 1525(I)/2026, bringing locally manufactured hybrids up to 2,000cc back down to 18pc. The relief was welcome, but the same car underwent three distinct tax rates in as many months, and the final adjustment came through SRO rather than any transparent policy. An investor deciding whether to establish an assembly plant does not mainly ask whether the tax is 1pc or 18pc. They ask what the tax will be in 2029, and whether that answer will survive the next budget cycle. A better structure would rest on five sensible pillars: First, agree on 18pc as the ultimate destination and legislate a clear roadmap to get there. Keep the 1pc tax rate until June 2027, when current concessions lapse, then step it up incrementally each year to reach the standard rate by FY2030. Low taxes in the early years build the market: China exempted electric cars from purchase tax for nearly a decade, and Norway waived VAT before both began phased rollbacks. Support should be generous at the start, but bound by a firm expiration date. Second, target the relief. A Rs30mn luxury import does not need the same fiscal cushion as a compact car, a taxi, or a rickshaw. Capping concessions by price or battery size concentrates public money where the public benefit actually lies. Third, set a clear tech ladder. The combined burden of duty and tax should be highest on conventional petrol cars, lower on hybrids, lower still on plug-in hybrids, and lowest on fully electric vehicles. Battery electric vehicles and range-extended EVs should share one tax structure at the bottom because their powertrain is fundamentally the same. Fourth, follow the National Tariff Policy honestly. Bring duties on fully built imports down as announced, eliminate additional and regulatory duties by FY30, and do not rebuild protectionism through excise duty. Maintain a moderate, transparent gap between duties on built-up imports and kits for local assembly, fixed for the life of the policy. Fifth, reward localisation through component tariffs, not sales tax exemptions. Offering low duties on battery packs, electric motors, and controllers for a fixed period from the start of local production is a far cleaner incentive that directly rewards genuine investment. The last important point is that established assemblers and vendor networks support over a million livelihoods, as per PAAPAM, and their fear of a uniform tariff cut is legitimate: localisation that took decades to build can be dismantled in a year. But ‘raw’ protectionism alone has failed to deliver scale or competitiveness. Local content in Pakistani passenger cars averages about 58pc, compared to roughly 80pc in India. The electric transition is a chance to rewrite that story. Localisation incentives should go to value added whether the carmaker is old or new, and vendors, who retool for electric components, should receive the same parts concessions and long-term financing. The government has a short window before the revised draft goes to the Cabinet. The right path forward is simple to state: agree on the end-state with the Fund, publish a binding dated schedule, target support where it moves the most people and goods, classify tax tiers by technology, and leave the rules alone long enough for someone to build a factory around them. Every day of delay is paid for in lost foreign exchange, dirtier air, and a target that slips further away. Low taxes will start the market. Only predictable taxes will build the industry behind it. Brewing crisis of shrinking farm size

Pakistan’s agricultural crisis is generally framed around water scarcity, rising input costs, declining productivity and profitability and climate change. Yet another structural problem – the subdivision of agricultural land into smaller and scattered holdings – is worsening silently. Despite implications for farm efficiency, rural incomes and national poverty, the issue has received far less attention. The seventh Agricultural Census released in July has come up with figures that must ring alarm bells. The number of farms has increased from 8.26 million in 2010 to 11.10m in 2024 – a rise of 34.4 per cent. Over the same period, average farm size fell from 6.4 acres to 5.3 acres, a decline of 17.2pc. Even more significant is the number of fragmented farms, which rose from 2.83m in 2010 to 4.98m in 2024 – an increase of 2.15m, or more than 76pc. The average division of separate plots held by each fragmented farm increased from three in 2010 to seven in 2024 – an increase of more than 133pc. The general distribution of landholdings reinforces the picture. In 2024, 59pc farms were smaller than 2.5 acres, compared with 43.5pc in 2010. The proportion of farms below five acres rose from 64.7pc to 78pc, while more than 95pc are under 12.5 acres, up from 89.5pc in 2010. This skewed structure has implications for all; individual farms, sectoral performance and policy makers. For farms, smaller and increasingly dispersed holdings make mechanisation next to impossible, raise cultivation and supervision costs, limit access to credit and modern technologies, and reduce farmers’ capacity to generate marketable surpluses and cash for themselves. For many smallholders, farming is becoming less of a commercial enterprise than a means of meeting household subsistence needs At the sectoral level, it risks slowing productivity, weakening commercial agriculture and increasing rural poverty as families turn to off-farm employment to supplement their incomes. The policy implications are equally significant: improving agricultural performance requires more than addressing water scarcity and input costs. It demands initiatives to help smallholders overcome the disadvantages of scale, shared machinery services, equipment suited to small plots, accessible agricultural credit, cooperative farming arrangements, zoning and cluster development and incentivising voluntary land consolidation. For many smallholders, farming is consequently becoming less of a commercial enterprise than a means of meeting household subsistence needs. Muhammad Nadeem, a three-acre farmer from Okara, describes the transition: “When a plot falls below a certain size, subsistence farming and traditional rural practices – growing one’s own staple and fodder for livestock – take precedence.” Nadeem owns three buffaloes and divides his land between fodder, wheat and vegetables to meet his family’s basic needs. As the land is ancestral, he shares a return with his brother and sister. Farming is now a part-time activity: Nadeem and his brother work as labourers in Okara city, while his sister teaches at a local private school. “The land generates virtually no income because the yields are barely enough to meet our needs,” he says. It is also because of expensive fertilisers. Credit is another obstacle. Even local middlemen, who typically finance inputs, are reluctant to lend as they know the limitations of the family’s landholding. “For us, farming is no longer a viable source of income. We continue to cultivate only for it provides a staple for family and fodder for animals.” Nadeem’s experience shows how shrinking holdings can turn farming into a part-time survival activity, forcing families to seek off-farm employment while leaving no money for agricultural investment. The challenge becomes greater when land is divided into scattered, non-contiguous plots. Five consolidated acres at one location are very different from the same size spread across several parcels. Dispersed holdings increase the time and cost of land preparation, irrigation, crop protection and harvesting. They also complicate supervision, delay essential operations and make machinery less efficient to deploy. Former irrigation minister and south Punjab farmer, Mohsin Leghari, is deeply concerned about this trend. “Five acres in one place and five scattered across several locations are two completely different propositions. The latter increases cultivation costs, complicates mechanisation, makes supervision difficult, and delays critical farming operations,” he says. The shrinking average holdings could accelerate poverty and urban migration. Mechanisation presents a particular challenge with multiplying division of land. While machinery can reduce labour requirements, improve efficiency and help farmers complete time-sensitive operations, the high cost of purchase and maintenance is difficult to recover from smaller acreage, especially if scattered across different locations. Agricultural economist Mirza Irfan Baig argues that this problem extends beyond affordability. “For small farms, mechanisation is not simply a question of affordability or commercial viability; it is also a question of whether appropriate machinery is available in the first place,” he says. Baig explains that modern agricultural mechanisation largely evolved to serve large-scale farming, particularly in the West, where extensive, contiguous holdings allow machinery to operate efficiently across large areas. “Such machinery is not scale-neutral. Equipment designed for large, consolidated farms cannot be used efficiently on small, scattered plots. Its size, operating costs and capacity are not suited to smallholders. There is little incentive for manufacturers to develop specialised machinery for such a limited and fragmented market.” This creates a structural barrier to modernisation. Even if money is available, farmers may not find machines suited to their holdings. Buying large equipment individually is often uneconomical, while the lack of affordable, appropriately sized alternatives limits mechanisation and productivity. Addressing the problem does not necessarily mean the disappearance of small farms or the forced consolidation. Shared services, equipment rental markets, cooperative arrangements, zoning and clustering of land, and better coordination among farmers could help them acquire efficiencies of larger operations. Improved access to credit and technologies designed for small plots would also be essential. The policy makers now need to look beyond cultivated area and the immediate pressures of water scarcity, input costs and climate change, and deal with this structural issue. It is also necessary because subdivision of agricultural land is not merely a farming problem; it is also a question of rural livelihoods and national poverty. If millions of farmers struggle to earn a viable income from land, the consequences extend from rural dwellings to national poverty. FBR reforms, again?

Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif’s warning against resistance to tax reforms has once again put a spotlight on the efforts to restructure the Federal Board of Revenue (FBR). The warning came after a pen-down strike call by the Inland Revenue Service Officers Association (IRSOA) in opposition to the reforms and subsequent punitive action against several officers. Since 2000, successive governments have borrowed hundreds of millions of dollars from the World Bank to restructure tax administration. However, persistent weaknesses in compliance, audit and enforcement raise a fundamental question: why have repeated reforms over two decades failed to deliver lasting results, and how will the latest transformation plan be different? The first major reform initiative began in 2000, followed by the World Bank’s approval of $102.9 million for the Tax Administration Reform Project in 2004. By 2012, the programme had established or upgraded 57 offices and facilities, including Large Taxpayer Units, Regional Tax Offices, Model Customs Collectorates and taxpayer facilitation centres. It also provided 11,445 items of equipment, including computers, alongside vehicles and improved office infrastructure. The reforms introduced partial automation, universal self-assessment and the transfer of sales tax and federal excise administration from Customs to Inland Revenue. However, manual procedures persisted, limiting efficiency gains. Much of the investment appeared to improve working conditions rather than revenue collection. The World Bank’s 2012 completion report recorded disbursements of approximately $48.4m and rated the project’s outcome as “moderately unsatisfactory”, warning of a high risk to its sustainability. Successive reforms since 2000 failed to produce results; investment in technology likely to remain ineffective sans central database to document transactions Former FBR chairman Shabbar Zaidi says he had over $200m available for reforms during his tenure but chose not to spend it on the projects unlikely to succeed without fundamental institutional changes. He says that investment in technology would remain ineffective unless the government establishes a central economic database to record and document transactions. Without reliable data on economic activity, tax officers could not effectively verify income declared in tax returns or identify concealed income, he argues. The priority, therefore, should be documenting economic transactions rather than spending more money on administrative reforms. Despite the earlier project’s un-favourable assessment, the World Bank approved another $400m under the Pakistan Raises Revenue Programme in 2019, followed by $70m in additional financing in 2025. Total approved financing for the two projects reached $572.9m, although this does not represent actual disbursements. A major institutional change came in 2009 with the creation of the Inland Revenue Service (IRS), integrating sales tax and federal excise with income tax administration. The restructuring triggered resistance from Customs officers and disputes over authority, senior positions and career progression. Both services subsequently expanded their organisational structures through dozens of new directorates and field formations. This expansion raises questions about whether new formations addressed operational needs or also accommodated promotions and postings. Their staffing, expenditure, overlapping responsibilities and contribution to revenue collection require independent evaluation. During the 2024 caretaker government, the then finance minister Shamshad Akhtar proposed separating Customs and Inland Revenue into distinct organisations with independent oversight. The proposals faced resistance from tax officials. Consequently, the restructuring was not implemented. International experience offers no single organisational model. India maintains separate central authorities for direct and indirect taxes, while Australia and Britain operate integrated revenue administrations. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) and Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) emphasise professional competence, accountability and effective enforcement. Tax expert Ikramul Haq says repeated restructuring and foreign financing would not improve FBR’s performance without fundamental institutional changes. “Technology is necessary. Faceless assessment, digital invoicing, production monitoring and intelligent risk selection can reduce discretion. They cannot substitute for institutional design.” He calls for separating tax policy from collection, merit based recruitment and promotions, and evaluating performance through voluntary compliance, recovery, refunds and reduced litigation rather than gross collection alone. He argues that corruption, poor performance and disagreement over reforms require separate treatment, while political accountability is equally necessary. The FBR’s revenue composition illustrates the continuing challenge. According to its FY25 yearbook, withholding taxes accounted for 58.2pc of gross direct tax collection, while collection on demand contributed only 4.6pc. These figures raise questions about the returns from investment in offices, vehicles, staff benefits and performance incentives. The Auditor General also identifies demand collection as an indicator of departmental performance. However, the biggest challenge is to minimise the tax gap in the sales tax, which is reported at over Rs3 trillion. Audit effectiveness remains another concern. The State Bank’s FY24 annual report noted that risk-based audits, introduced in 2019, had not been implemented across all functional levels. Manual interventions and poor recovery continued to weaken enforcement. The Auditor General’s FY25 report also identified deficiencies in tax assessments, recovery and litigation management. Procedural failures in tax litigation have attracted scrutiny. In December 2025, the Lahore Tax Bar Association sought an investigation into appeals dismissed by Lahore High Court because of missed statutory deadlines, raising questions about legal supervision and accountability within the revenue machinery. The government’s emphasis on rising income tax return filings also warrants examination. The FBR reported 6.7m returns by Oct 14, compared with nearly 5m during the corresponding period last year, an increase of 34.38pc. However, higher filing numbers alone do not establish improved compliance, particularly when differential withholding rates encourage taxpayers to enter the active taxpayer list. The latest FBR transformation plan focuses on digital enforcement. Faceless customs assessment began in December 2024, while the National Faceless Centre for Inland Revenue was established in Islamabad last month with nationwide expansion planned by June 2027. Automated case allocation and reduced interaction between taxpayers and officers may limit discretion and improve transparency. However, digital systems cannot independently guarantee competent audits, legally sustainable assessments or recovery of outstanding demands. The question is whether artificial intelligence, digital invoicing, production monitoring and automated risk assessment can improve tax compliance without stronger audit procedures and enforcement accountability. These technologies can detect discrepancies and verify transactions, but their effectiveness depends on proper investigation, legally sustainable assessments and actual recovery. For instance, identifying fraudulent input tax adjustments or mismatches between production and sales achieves little unless violations lead to enforcement action. The success of digital reforms should therefore be measured not by the number of suspicious cases detected but by recoveries, reduced fraudulent claims and measurable improvements in tax compliance. Pakistan’s food supply fails own dietary guidelines: FAO

ISLAMABAD: Pakistan’s food supply remains sharply misaligned with national dietary guidelines, with major shortages of vegetables, fruits, roots and tubers, and pulses, while cereals and sugar remain in excess of dietary requirements, according to a Food and Agricul­ture Organisation (FAO) policy brief. FAO’s new policy brief, “Food Supply for Healthy Diets in Pakistan,” says ensuring equitable access to diverse, nutritious, safe and affordable foods is a policy imperative, particularly as more than 60 per cent of Pakistanis cannot afford a healthy diet. The situation contributes to high rates of malnutrition, while diet-related noncommunicable diseases are rising alongside persistent unde­rnutrition, the report says. Pakistan adopted its food-based dietary guidelines in 2018, setting out evidence-based recommendations for the types and quantities of foods needed for healthy diets. But efforts to promote healthy eating cannot succeed unless nutritious foods are available and accessible, the brief says. The assessment compared Pakistan’s annual food supply with population-level requirements across 10 dietary guideline food groups from 2010 to 2023, identifying surpluses and gaps. Population growth is adding pressure. Pakistan’s population increased from 189.3 million in 2010 to 235.2m in 2023 and is projected to reach about 330m by 2050. Climate change and other challenges are expected to intensify demands on the agrifood system. The report identifies persistent shortages in several nutritious food groups, particularly vegetables, roots and tubers, fruits, and legumes and pulses. The estimated vegetable supply gap widened from 10.9m to 17.8m tonnes in 2010 to 13m to 21.6 m tonnes in 2023. For fruits, the gap increased from 1.9m to 10.3m tonnes to 3.9m to 14.3m tonnes over the same period. However, milk, milk products, meat and eggs showed improvement. Fats and oils moved from a small gap in 2010 into the estimated required range in subsequent years. To address the imbalance, FAO recommends coordinated fiscal and regulatory measures to realign agricultural production and trade priorities, improve food distribution, reduce post-harvest losses and food waste, and strengthen demand for healthy foods. The brief calls for updating Pakistan’s dietary guidelines to reflect nutrition, health and environmental priorities, along with nutrition education and behaviour-change efforts. It recommends evidence-based taxes on beverages and foods high in sugar, salt and unhealthy fats, and front-of-package nutrition labelling to help consumers make informed choices. It urges greater agricultural investment in vegetables, fruits, pulses, roots and tubers, with support for milk production in some regions. Terrorist commander killed during IBO in Balochistan's Qila Saifullah: state media

Security forces killed a terrorist commander during an intelligence-based operation (IBO) in Balochistan’s Qila Saifullah, state-run Radio Pakistan reported on Sunday, citing security sources. The slain terrorist was identified as Adil Shah alias Darvesh in the state broadcaster’s report, which said he belonged to Fitna-al-Khawarij — a term the state uses for terrorists belonging to the banned Tehreek-i-Taliban Pakistan. “Security forces are actively carrying out operations against terrorists in Balochistan under ‘Operation Radd-ul-Fitna-3’. According to security sources, the terrorist commander had been associated with Fitna-al-Khawarij since Dec 2020 and had been managing the financial affairs of the group since 2024,” the report said. The commander was also involved in facilitating permanent residences for “foreign terrorists” in Balochistan’s Zhob, Sherani, and Shaban, the report quoted the sources as saying. It added that the slain terrorist had been involved in attacks on security forces and kidnapping for ransom since 2022. Nineteen first information reports were registered against him and a bounty of Rs2.5 million was placed on his head, the report further stated. Balochistan Chief Minister Sarfraz Bugti said in a post on X that his killing showed the security forces’ “professional expertise and readiness” The province has witnessed terrorist attacks and security challenges for a long time. On October 19, an army major was martyred, and four terrorists were killed during IBOs in Balochistan. A day before that, security forces killed eight “India-sponsored” terrorists during IBOs conducted in the Quetta, Nushki and Khuzdar districts of Balochistan. On Oct 14, five terrorists were killed in an IBO in Balochistan’s Washuk by security forces, while 10 terrorists were killed in a targeted IBO conducted by the Counter-Terrorism Department in Balochistan’s Barkhan district on Oct 10. On Sept 17, three terrorists were eliminated in an IBO in Balochistan’s Awaran district’s Badrang area. Another three were killed in Mastung on Sept 14. Earlier in September, 21 terrorists were killed in separate engagements in Nokcha, Chagai, Ziarat Cross, and Pishin. According to Pakistan Institute for Conflict and Security Studies (PICSS), terrorist violence increased substantially last month, with 225 attacks recorded nationwide, compared with 199 in August, representing a 13.1 per cent increase and the highest monthly total recorded in 12 years. As per the report, terrorism related fatalities rose by 64 per cent from 158 to 259, while injuries increased by 33.7 per cent to 242. Balochistan remained the most affected province, the report said, accounting for 131 attacks — 58.2pc of the national total. Chief of Defence Forces and Chief of the Army Staff Field Marshal Asim Munir said last week that the armed forces will continue the counterterrorism campaign against Indian-sponsored terrorist groups “to the fullest”, vowing to “hit them hard wherever they are”.
