Tale of two private jets

This is a tale of two private jets, bought for two chief ministers, on two different continents, in two different worlds. While a debate rages in Pakistan about the purchase and use of a private jet by Punjab Chief Minister Maryam Nawaz, the Premier (chief minister) of Ontario, the largest province of Canada with a GDP of roughly C$900.8 billion (US $643.54bn) in 2017 terms, also had to face a similar outcry when the provincial government announced on April 17 this year, the purchase of a Canada made, ten years old, 2016 model, pre-owned Bombardier Challenger 650 jet for Premier Doug Ford’s “extensive travel(s)”. The jet, bought for C$28.9 million, was to facilitate the Premier’s travel within Ontario and the United States, the announcement said. Announcing the purchase, a spokesperson for the Ontario government then said that the plane would be used solely to conduct “government business”, and not for any personal trip. Premier Ford argued then that the province needed the plane because Ontario is vast, and he needed something more secure to travel to the United States to push back against President Donald Trump’s trade war. Compared to what happened in Pakistan, when the premier of Canada’s Ontario faced backlash over the purchase of a plane, he reversed his decision While defending the purchase, Mr Ford’s office also cited that the plane was necessary for “more certain, flexible, secure and confidential travel,” citing the premier’s trips across the province as well as to meetings with fellow premiers and the prime minister. The purchase resulted in an uproar. Critics quickly derided it as the “gravy plane” — a play on Ford’s boasts in the past that he was elected to “stop the gravy train” of government spending. All guns targeted Premier Doug Ford for the purchase. Ontario Opposition Leader Marit Stiles, of the New Democratic Party (NDP), called the jet a “gravy plane” in a social media post, adding that Ford should be “flying economy like the rest of us.” Premier Doug Ford faced an outcry when the provincial government announced the purchase of a Canada-made, ten-year-old, pre-owned jet for C$28.9 million “I think it’s outrageous that the premier would be considering spending 30 million [Canadian] dollars on a luxury private plane at a time like this — when Ontarians are struggling with the price of rent, the price of food,” Stiles said at an unrelated news conference then. The NDP, in its statement, also cited a 2019 Toronto Star story, in which Ford had said that he was “the only premier in history” who would prefer to drive around the province than spend taxpayer dollars flying. Interim Ontario Liberal Leader John Fraser strongly condemned the Ford government’s purchase of the pre-owned Bombardier Challenger 650 private jet, calling it completely “out of touch”. Groups outside of the opposition benches were also quick to denounce the provincial government’s purchase. The Canadian Taxpayers Federation (CTF) demanded in a statement that Ford reverse plans to spend taxpayer dollars on a private jet. “Instead of taking commercial flights like many premiers in this country do, Ford would rather cosplay the life of the rich and famous on taxpayers’ dime,” said Noah Jarvis, CTF Ontario director. “Ordinary taxpayers are struggling to make ends meet, and (Ontario premier) Ford is making them pay for a personal jet so that he doesn’t have to wait in line at the airport like everyone else.” And with public pressure piling on the purchases, both in Pakistan and in Canada, the outcomes of the two were starkly opposite to each other. Here in Toronto, the outcry forced Premier Ford to change his decision. He had to take a U-turn. Within a few days of the announcement of the jet purchase, Mr Ford gave in, conceding that he had heard the public’s concerns. “Despite the best of intentions, I have heard and agree that now is not the right time for the expense of a government plane. The province is working with Bombardier and other partners to sell the plane as quickly as possible,” he said. Speaking at an unrelated announcement in Ottawa, he then added, “I’m the premier that listens to the people, and if they don’t like something, I’m not too shy to change my mind”. Within days, the plane was sold back to Bombardier at the purchase price of C$28.9 million. But Premier Ford is not out of the woods. The purchase, and the expenses involved in the process, cost the provincial exchequer an additional amount of around C$200,000. He will have to answer that too. Premier Doug Ford is known as a go-getter. He has won three consecutive elections in the province for the provincial Conservatives. Yet, pundits now believe his party may have to pay a political price for the decision to buy a private jet for the premier of Canada’s largest province, which boasts the highest GDP among the provinces of the country. Special delivery: India's Tamil Nadu state hands out gold for newborns

Authorities in India’s Tamil Nadu state gave babies born in government-run hospitals a free gold ring on Monday, an initiative aimed at boosting confidence in the public healthcare system. Under the scheme launched by actor-turned-Chief Minister Vijay, all babies born in public health facilities in the state are eligible for a one-gramme gold ring, worth some $143. Gold has immense cultural significance in India, featuring prominently in weddings and religious ceremonies, and seen as a safe generational asset. “The gold ring would give the child something valuable to keep for the years ahead,” said one mother at a government hospital in the state’s Madurai district, who declined to give her name. She was among dozens of mothers who cradled their newborns as they queued up to receive the gift. “This scheme feels personal,” another grateful mother, Stella Mary, told AFP. Officials say the initiative aims to highlight maternal and newborn care, while strengthening faith in public healthcare institutions in the southern state of over 72 million people. The scheme, however, drew some criticism from the Bharatiya Janata Party, which is in the opposition in Tamil Nadu. “A gold ring for a newborn may be a welcome gesture, but for families, the most immediate need is ensuring that essential nutrition is available every day,” party member Tamilisai Soundararajan said in a social media post. India’s overcrowded public hospitals often struggle with staff shortages, long waiting times and patchy infrastructure, prompting many patients to seek treatment in the costly private sector. While most women in India give birth in hospitals, home deliveries still occur in some rural communities, making institutional childbirth a priority for health authorities. Despite significant gains in recent years, infant and maternal mortality rates continue to languish in India. The country recorded 24 infant deaths per 1,000 live births while maternal deaths stood at 88 per 100,000 live births, according to latest official data. The gold ring scheme, known as the “Thaimaman” (maternal uncle) programme, draws on the special cultural significance of a child’s maternal uncle, who is often expected to play a key role in family milestones and celebrations. It also taps into India’s enduring obsession with gold. For many women, particularly in rural areas, gold jewellery can serve as a form of personal financial security. India is one of the world’s largest consumers of the metal. According to the World Gold Council, demand in the country reached $25 billion in the first quarter of this year.

Country braces for 25pc Rabi water shortage

• Reservoir carryover falls below 9.5MAF, lowest since 2021• Wheat crop faces added pressure after estimated shortfall last season• Irsa technical committee to finalise Rabi water availability, provincial shares• Three-tier water distribution formula may revive acrimony among traditional rivals Sindh, Punjab ISLAMABAD: After many years, Pakistan is bracing for a water shortage of around 25 per cent during the Rabi season starting this week, raising concerns about food security next year as wheat is the season’s main crop. The last Rabi season faced no water shortage, yet the country is estimated to have suffered a wheat shortfall of more than two million tonnes. This forced the government to order imports of one million tonnes, including 750,000 tonnes in the first phase for delivery in November. Against a production target of 29.7m tonnes and consumption req­uirements of 31.9m tonnes, the country is estimated to have produced around 27.5m tonnes as far­mers faced challenging conditions following changes in wheat policy. Part of the estimated Rabi water shortage is man-made, owing to the political needs of the coalition government, informed sources told Dawn, adding that more than one million acre feet (MAF) of avoidable water releases were made from reservoirs during the outgoing Kharif season. The sources said parliamentary interventions had allowed higher discharges to Sindh with commitments for subsequent adjustments in provincial shares, which could not be materialised within the Kharif season. Total storage in the two major reservoirs peaked at 11.6MAF on Sept 9, when Tarbela remained full at its 5.8MAF capacity bet­ween Aug 9 and Sept 9 while Mangla touched 5.733MAF on Sept 9. Both reservoirs started depleting since then, with total storage falling below 9.5MAF ahead of the Rabi season — the lowest carryover since 2021. Rabi begins on Oct 1 and generally ends on March 31. Last year, the season started with a healthy carryover of 13MAF in the reservoirs, almost 27pc higher than this year. Wheat is the largest Rabi crop, while gram, lentil, tobacco, rapeseed, barley and mustard are among the other crops grown during the season. The sources said Rabi 2024 had started with carryover storage of 10.5MAF and the country faced a water shortage of 15-18pc, while Rabi 2023 began with 11.6MAF. Rabi 2022 started with 9.5MAF, when the country faced a 20-22pc shortage. Rabi 2021 was the worst among recent years, beginning with 7.42MAF in reservoir storage and resulting in a 28pc shortage. Against this backdrop, the technical committee of the Indus River System Authority (Irsa) will meet on Monday (today) to finalise water availability estimates and recommend criteria for distribution among the provinces for the Rabi cropping season. The Irsa Advisory Committee (IAC) has also been tentatively called to meet on Oct 5 to approve provincial water shares on the recommendations of the technical committee. The anticipated shortages are likely to revive provincial acrimony, particularly between Sindh and Punjab, as Irsa seeks to allocate water under the three-tier formula it uses to manage shortages. Tarbela Dam reached its maximum conservation level of 1,550 feet on Aug 9 and remained there until Sept 9. Mangla Dam peaked at 1,222 feet on Aug 9 against its maximum conservation level of 1,242 feet. Flows in the Jhelum River remained weak throughout the Kharif season, while subsequent rel­e­a­ses further reduced Man­gla’s conservation level. As a result, Mangla’s storage stood at 5.12MAF on Sunday night against its maximum storage capacity of 7.277MAF. Mangla had reached 1,233.25 feet at the end of Kharif 2023 and 1,224 feet in 2025. Around 10.33MAF of water has flowed into the sea since the Kharif season began on April 1 against an annual environmental requirement of about 8.2MAF. Total water requirements for Rabi crops under the Water Apportionment Accord of 1991 are about 37-38MAF. With limited carryover storage from Kharif, water availability is expected to be around 21-22MAF, along with river flows over the next six months. The accord empowers Irsa to determine the country’s water availability and provincial shares twice a year. The 1991 water accord works well in rare cases of sufficient annual water availability of 114MAF. However, with shortages exceeding 10pc becoming increasingly common, Irsa has been using a de facto three-tier formula for distributing provincial shares — a mechanism opposed by Sindh, which has been pushing for implementation of Para 2 of the accord. Irsa has maintained for several years that it cannot change the existing distribution mechanism used to absorb shortages. Under Para 2 of the 1991 accord, provincial water shares were fixed. However, amid persistent shortages, Irsa, with the involvement of the federal and provincial governments, introduced a cascading distribution mechanism in 2002. The three-tier formula now combines Para 2, Para 14(a) and historical water use from 1977 to 1982. Para 14(b) requires that 10 daily uses be adjusted pro rata to correspond with seasonal allocations of different canal systems and form the basis for sharing shortages and surpluses across the country. Under the three-tier formula, Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are exempt from shortages, leaving the shortfall to be shared by Sindh and Punjab. However, Sindh maintains that it bears a disproportionately larger shortage under this mechanism. The province has argued that Irsa exceeded its mandate by introducing the three-tier formula and devised its own formulas despite Sindh’s objection through a meeting of the provincial committee of ministers.
